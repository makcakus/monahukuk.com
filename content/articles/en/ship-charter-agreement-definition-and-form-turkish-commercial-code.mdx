TTK Art. 1119-1121 open the Code's chapter on what is internationally known as bareboat (demise) charter — a full transfer of operational control over a ship for a fee.

## Full Operational Control, Crew or No Crew

Under Art. 1119, the defining feature of a ship charter agreement is that the lessor hands over *use* of the ship — meaning operational control — for a fixed period against a fee; the charterer operates the ship at its own commercial risk. Crucially, Art. 1119(2) clarifies that leasing the ship together with crew does not change this classification. What defines the contract is who holds operational and commercial control, not who staffed the vessel — a distinction that matters enormously when comparing this to time charter, where the shipowner retains technical management. Turkish courts have held that whether crew remain answerable to the owner or the charterer, as shown by who bears liability for crew fault under the contract's own terms, is the decisive fact in classifying an agreement as bareboat charter or something else — a classification with direct consequences, for instance, for insurance coverage exclusions tied to bareboat charters.

## Documenting the Deal and Protecting It Against a Sale

Art. 1120 lets either party demand, at its own cost, a written charter party memorializing the agreed terms — not a validity requirement, but strong evidence in any later dispute. Art. 1121 goes further: unless the contract says otherwise, either party may have the charter registered (sherh) on the Turkish Ship Registry, and once registered, this sherh obliges any subsequent buyer of the ship to let the charterer continue using it under the existing terms. Without this registration mechanism, a sale during the charter term could otherwise leave the charterer's use right — a merely personal right against the original owner — unenforceable against a new owner.

## Practical Takeaway

For foreign investors chartering or considering chartering a Turkish-flagged vessel, confirming whether an agreement is truly bareboat (versus time charter) and whether it has been registered on the ship registry are both essential due-diligence steps before relying on continued use rights against a future buyer.
