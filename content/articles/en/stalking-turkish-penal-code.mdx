Turkish criminal law generally treats an offense as complete the moment a single act is carried out. Stalking is a deliberate departure from that model. What Article 123/A of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) punishes is not one isolated act — following someone once, or sending a single message, is rarely unlawful on its own — but the coercive character that ordinary, often innocuous conduct acquires when it is repeated against a person's will. Added to the Code in May 2022, this provision was designed to reach a pattern of behavior whose danger lies less in any single incident than in its persistence and in the psychological toll it takes on the person being pursued.

## Why Turkish Law Needed a Separate Stalking Offense

Article 123/A sits in the part of the Penal Code dealing with offenses against personal liberty, immediately after the older offense of disturbing a person's peace and quiet (TCK Art. 123). Before 2022, conduct that today would be classified as stalking had to be forced into existing offenses — threats, insults, or the general peace-disturbance provision — none of which was built to capture a drawn-out campaign of unwanted contact. The new provision protects a combination of interests: a person's everyday sense of calm, their subjective feeling of safety, and their freedom to move through their own life without altering their routine. In practice, stalking victims frequently change commuting routes, working hours, or social media habits long before any physical harm occurs, and it was precisely this gap that the legislature intended to close.

## Two Forms of Conduct: Physical Following and Attempted Contact

The offense can be committed in either of two ways. The first is physically following the victim — trailing them, waiting at places they frequent, or repeatedly appearing near their home or workplace. The second is attempting to reach the victim from a distance, whether through communication or information technology tools or through third parties acting on the offender's behalf. Phone calls, messaging apps, social media accounts, and even fake profiles created to approach the victim indirectly all fall within this second category.

Two features of the wording matter. First, the law criminalizes attempting to make contact, not succeeding at it — a victim who never answers, blocks the offender, or never actually communicates with them does not deprive the conduct of its criminal character. Second, using a third party as an intermediary does not shield the offender: sending repeated messages through friends or acquaintances is treated no differently than contacting the victim directly.

## The Persistence Requirement: Where the Threshold Lies

What separates stalking from a single unwelcome encounter is persistence. The Code sets no fixed numerical threshold — how many calls, how many follow-ups, over what span of time — and leaves that assessment to the trial judge. What the law is looking for is whether the repetition created, in the victim's experience, a sense of continuity and inevitability. A single phone call or a one-off instance of following someone will not amount to persistence, but dozens of messages sent in quick succession can qualify just as much as a sparser, occasional pattern of contact stretched out over months. Frequency matters, but so does whether the conduct continued after the victim made clear that it was unwanted.

## The Result Element: Serious Unease or Fear for Safety

Stalking is a result-based offense, meaning it is not complete merely because the persistent conduct occurred — it must also have produced one of two specified outcomes: causing serious unease in the victim, or causing the victim to fear for their own safety or that of someone close to them. Either outcome suffices, and because concern for a third party's safety is enough, the class of people the provision protects is not limited to the direct target of the stalking.

The qualifier "serious" is deliberate: it prevents the offense from being satisfied by a bare, subjective claim of discomfort. Courts apply something closer to a mixed standard — the victim's actual circumstances are considered, but the question also asks whether a reasonable person in the same situation would have experienced comparable unease. Where the persistent conduct actually drove the victim to change schools, jobs, or housing, that outcome stands as the clearest evidence that the required result has been reached.

## Distinguishing Stalking From Disturbing the Peace (TCK Art. 123)

Comparing Article 123/A with the older Article 123 clarifies both provisions. Article 123 requires that the offender act with the specific purpose of disturbing the victim's peace and quiet, and it is satisfied once that peace is in fact disturbed; it carries a penalty of three months to one year's imprisonment. Stalking under Article 123/A requires no such specific purpose — an offender who claims to be motivated by affection, a wish to reconcile, or an attempt to persuade the victim can still be convicted — but in exchange it demands a more serious result: genuine serious unease or fear for one's safety, not mere disturbance of peace. Where a single course of conduct satisfies the elements of both offenses, the more severely punished stalking provision takes precedence.

## Fault Requirement

Stalking can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent version of the offense. It is enough that the offender was aware that their conduct was persistent and capable of causing the victim unease or safety concerns, and acted regardless — dolus eventualis (acting while accepting that outcome as possible) satisfies this standard. Because no special motive is required, an offender's claim of good intentions — romantic interest, pursuing a debt, wanting to see a child — does not negate intent. What matters more for establishing intent is whether the victim's refusal was actually communicated to and understood by the offender.

## Aggravating Circumstances

The second paragraph of Article 123/A sets out three situations that raise the penalty: the offense is committed against a child, or against a spouse from whom the offender is separated or divorced; the conduct causes the victim to change school, workplace, or residence, or to leave their job or studies; or the offense is committed by someone already subject to a protective restraining order barring them from approaching the victim's home, school, or workplace. The first reflects the reality that stalking most often arises out of failed intimate relationships; the second separately punishes the clearest concrete manifestation of the harm; and the third ties the offense directly to enforcement of Turkey's domestic-violence protection framework under Law No. 6284, since it targets conduct that continues in defiance of an existing protective order.

## Attempt, Complicity, and Concurrence With Other Offenses

Because stalking is a result-based offense, an attempt is theoretically possible — persistent conduct may occur without the required result ever materializing — although in practice this is uncommon, since behavior that reaches the threshold of persistence tends to produce the required unease or fear as well. General rules on complicity apply: a third party who knowingly assists the offender in making contact is liable as an accomplice, while one who acts without that knowledge does not incur liability. On concurrence, the key issue is how to treat multiple repeated acts: since persistence is already built into the definition of the offense, repeated conduct against the same victim is treated as a single offense rather than triggering the rules on continuing (chain) offenses. Independent offenses committed in the course of the stalking — threats, insults, sexual harassment, or violation of the inviolability of the home — are punished separately under the ordinary rules on concurrence of offenses.

## Penalties and Procedure

The basic form of the offense carries a sentence of six months to two years' imprisonment. Where one of the aggravating circumstances in the second paragraph applies, the sentence rises to one to three years. Prosecution is conditional on a complaint by the victim, and that complaint must be filed within six months of learning the identity of the offender and the facts of the offense. Jurisdiction lies with the courts of first instance for criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi). Although the offense is subject to a complaint requirement, the 2022 amending law expressly excluded it from mediation — a choice made in view of the victim's vulnerable position, and one that in practice is applied alongside the protective and preventive measures available under Law No. 6284. Because the offense is still relatively new, settled case law remains limited, and the boundaries of concepts such as "persistence" and "serious unease" are expected to be shaped further by appellate decisions in the years ahead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a single message or a one-time instance of following someone count as stalking?**
Generally no. The offense requires persistence — a pattern the court finds created a sense of continuity for the victim. An isolated act, evaluated on its own, typically will not meet that threshold, though it may still be relevant under other provisions, such as disturbing the peace.

**Does it matter that the person contacting the victim believed their intentions were good?**
No. Because Article 123/A does not require any specific motive, claims of affection, a wish to reconcile, or a desire to see a child do not remove criminal intent once the conduct is persistent and capable of causing serious unease or safety fear.

**How long does a victim have to file a complaint, and can the case be resolved through mediation?**
The complaint must be filed within six months of learning who the offender is and what occurred. Unlike many complaint-based offenses, stalking is expressly excluded from mediation, so the case proceeds through ordinary criminal prosecution once a valid complaint is filed.

**What makes the penalty increase from the basic range of six months to two years?**
Three circumstances raise the sentence to one to three years: the victim is a child or a separated/divorced spouse of the offender; the conduct forces the victim to change school, job, or residence; or the offender acts in violation of an existing restraining order.
