Collective bargaining between a union and an employer does not always end in agreement. When the parties cannot reach a collective labour agreement within the periods set out by law, Law No. 6356 on Trade Unions and Collective Labour Agreements (STISK) does not leave the parties to simply walk away or move straight to a strike. It sets up a staged dispute-resolution track: first official mediation, then — only in defined circumstances — the High Board of Arbitration, and, at any point the parties agree, private arbitration. The purpose of this structure is to push the parties toward a negotiated outcome before resorting to strikes or lockouts, and, importantly, going through mediation is itself a legal precondition for a lawful strike decision. For employers and HR teams operating in Turkey — particularly foreign-owned businesses unfamiliar with this framework — understanding how the stages fit together, and how strictly the statutory deadlines are enforced, is essential to protecting both the bargaining position and the union's authority to conclude the agreement.

## Establishing That a Dispute Exists

Mediation does not start automatically. Article 49 of Law No. 6356 requires that the failure of collective bargaining first be formally established through one of four scenarios: one party fails to show up at the first scheduled bargaining meeting, or shows up but does not begin negotiating; a party stops attending after negotiations have begun; the parties jointly record, in a minutes, that they cannot reach agreement; or the sixty-day statutory bargaining period simply expires without an agreement. Once any of these situations occurs, one of the parties must notify the competent authority of the dispute within six working days. Failing to do so is not a minor procedural lapse — it causes the employee union to lose its authority to conclude the collective agreement altogether. Turkish courts treat this six-working-day period as a strict, non-extendable time limit running from the date of the missed meeting or the minutes recording the disagreement, and the competent authority may close the file on its own initiative once the deadline passes, without needing to serve any further notice on the parties.

## Appointment of an Official Mediator and the Mediation Process

Once the dispute notice reaches the competent authority, Article 50 governs what happens next in detail. The authority must appoint a mediator from the official mediator roster within six working days; the parties may instead agree on a name from that same roster, in which case the authority appoints the person they have chosen. The mediator's function is to unblock bargaining that has stalled and to put forward proposals that help the parties reach agreement. The mediator's term runs for fifteen days from notification of the appointment and can be extended by up to six further working days if the parties agree. If mediation succeeds, the resulting agreement takes effect as a collective labour agreement. If it does not, the mediator must draft minutes recording the dispute within three working days, attach any recommendations considered useful, and submit the file to the competent authority, which must then serve the minutes on the parties within a further three working days. The date of that service is a critical turning point, because it is what starts the clock for the next stage of the process.

## When Mediation Fails: Applying to the High Board of Arbitration

A failed mediation does not automatically send the parties to the High Board of Arbitration (Yüksek Hakem Kurulu). As a rule, the next step is the strike-and-lockout procedure. Under Article 51, recourse to the High Board is only available in a limited set of circumstances defined by law: where a strike ballot has produced a final decision against striking; where the dispute concerns work or a workplace in which strikes and lockouts are prohibited, running from service of the mediation minutes; or where a postponed strike or lockout remains unresolved by the time the postponement period expires. In any of these situations, either party may apply to the High Board within six working days, and again, missing this deadline causes the employee union to lose its bargaining authority. The High Board is a constitutional body chaired by the head of the Court of Cassation chamber responsible for labour cases and composed of worker, employer and state representatives. Its decisions are final, are not subject to further appeal, and take direct effect as a collective labour agreement. That finality, however, does not put the underlying process entirely beyond judicial reach: while the High Board's substantive decision cannot itself be appealed, preliminary questions on which that decision rests — for example, whether a given workplace actually falls within a strike-and-lockout prohibition — can still be examined by the courts.

## Private Arbitration: A Voluntary Alternative

Article 52 offers a route that, unlike the compulsory nature of the High Board, is entirely voluntary: the parties may jointly agree to submit a collective dispute to a private arbitrator at any stage. This can happen in two ways. First, the collective labour agreement itself may contain a clause providing that future disputes will go to a private arbitrator at the request of either party; absent a contrary provision, the arbitration rules of the Code of Civil Procedure (Law No. 6100) apply, and decisions on collective rights disputes are subject to the ordinary legal regime that governs arbitral awards generally. Second, once a collective interest dispute has actually arisen, the parties may agree in writing to submit it to private arbitration; in that case, the rules on mediation, strikes, lockouts and statutory arbitration cease to apply altogether, and the private arbitrator's award on the interest dispute takes effect directly as a collective labour agreement. The law also allows the parties to appoint the High Board of Arbitration itself to act as their chosen private arbitrator at any stage of the dispute.

## Judicial Oversight of the Process

The most litigated question in practice is whether the finality of High Board decisions excludes judicial review altogether. Turkish courts have consistently confirmed that the High Board's substantive award — which takes effect as a collective labour agreement — cannot be appealed. At the same time, courts accept that a separate declaratory action may be brought to examine the preliminary issues underlying that award, such as whether a particular workplace is genuinely covered by a strike-and-lockout prohibition, since that determination belongs to the judiciary rather than to the High Board. Similarly, settled case law holds that a private-arbitration clause in a collective agreement does not strip an employee of the right to bring an individual claim, nor does it remove the labour court's public-policy-based jurisdiction over such claims. The short statutory deadlines running through the entire process — six working days, fifteen days, three working days — are applied strictly as time-bar periods, and administrative acts such as the appointment of an official mediator or the conduct of the bargaining process can themselves be challenged before the labour courts, independent of the underlying dispute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What happens if a party misses the six-working-day deadline to notify the dispute under Article 49?**
The employee union loses its authority to conclude the collective labour agreement. Turkish courts treat this deadline as strict and non-extendable, and the competent authority can close the process without further notice to the parties.

**Can the parties choose who acts as the official mediator?**
Yes. Although the competent authority normally appoints a mediator from the official roster, the parties may instead agree on a specific name from that roster, and the authority will appoint the person they have chosen.

**Is every failed mediation followed by an application to the High Board of Arbitration?**
No. Recourse to the High Board is only available in the limited situations set out in Article 51 — for example, following a strike ballot against striking, or in workplaces where strikes and lockouts are prohibited. Otherwise, the strike-and-lockout procedure is the next step.

**Are High Board of Arbitration decisions completely immune from court review?**
The High Board's substantive decision, which takes effect as a collective labour agreement, cannot be appealed. However, preliminary questions underlying that decision — such as whether a workplace falls under a strike-and-lockout ban — can still be examined through a separate action before the labour courts.
