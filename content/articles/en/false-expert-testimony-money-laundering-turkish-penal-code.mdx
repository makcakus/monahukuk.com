Two provisions of the Turkish Penal Code that rarely appear in the same conversation are, in practice, closely related in Turkish criminal defense work: Article 276, which criminalizes a false statement by a court-appointed expert or interpreter, and Article 282, which criminalizes laundering assets derived from another criminal offense. They protect different interests — the accuracy of judicial fact-finding on one hand, the transparency of the financial system on the other — but both are built around the same structural idea: a person occupying a position of institutional trust abuses that position, and the law responds with a criminal sanction rather than leaving the matter to civil liability. For businesses and individuals involved in Turkish litigation or cross-border asset transfers, understanding how these two offenses are defined, who can be charged, and what mental state the prosecution must prove is essential to assessing exposure and building a defense.

## Two Offenses, One Underlying Logic

Article 276 sits within the Penal Code's chapter on offenses against the judiciary, while Article 282 sits within the chapter on offenses against society and the economic order. Despite that separation, both offenses share a defining technical feature: they can only be committed by a person who occupies a specific role or status, not by the general public. In Article 276, that role is a court-appointed expert or interpreter; in Article 282, no formal role is required, but the offense is structurally anchored to the existence of an earlier, separate criminal act — the so-called predicate offense — from which the laundered assets must originate. This shared architecture means that in both cases, a defense strategy typically turns on either disproving the defendant's qualifying status or role, or disproving the mental element the prosecution must establish, rather than disputing the underlying facts in isolation.

## False Expert Opinion or Interpretation: Who Can Be Charged

Article 276 criminalizes a false statement made by an expert or interpreter who has been formally appointed by a court, or by any authority or body legally empowered to conduct a criminal investigation or to hear witnesses under oath. The offense is structurally close to perjury, but it is a distinct crime defined by the status of the person making the statement. A private consultant hired informally by one of the parties — someone the court itself never appointed — does not fall within this article, even if that person's opinion is inaccurate or self-serving; at most, other general provisions might apply. This distinction matters in practice, because whether a given individual qualifies as a "court-appointed" expert or interpreter is often the first and most consequential question in any Article 276 case.

The conduct element is narrow: the expert's opinion, or the interpreter's translation of a statement or document, must diverge from objective reality. No further consequence is required — the offense does not depend on the court's decision actually changing, or on any party suffering concrete harm as a result. This makes it what Turkish doctrine describes as an abstract-danger offense: the risk to the integrity of the proceeding is treated as sufficient in itself. As for the mental element, the offense can only be committed intentionally. The expert or interpreter must know that the opinion or translation being submitted is inaccurate and proceed to submit it anyway. A mistaken diagnosis, a flawed technical assessment, or an inaccurate translation produced through negligence rather than knowing falsity does not trigger criminal liability under this article — that scenario instead falls within the expert's civil liability for professional error, addressed through compensation claims rather than criminal sanction.

Turkish law also gives weight to correction before the fact: if the false statement is corrected before the judgment in the underlying proceeding becomes final, that correction can work in the defendant's favor, mirroring the logic applied to retracted false witness testimony. In practice, proving that an expert report is actually false is rarely straightforward. It is typically established through a subsequent report from a different panel of experts, technical data already in the case file, or internal inconsistencies within the report itself — the mere fact that a report's conclusion disadvantaged one of the parties does not, by itself, establish that the report was false.

## Money Laundering: Why the Predicate Offense Is Everything

Article 282, commonly referred to as money laundering, is defined by one non-negotiable requirement: the assets in question must be traceable to a specific predicate offense that itself carries a minimum statutory sentence of six months' imprisonment or more, whether that offense was committed inside or outside Turkey. Whether the predicate offense has actually been prosecuted, or whether that prosecution has concluded, does not affect whether the laundering offense itself is complete — what matters is whether the tainted origin of the assets can be established, not whether a separate conviction for the source crime exists. In practice, Turkish prosecutors and courts frequently rely on circumstantial indicators to establish that origin: sudden and unexplained increases in wealth, spending or transfer patterns disproportionate to any documented lawful income, and layered transactions running through chains of shell entities.

The conduct that constitutes laundering is broad by design: moving assets abroad, disguising their illicit origin, or subjecting them to transactions intended to create the appearance of a legitimate source — inter-account transfers, layering through corporate structures, or conversion into physical assets are all examples. The mental element is what separates laundering from simple possession or participation in the predicate crime: the defendant must both know that the assets originate from criminal conduct and act with the specific purpose of concealing that illicit origin or creating an appearance of legitimacy. That purpose requirement is the dividing line the prosecution must prove, and it is frequently the most contested issue in a laundering defense. Separately, Turkish law imposes a lighter sanction on a person who, without having participated in the predicate offense, later acquires, receives, holds, or uses the asset while aware of its tainted origin — a provision aimed at bad-faith participants further down the laundering chain.

## Aggravating Factors and the Route to Leniency

Sentencing is increased where the offense is committed by a public official, or by a member of a specific profession acting in the course of that profession, and increased further where the offense is carried out within the activity of an organized criminal group. Legal entities can also face security measures of their own, reflecting the reality that laundering is frequently executed through corporate vehicles — shell companies and financial intermediaries in particular. At the same time, Turkish law creates considerable room for leniency: a defendant who, before the investigation begins, either ensures that the tainted assets are recovered or notifies the competent authorities in a way that facilitates their recovery, is not punished for the offense. This mechanism is designed to encourage cooperation and improve the odds of actually recovering laundered assets, rather than pursuing punishment for its own sake.

## The Financial Intelligence Link

Money laundering investigations in Turkey are closely tied to the financial intelligence and suspicious-transaction reporting regime established under Law No. 5549 on the Prevention of Laundering Proceeds of Crime. Reports filed with Turkey's financial intelligence unit, MASAK, are frequently the starting point of a criminal investigation, giving prosecutors their first concrete evidentiary basis for opening a case. Because banks and other financial institutions carry a statutory reporting obligation, a substantial share of laundering investigations originates through this administrative channel rather than from a criminal complaint. Article 276 proceedings follow a different track: because a false expert opinion or translation typically surfaces as an incidental issue within an existing case, it is usually investigated as a separate matter once the presiding court notices the possible offense and refers it to the public prosecutor's office. In both offenses, the common thread is that a role or position the public relies on — professional expertise, or the appearance of a legitimate financial transaction — has allegedly been misused, and any defense necessarily focuses on disproving either that status or the required intent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a private expert hired by one of the parties, rather than appointed by the court, be prosecuted under Article 276?**
Generally no. Article 276 is a status-based offense that requires formal appointment by a court or another authority legally empowered to investigate or take testimony under oath. A privately retained consultant who was never appointed by the court typically falls outside this article.

**Does a mistaken expert report automatically amount to a crime?**
No. Article 276 requires intent — the expert must know the opinion is false and submit it anyway. An honest mistake, misdiagnosis, or negligent error is a matter for civil liability and compensation, not criminal prosecution.

**Does a person have to be convicted of the underlying crime before someone else can be charged with laundering its proceeds?**
No. What matters is whether the prosecution can establish that the assets originated from a predicate offense carrying a minimum sentence of six months or more. A separate conviction of the predicate offense's perpetrator is not a precondition, and that offense need not even have been prosecuted.

**What is the difference between money laundering and simply receiving stolen or tainted property?**
The key distinguishing element is purpose. Laundering requires that the defendant act specifically to conceal the illicit origin of the assets or to create an appearance of legitimacy. Turkish law separately, and more leniently, punishes a person who later knowingly acquires or uses tainted assets without having participated in disguising their origin.
