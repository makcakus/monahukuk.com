TTK Art. 1182-1183 round out the carrier liability framework with the "excepted perils" list — functionally close to Hague-Visby Rules Art. IV(2) — and a rule for losses caused by more than one factor at once.

## Eight Excepted Perils

Art. 1182/1 presumes the carrier and its servants fault-free when damage stems from: perils of the sea; war, riots, public enemies, official orders, or quarantine restrictions; judicial seizure; strikes, lockouts, or other labor stoppages; acts or omissions of the shipper, charterer, owner, or their representatives; inherent volume/weight loss or latent defects in the cargo itself; inadequate packing; or inadequate marking. These fall into four natural groups: sea perils, state/collective action, cargo-side conduct, and the cargo's own inherent condition — each covering causes a diligent carrier could not have prevented.

## Limits on the List

Two safeguards keep this list from becoming a blanket shield. Art. 1182/2 strips the carrier of the defense if an event the carrier is responsible for actually caused one of the eight listed perils to arise — an unseaworthy ship that couldn't withstand an ordinary sea peril, for instance, doesn't let the carrier hide behind "perils of the sea." Art. 1182/3 works the other way, in the carrier's favor: if it's merely *probable*, given the circumstances, that the loss stemmed from one of the eight causes, that cause is presumed — though the presumption is rebuttable.

## Concurrent Causation: Partial Liability, But the Carrier Must Prove the Split

Art. 1183 addresses losses caused partly by the carrier's fault and partly by an unrelated cause: the carrier answers only for the portion attributable to its own fault — but only if the carrier itself proves what portion is *not* attributable to it. Failing that proof, the carrier risks being held liable for the entire loss. This inverts the ordinary rule that a claimant must prove causation; here, the carrier — as the party with the best knowledge of what happened aboard — bears the burden of disentangling causes.

## Practical Takeaway

For cargo interests, these provisions mean the excepted-perils list is not automatically a dead end: courts scrutinize whether the carrier's own conduct (inadequate stowage supervision, an open-top container used without checking packing) contributed to the loss before accepting a peril defense. Turkish courts have applied the packaging-inadequacy exception narrowly, distinguishing internal packing failures from external impact damage the carrier should have guarded against.
