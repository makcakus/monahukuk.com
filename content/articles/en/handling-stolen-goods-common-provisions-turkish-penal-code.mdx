Turkish criminal law does not stop at punishing the person who commits a theft, a fraud or a breach of trust. It also reaches everyone downstream of that crime: the person who later buys or accepts the stolen property, the person who innocently receives it and then stays silent after learning its origin, and even the legal entity that ends up benefiting from the proceeds. The Turkish Penal Code (TCK) groups these situations together in a cluster of closing provisions to the chapter on property offenses — Articles 165 through 169 — each doing a distinct job. Foreign nationals and businesses operating in Turkey are most likely to encounter Article 165, which criminalizes handling stolen goods, but understanding how it interacts with the surrounding articles is essential to assessing exposure or building a defense.

## Handling Stolen Goods as an Independent Offense (Article 165)

Article 165 punishes a person who, without having taken part in the underlying crime, sells, transfers, buys or accepts property or other assets that were obtained through that crime, with a prison sentence ranging from six months to three years plus a judicial fine. The decisive feature of this offense is timing: the conduct occurs after the original crime — the theft, the fraud, the breach of trust — has already been completed. The person charged under Article 165 contributed nothing to how the property was obtained; they step in afterward, seeking to benefit from what already exists. This is precisely what separates Article 165 from complicity in the underlying offense: an accomplice assists during or before the crime itself, while the Article 165 offender only appears once the crime is over. The rule exists to discourage a secondary market for criminal proceeds — conduct that makes it harder for stolen property to find its way back to its rightful owner and easier for criminal gains to re-enter ordinary economic circulation. The victim of an Article 165 offense remains, in substance, the victim of the original crime.

On the mental element, the law requires that the person knew the property came from a crime. Ordinary criminal-intent principles apply, so acquiring the item through carelessness or without realizing its origin does not trigger the article — negligence is not enough. In practice, this knowledge is usually inferred from circumstantial factors: an unusually low price, the absence of any invoice or paperwork, or acquiring goods from a seller whose reliability was plainly doubtful are the kinds of facts courts treat as evidence that the buyer knew, or must have known, what they were dealing with.

## Failing to Report Property Later Discovered to Be Stolen (Article 166)

Article 166 addresses a different scenario. Here, the person acquired the property lawfully — through an ordinary legal transaction, with no criminal intent at the time — and only afterward discovers that it was in fact obtained through a crime. The acquisition itself is not the problem; the offense lies in what happens next. Once the person learns the truth, the law imposes a duty to notify the authorities responsible for investigating the offense without delay, and failing to do so is what is punished. Unlike Article 165, Article 166 is what is known as an offense of omission — the person does not "do" anything; they simply withhold information they are now obligated to share. The dividing line between the two articles is exactly this timing of knowledge: under Article 165, the buyer already knows the item is tainted at the moment of acquisition, which makes the acquisition itself a crime; under Article 166, knowledge arrives only after a lawful acquisition, and it is silence in the face of that knowledge that becomes the offense.

## Family-Based Immunity in Property Offenses (Article 167)

Article 167 reflects a deliberate policy choice rather than a definition of any new offense. It provides that, with the important exception of extortion and aggravated extortion, property offenses within this chapter of the Code committed against certain close relatives are treated differently depending on how close the relationship is. Where the offense is committed against a spouse (provided the couple has not been legally separated), against an ascendant or descendant relative or an in-law of the same degree, against an adoptive parent or adopted child, or against a sibling who lives in the same household, the relative responsible for the offense receives no punishment at all. Where the offense instead involves a legally separated spouse, a sibling who does not share the same household, or an uncle, aunt, nephew or niece, or a second-degree in-law who does live in the same household, the penalty — which is prosecuted only upon complaint — is reduced by half. Note that for this second, wider group of relatives, cohabitation is a genuine precondition: an uncle, aunt, nephew or niece living apart from the offender falls outside the reduction entirely, since the law only extends leniency to this more distant category when an actual shared household exists.

This mechanism does not make the underlying conduct lawful; it is a personal bar to punishment (or a personal reduction) that applies only to the relative in question. Any non-relative who took part in the same offense is judged normally, since the immunity or reduction is strictly personal to the family member and does not extend to co-offenders. The underlying policy is straightforward: the legislature prefers that property disputes within a family be resolved, where possible, inside the family rather than through criminal prosecution.

## Effective Repentance and Reduced Sentences (Article 168)

Article 168 introduces a restorative mechanism that applies once offenses such as theft, damage to property, breach of trust, fraud, and fraudulent or negligent bankruptcy have already been completed. If the offender, the instigator or an accomplice voluntarily returns the exact property or otherwise fully compensates the victim's loss, the sentence is reduced — with the size of the reduction tied to how early the restitution happens. Repentance shown before prosecution begins — meaning during the investigation stage, up until the indictment is accepted — can reduce the sentence by up to two-thirds; repentance shown after prosecution has started but before judgment is rendered can reduce it by up to half. For extortion specifically, these two ceilings drop to one-half and one-third respectively. Where only partial restitution or compensation is made, the reduction is only available with the victim's consent. A separate provision dealing with the offense of benefiting from lost or misplaced property allows for no prosecution at all where full compensation is made before the investigation concludes, and a one-third reduction where full compensation is made before judgment — though a person may not rely on this particular route more than twice. The underlying logic favors making the victim whole over pure punishment: the earlier the timing and the fuller the compensation, the greater the benefit to the offender.

## Security Measures Against Legal Entities (Article 169)

Article 169 addresses situations where a legal entity — a company or similar organization — ends up with an unlawful benefit as a result of theft, breach of trust or fraud committed on its behalf. A legal entity cannot bear criminal responsibility in the way an individual can, but where it has materially profited from the crime, it can still be made subject to its own dedicated security measures. This ensures that criminal law's reach extends past the individuals who acted and captures unlawful gains sheltered behind a corporate structure.

## Attempt, Complicity and Procedural Notes

Whether an attempt can exist under Article 165 depends on whether the acts aimed at acquiring the property were left incomplete; ordinary complicity rules are already excluded by the article's own definition, since anyone who participated in the underlying crime is treated as an offender in that crime, not under Article 165. Articles 167 and 168 do not define offenses in their own right, so questions of attempt or complicity do not arise for them — they are rules that affect how an already-established underlying offense is punished. On the procedural side, the reduced-penalty branch of Article 167 is expressly complaint-dependent, while the outright bar to punishment under its first paragraph is applied by the court on its own motion. Successfully invoking these provisions in practice requires solid evidence of the relationship between victim and offender, the precise degree of kinship, whether the parties actually shared a household, the timing of any restitution, and the scope of compensation actually made.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it a defense that I did not know the goods I bought were stolen?**
Yes, in principle. Article 165 requires actual knowledge that the property came from a crime; acquiring it without that knowledge, even carelessly, does not satisfy the offense. Courts look at circumstantial indicators — price, documentation, the seller's reliability — to decide whether the buyer genuinely knew or must have known.

**What should I do if I later find out that something I bought legally was originally stolen?**
Under Article 166, once you learn the property's true origin, you are expected to notify the competent authorities without delay. Staying silent after learning the truth is itself what the law punishes, even though your original acquisition was lawful.

**Does the family exemption under Article 167 mean the conduct was not a crime?**
No. It is a personal bar to punishment or a personal sentence reduction that applies only to the qualifying relative — it does not erase the unlawfulness of the act, and it does not extend to any non-relative involved in the same offense.

**How much can effective repentance under Article 168 reduce a sentence?**
It depends on timing and completeness. Full restitution or compensation made before prosecution begins can reduce the sentence by up to two-thirds, and up to half if made after prosecution starts but before judgment; for extortion the applicable ceilings are one-half and one-third. Partial restitution requires the victim's consent to qualify for any reduction.
