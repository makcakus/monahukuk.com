TTK Art. 1190 closes a loophole that would otherwise let claimants sidestep the entire carrier liability framework with a simple relabeling exercise.

## No Escaping the Regime by Suing in Tort

Every defense and liability limit built up across Art. 1178-1189 — exoneration grounds, the excepted-perils list, SDR-based caps, notice and inspection requirements, the one-year preclusive period — applies to *every* claim arising from cargo loss, damage, or delay, "whether based on tort or another cause." Without this rule, a claimant could bypass all of the carrier-protective machinery simply by framing the same claim as a general tort action under the Turkish Code of Obligations rather than a contractual one. This mirrors the international policy behind Hague-Visby Rules Art. IV bis, which closes the identical loophole for common-law tort claims.

## The Protection Extends to the Carrier's Servants

If a claim targets a servant of the carrier instead — the master, an officer, or anyone acting on the carrier's behalf — that person can invoke the same defenses and limits, but only by proving they were acting within the scope of their duties or authority. A servant who went beyond that scope, or acted on a frolic of their own, gets no such protection merely from having an employment relationship with the carrier. Consistent with the regime's general pattern, the burden of proving this scope-of-duty condition sits with the servant claiming it.

## One Loss, One Ceiling

The combined total recoverable from the carrier and its servants together cannot exceed the single Art. 1186 liability ceiling (subject to the Art. 1187 exception for reckless conduct). This closes a second potential loophole: without it, a claimant naming multiple defendants — the carrier plus several individual crew members — could multiply total exposure simply by adding parties. The cap attaches to the loss event, not to each defendant.

## Practical Takeaway

Reported case law confirms the practical bite of this provision: even where a claim is pleaded in tort against an actual carrier, Turkish courts have held that the carrier's contractual defenses and exoneration grounds still apply in full — the label on the lawsuit doesn't change the underlying risk allocation.
