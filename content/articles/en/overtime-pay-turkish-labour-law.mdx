Overtime is one of the most frequently litigated wage issues in Turkish employment practice, and Article 41 of Labour Code No. 4857 is the provision that governs it. The rule starts from a simple baseline: the standard working week in Turkey is 45 hours, and any work beyond that threshold is, in principle, exceptional rather than routine — it may only be required for reasons such as the general interest of the country, the nature of the work, or the need to increase production, and it must be compensated at a premium rate or offset with paid time off. For foreign investors and HR teams managing staff in Turkey, understanding how this threshold, the premium rates, and the employee's right to choose between pay and time off actually work in practice is essential, because overtime disputes are one of the most common sources of labour litigation after termination.

## The 45-Hour Threshold and the 50% Overtime Premium

Under Article 41, "overtime" (fazla çalışma) refers specifically to hours worked beyond the statutory 45-hour week, and each such hour must be paid at the normal hourly wage rate increased by 50 percent. This threshold is not always measured on a strict week-by-week basis. Where a workplace applies an averaging (equalization) arrangement under Article 63 of the Labour Code, an employee's working hours can be averaged across a reference period; as long as the *average* weekly working time does not exceed the normal weekly limit, hours that spike above 45 in a particular week are not treated as overtime at all. This mechanism is what allows employers with seasonal or fluctuating workloads to schedule longer weeks at peak times without triggering a 50% premium every single week, provided the average stays within bounds over the agreed period.

A separate category exists for workplaces where the contractual weekly working time is set *below* 45 hours. Hours worked beyond that shorter contractual figure but still within the 45-hour ceiling are called "work with extended hours" (fazla sürelerle çalışma) rather than overtime proper, and they carry a lower premium of 25 percent rather than 50 percent. This distinction matters most in part-time or reduced-hour arrangements, where the line between the two categories determines which premium applies.

## Proving Overtime: Timekeeping Records, Witnesses and Judicial Reductions

Because overtime disputes typically surface after the employment relationship has ended, proof becomes central. Turkish courts generally rely on workplace attendance records (time sheets, clock-in/clock-out data) and, where such records are absent or incomplete, on witness testimony. Where an employer produces signed or otherwise unchallenged timekeeping records showing the actual hours worked, Turkish case law does not allow an employee to rebut those records with witness statements alone — the documentary record prevails. Conversely, where no written record exists, overtime can be proven through witness evidence. In practice, however, courts scrutinize testimony-based overtime claims for plausibility, and where the overtime hours alleged appear excessive relative to ordinary working conditions and life patterns, they apply a discretionary equitable reduction to the amount awarded rather than accepting the claimed hours at face value.

## Choosing Between Premium Pay and Compensatory Time Off

Article 41 gives the employee — not the employer — the right to choose how overtime is compensated. An employee who has worked overtime or extended hours may request the premium wage, or may instead take compensatory time off: one hour and thirty minutes of free time for each hour of overtime worked, and one hour and fifteen minutes for each hour of extended-hours work. This choice belongs exclusively to the employee; the employer cannot impose the time-off option unilaterally or force a particular outcome. Time off earned this way must be used within six months, during normal working hours, and without any deduction from wages; if it is not granted within that period, it converts back into a premium-wage entitlement.

A recurring source of dispute is employers attempting, after a lawsuit has already been filed or without the employee's consent, to unilaterally grant compensatory time off and then offset it against an overtime wage claim. Turkish appellate case law treats this kind of retroactive, unconsented "netting off" as contrary to the statute — an offset can only be recognized where there is a properly executed record reflecting the employee's own choice and consent.

## Consent, the Annual Cap, and Special Restrictions

Employers cannot simply direct staff to work overtime: Article 41 requires the employee's consent, which may be given as a general clause in the employment contract or obtained separately for each specific instance of overtime. The law also protects employee health and rest by capping total overtime at 270 hours per year. Overtime is prohibited outright in certain categories of work: short or limited-duration jobs assigned for health reasons under Article 63, and night work under Article 69.

Case law has carved out a significant — but not absolute — exception for senior executives. An employee occupying a genuine senior managerial position, whose salary already reflects the weight of their duties and who can independently set their own working schedule, is generally not entitled to a separate overtime claim. This exception is not automatic, however: where such a manager is still subject to instructions from another manager or a company partner at the same workplace and cannot freely determine their own hours, the overtime claim can resurface despite the nominal title.

## Compulsory and Extraordinary Overtime Under Articles 42 and 43

Beyond the general regime of Article 41, the Labour Code recognizes two narrower categories of overtime for exceptional circumstances. Article 42 permits overtime — for all or part of the workforce, and only to the extent needed to keep the workplace running normally — in the event of an actual or anticipated breakdown, urgent repair work on machinery or equipment, or the occurrence of force majeure; in such cases the employer must grant an appropriate rest period to the employees involved, and the wage premiums remain governed by the same 50%/25% structure set out in Article 41. Article 43 addresses a purely national-emergency scenario: during mobilization, in workplaces meeting national defense needs, presidential decree may extend daily working hours up to an employee's maximum working capacity, but wages for such work are still calculated under the same premium rules found in the first three paragraphs of Article 41. Both provisions confirm that departures from the ordinary overtime regime are permitted only for concrete, legally specified reasons, while the underlying premium-wage protection is preserved even in these exceptional situations.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can my employer require me to work overtime without my consent?**
No. Article 41 requires the employee's consent to overtime, whether given generally in the employment contract or for each specific instance. Certain categories of work — such as night work and short-duration health-related assignments — prohibit overtime altogether regardless of consent.

**Can my employer decide unilaterally to give me time off instead of paying the overtime premium?**
No. The choice between premium wages and compensatory time off belongs to the employee, not the employer. Turkish case law has specifically rejected attempts by employers to impose time off, or to offset previously worked overtime against time off granted without the employee's consent, particularly once litigation has begun.

**Is there a limit on how much overtime I can be required to work in a year?**
Yes. Article 41 caps total overtime at 270 hours per year, reflecting the law's underlying concern for employee health and rest.

**If I am a senior manager, am I still entitled to overtime pay?**
Generally not, if your compensation already reflects your managerial responsibilities and you can independently set your own schedule. However, if you remain subject to another manager's or partner's instructions and cannot determine your own working hours, you may still have a valid overtime claim despite your title.
