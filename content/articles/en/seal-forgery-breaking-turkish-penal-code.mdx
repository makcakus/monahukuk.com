Turkish criminal law treats the official seal as more than a stamp of convenience — it is the physical embodiment of a state authority's approval, prohibition, or protective measure, and the Penal Code criminalizes attacks on it in two distinct ways. Article 202 targets the counterfeiting of a seal itself, while the neighboring Article 203 targets defiance of a genuine seal that has been lawfully affixed. Foreign businesses and individuals encountering a customs seal, a notarial seal, a seizure seal on premises, or any similar official mark in Turkey should understand that interfering with either can trigger criminal exposure, even where no tangible harm results from the act. This article explains how the two offenses are structured, what separates them, and how Turkish courts and prosecutors typically approach them.

## The Public Interest Behind Seal Protection

Both offenses sit within the Penal Code's chapter on crimes against public trust, and both exist to protect the same underlying interest: society's confidence that when a public authority expresses its will through a seal, that expression can be relied upon. A seal is a physical vehicle for an official approval, a prohibition, or a protective measure — when confidence in what it represents is undermined, the damage extends beyond the specific agency involved to public order generally. The two provisions protect this interest from opposite directions. Article 202 addresses the authenticity of the seal itself — someone has manufactured or used something that is not genuine. Article 203 addresses obedience to a seal that is genuine — someone has disregarded or defeated what an authentic seal was placed there to accomplish. This distinction is the single most important dividing line between the two crimes, and mischaracterizing which one applies is one of the most common sources of error in practice. Neither offense requires proof that anyone was actually deceived or that concrete harm occurred; both are complete once the underlying conduct takes place, because the law protects public trust at an abstract, preventive stage rather than waiting for a demonstrable injury.

## Seal Forgery: A Two-Tier Structure Based on Rank

Article 202 is not a single offense but two, distinguished by the rank of the authority whose seal is involved. The first tier covers seals used by the Presidency, the Grand National Assembly of Turkey (the Turkish parliament), and the Office of the Prime Minister — reflecting the constitutional weight of these institutions, forging or using a counterfeit version of their seals carries a sentence of two to eight years' imprisonment. The second tier covers approving or certifying seals used by public institutions and agencies, or by professional bodies with public-institution status — the seals of notaries, universities, bar associations, or chambers of commerce are the typical examples here — with a correspondingly lighter sentence of one to six years. This tiered structure is not arbitrary: the legislature calibrated the penalty to the constitutional standing of the body behind the seal, so correctly identifying which tier a given seal falls under is decisive for how the case will be charged and punished.

The conduct itself takes one of two alternative forms: manufacturing a counterfeit seal, or using one known to be counterfeit. Manufacturing means physically or digitally reproducing the seal so that it imitates the genuine article; using means applying a seal known to be fake to a document, transaction, or object as though it were authentic. Either act on its own completes the offense — there is no requirement that the fake seal actually caused harm or deceived a third party. Because of this, the crime is understood as an abstract endangerment of public trust rather than an offense defined by a concrete outcome. No special status is required of the offender; anyone can commit it. The victim, correspondingly, is not the specific institution whose seal was copied but the public at large, although the institution itself typically appears in the proceedings as the complainant. Because manufacturing a seal is often a multi-step process, an attempt can more readily be identified and prosecuted at that stage; using a counterfeit seal, by contrast, is usually a single, instantaneous act, which makes it harder in practice to prove that the act was interrupted before completion.

As for intent, ordinary general intent suffices, but the offender must know that the seal is fake — someone who uses a counterfeit seal without any awareness of its falsity lacks the intent the offense requires. Turkish legal commentary treats manufacturing a counterfeit seal as an act that, by its nature, is normally committed with direct intent, while using one may in some circumstances be committed with a lesser form of intent if the offender foresaw the likelihood of falsity and proceeded regardless. Courts infer intent from concrete circumstances — where the seal came from, how it was used, how it was obtained — since mere suspicion or carelessness on the offender's part does not, by itself, establish the intent element.

## Breaking a Seal: Genuine Authority and the Duty of Obedience

What separates Article 203 from Article 202 is that the seal here is authentic, not counterfeit. The object of this offense is a seal placed pursuant to law or an order of a competent authority to preserve something or keep it intact — a seal affixed following a distraint, a seizure, or a closure order is the classic example. What the law protects here is not the accuracy of the seal but the obedience owed to the prohibition or precautionary measure the seal represents; the offender does not dispute the seal's genuineness, but instead defies the order it embodies. For a seal to qualify as the object of this offense, it must have been placed properly, in accordance with law or a competent authority's order — commentators have debated whether removing a seal that was affixed unlawfully in the first place even falls within the scope of this crime.

The conduct again takes alternative forms: removing the seal, or acting contrary to the purpose for which it was placed. This second option shows that the legislature did not limit the offense to the physical act of tearing off a seal — using sealed property, opening a sealed space, or any other conduct that practically defeats the purpose of the precautionary measure falls within scope as well. This broad framing reflects the view that breaking a seal is not a mere formal violation but an attack on the substance of the measure itself. No special status is required of the offender here either, though in practice this offense is most often committed by the person holding or entrusted with custody of the sealed property. The penalty — six months to three years' imprisonment or a judicial fine — is markedly lighter than under Article 202, reflecting that a genuine seal being disobeyed represents a less severe breach of order than the loss of trust caused by an outright counterfeit.

## Attempt, Concurrence, and Procedural Considerations

Attempt is theoretically possible under both offenses where the conduct can be broken into separable stages — manufacturing or removing a seal in a piecemeal fashion is the setting where an incomplete attempt is most likely to be debated. General rules on participation apply, and there is no special provision governing instigation or aiding. Where a forged seal is also applied to an official document, the relationship between Article 202 and the offense of forging an official document must be assessed according to whether the two acts can be separated on the facts of the case; because seal forgery is itself a specialized provision, it takes precedence under the principle that a more specific rule displaces a general one. Where breaking a seal is accompanied by a separate offense against the sealed property itself — misappropriation, for instance — the ordinary rules on concurrence of offenses apply, and if the offender is also a public official charged with safeguarding the seal, the relationship with the offense of misconduct in office must be considered separately as well.

Procedurally, both offenses are treated as crimes against public order and are therefore prosecuted ex officio — they do not depend on a private complaint. The applicable maximum sentence is the principal criterion used to determine which court has jurisdiction, so correctly identifying which tier of Article 202 applies affects not only the sentence but which court will hear the case. In practice, the most frequently litigated question is whether a given seal is a "counterfeit seal" under Article 202 or a "genuine but violated seal" under Article 203; getting this classification right is essential both to the sentence that will be applied and to building an effective defense strategy. That determination usually requires careful evidentiary work — examining the seal's physical characteristics, the official documentation of how and when it was affixed, and, where necessary, expert forensic examination.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the key difference between Article 202 and Article 203?**
Article 202 punishes forging or using a fake seal — the seal itself is not genuine. Article 203 punishes defying a genuine, lawfully affixed seal, such as removing it or otherwise defeating its purpose. The seal's authenticity is what separates the two offenses.

**Does the prosecution need to prove that someone was actually deceived or harmed?**
No. Both offenses are treated as complete once the prohibited conduct occurs — manufacturing or using a counterfeit seal, or removing or defeating a genuine one — regardless of whether any actual harm or deception followed.

**Why does the sentence for seal forgery vary so widely?**
Article 202 has two tiers based on the rank of the authority behind the seal. Counterfeiting the seal of the Presidency, Parliament, or the Prime Minister's Office carries two to eight years' imprisonment; counterfeiting the seal of an ordinary public institution or a public-status professional body (such as a notary, university, bar, or chamber) carries one to six years.

**Can these charges be brought only if the affected institution files a complaint?**
No. Because both offenses are classified as crimes against public order, prosecutors pursue them ex officio; a private complaint is not a precondition for investigation or prosecution, though the affected institution typically participates in the proceedings.
