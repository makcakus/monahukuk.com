Foreign nationals living, working or studying in Turkey are sometimes surprised to learn that Turkish criminal law treats unwanted sexual remarks, messages or gestures as a distinct offense from sexual assault, with its own definition, its own aggravating circumstances, and — critically — a short six-month window in which the victim must file a complaint. Article 105 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) governs this offense, generally translated as sexual harassment, and it sits at the boundary of Turkey's sexual-offenses chapter precisely because it does not require any physical contact with the victim's body. Whether the conduct involves a coworker's persistent messages, a supervisor's remarks, or unwanted contact through phone or social media, understanding how this provision is structured — and how quickly a complaint must be lodged — matters for victims, for accused individuals defending themselves, and for employers managing workplace incidents.

## What Distinguishes Harassment From Sexual Assault

Article 105 is the closing provision of the chapter of the Turkish Penal Code dealing with offenses against sexual inviolability. Unlike the other offenses in that chapter, it does not require any touching of the victim's body. The conduct it punishes is carried out through words, writing, gestures, images or messages — forms of behavior that convey a sexual intent without physical contact. This is what separates the offense from sexual assault under Article 102: the moment an act crosses into bodily contact with the victim, even a brief or fleeting one, the applicable charge shifts toward assault (or its lesser, groping-level form) rather than harassment. In practice, this boundary is frequently the central issue litigated in harassment cases, and the growth of digital communication — messaging apps, social media, email — has significantly expanded how often the provision is applied, since a large share of modern harassment complaints now arise from electronic contact rather than face-to-face encounters.

## The Interest the Law Protects

The provision protects a person's sexual autonomy and the sense of personal peace that comes with it. Every individual has the right to decide how they engage with their own sexual life, and correspondingly the right not to be subjected to unwanted sexual conduct directed at them without their consent. Article 105 punishes an intrusion on a person's psychological integrity and their freedom to feel safe in daily life, even where no bodily contact occurred. Tellingly, the law raises the minimum sentence when the victim was forced to quit a job, leave school, or leave their family as a result of the harassment — a clear signal that the interest being protected is not confined to sexual autonomy narrowly defined, but extends to the victim's ability to continue their ordinary social and professional life undisturbed.

## Who Can Commit and Be a Victim of the Offense, and What Conduct Qualifies

Anyone can commit this offense; the law places no restriction on who the perpetrator may be, and it makes no difference whether the perpetrator and victim are the same or different sex. That said, certain professional or relational positions held by the offender trigger the aggravating circumstances discussed below, so the offender's status matters for sentencing rather than for whether the offense exists in the first place. Likewise, anyone can be a victim, though harassment committed against a minor carries a heavier penalty and, in that case, a prison sentence is mandatory rather than optional. The victim must be an identifiable, specific person — conduct directed at no one in particular does not satisfy the offense — and the victim must actually become aware of the conduct, since behavior a person never learns was directed at them cannot disturb their sexual peace.

As for the conduct itself, the statute defines the act only in general terms — "harassing a person for a sexual purpose" — without listing specific qualifying behaviors. This makes it an offense that can be committed through virtually any means: sexually charged remarks, persistent messages or letters, gestures or body language with sexual connotations, sending explicit images, or exposing oneself to a specific person. That last example only qualifies as harassment if it targets an identifiable victim; indecent exposure directed at the public at large falls instead under the separate offense of public indecency (Article 225). The decisive test throughout is the absence of bodily contact — once contact occurs, the conduct is assessed instead under sexual assault or its milder, groping-level variant, applying criteria such as whether the contact was fleeting and interrupted or sustained, and whether it was actually directed at the victim's body. The offense is also complete the moment the conduct occurs; no particular consequence needs to follow, and the victim does not need to prove they were actually distressed — it is enough that the conduct was objectively capable of disturbing a person's sexual peace.

## Intent Requirement: Why Motive Matters

Sexual harassment can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent version of the offense. General intent alone is not sufficient, however. Because the statute expressly requires the conduct to be "for a sexual purpose," the offender must additionally be shown to have acted with a specific sexual motive — the conduct must be aimed, at least in part, at satisfying sexual desire or otherwise carry a sexual objective.

Where that sexual motive is absent, the same words or gestures may still constitute a different offense — insult, threat, or disturbing a person's peace and quiet — depending on the circumstances. A sexually charged insult uttered purely out of anger, with no sexual objective behind it, is treated as an insult rather than harassment. This distinction turns less on the literal content of what was said than on the offender's actual purpose, which must be established from the concrete facts of the case rather than assumed from the words alone.

## Aggravating Circumstances and the Consequences They Trigger

The second paragraph of Article 105 lists circumstances that increase the penalty by half. These include: exploiting the ease provided by a public office, an employment relationship, or a family relationship; committing the act in a caretaking capacity — as a guardian, educator, trainer, caregiver, foster family, or healthcare provider, or anyone with a duty of protection, care or supervision; exploiting the ease provided by working at the same workplace; exploiting the ease provided by postal services or electronic communication tools; and committing the act by exposing oneself. The unifying idea behind these grounds is that the offender exploited a position, relationship or means of communication that made it easier to reach the victim. In current practice, the most frequently applied grounds are the workplace-proximity ground and the postal/electronic-communication ground, the latter of which covers digital harassment carried out through text messages, email and social media; what matters for the increase is not merely that such a channel was used, but that the offender actually exploited the ease it provided.

A separate rule closes out this paragraph: where the harassment forces the victim to quit their job, leave school, or leave their family, the sentence cannot be lower than one year, regardless of how the base penalty would otherwise be calculated.

## Attempt, Complicity and Concurrence

**Attempt.** Because harassment is complete the moment the conduct occurs, an attempt is generally not possible where the conduct is directed straight at the victim. Where the execution can be broken into separate steps, however — for example, a sexually explicit letter that never reaches the victim for reasons outside the offender's control — an attempt can still be recognized.

**Complicity.** Every form of participation in the offense is possible. Where an aggravating circumstance arises from the personal status of the principal offender (such as a caretaking role), an accomplice who does not share that status is assessed under the general rules governing complicity rather than the aggravated provision.

**Concurrence.** Where the same victim is subjected to repeated harassing acts over time as part of a single criminal decision, the rules on continuing offenses apply, treating the acts as one continuing crime rather than multiple separate ones. Where the harassing conduct instead escalates directly into a sexual assault, it is absorbed into that more serious offense rather than being punished separately.

## Penalties, Prosecution and the Complaint Deadline

In its basic form, the offense carries a prison sentence of three months to two years or a judicial fine, with the court choosing between the two. Where the victim is a minor, the sentence rises to six months to three years' imprisonment and the judicial-fine option is no longer available. Where any of the aggravating circumstances in the second paragraph apply, the applicable sentence is increased by half.

The basic offense under the first paragraph is contingent on a complaint from the victim — a requirement written directly into that paragraph, meaning it also governs harassment committed against a minor, since the complaint requirement sits within the same paragraph covering that scenario. The victim must exercise the right to complain within six months of learning of the act and the offender's identity. The aggravated forms in the second paragraph, by contrast, are prosecuted by the public prosecutor on its own initiative, without needing a victim complaint. Mediation is not available for offenses against sexual inviolability even where prosecution is otherwise complaint-dependent, so sexual harassment cases are excluded from the mediation process regardless of which paragraph applies.

Given the penalty range, jurisdiction over these cases sits with the court of first instance for criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi). The statute of limitations for prosecution is eight years, since even the aggravated sentencing range stays below the five-year threshold that would trigger a longer period. Venue lies with the court for the place where the offense was committed; in cases of harassment carried out through electronic communication, the place where the conduct originated and the place where its effect was felt may differ, which can affect which court has jurisdiction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does sexual harassment under Turkish law require physical contact?**
No. The defining feature of this offense is the absence of bodily contact. Conduct that does involve physical contact with the victim is assessed instead under sexual assault (Article 102) or its milder, groping-level form.

**How long does a victim have to file a complaint?**
For the basic offense, the victim must file a complaint within six months of learning both the conduct and the identity of the offender. This deadline applies to the basic offense whether or not the victim is a minor. The aggravated forms involving abuse of position, caregiving roles, workplace proximity, electronic communication tools, or exposure are prosecuted automatically, without requiring a victim complaint.

**Can a compliment or an expression of romantic interest amount to harassment?**
Not on its own. Turkish courts have distinguished polite expressions of admiration, requests to get acquainted, or proposals made within accepted social norms from conduct that is crude, unwelcome, or accompanied by circumstances such as one party being married. What matters is whether the offender's purpose, established from the facts of the case, was genuinely sexual rather than a legitimate social overture.

**Can workplace harassment sent by text message or email be prosecuted even without the victim's complaint?**
Yes, if it falls under one of the aggravating grounds — such as exploiting the ease of a shared workplace or of electronic communication tools — because those aggravated forms are prosecuted on the public prosecutor's own initiative rather than being complaint-dependent.
