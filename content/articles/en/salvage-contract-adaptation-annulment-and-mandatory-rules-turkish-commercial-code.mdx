This article covers the two provisions that close out the "salvage contract" subsection of the TTK salvage chapter: judicial adaptation or annulment of a salvage contract, and which rules of the chapter can be varied by agreement (TTK Art. 1301-1302).

## Judicial Adaptation or Annulment of the Salvage Contract

Art. 1301 lets a court adapt or annul a salvage contract on request, but only where two conditions combine. First, the contract must have been made under misrepresentation or under the influence of danger — a ground specific to salvage law, not identical to ordinary contract-law defects of consent (mistake, fraud, duress), though related in spirit. Second, at least one of two outcomes must also be present: either the accepted terms are contrary to equity and fairness, or the salvage reward is grossly disproportionate to the services actually rendered. Where both elements are met, the court may either adapt the contract to present circumstances or annul it outright — the choice depends on what the case requires.

The rationale tracks the unusual circumstances in which salvage contracts are typically struck: often within minutes, in the middle of a maritime emergency. That same urgency that justifies broad authority for the master and shipowner to conclude a salvage contract (as discussed in the prior article on Art. 1300) can also leave the other party — a panicked owner facing the loss of a vessel or cargo — with little real bargaining power. Art. 1301 gives courts a corrective tool for exactly this risk of after-the-fact exploitation, without disturbing salvage contracts generally.

## Which Rules Can Be Varied by Contract

Art. 1302 sets a default rule, then carves out an exception. By default, the chapter's provisions may be varied — expressly or implicitly — by a salvage contract; the chapter functions as a default (non-mandatory) regime that fills gaps the parties leave open. The exception covers two things that remain mandatory regardless of contrary agreement: the adaptation/annulment rules of Art. 1301, and the duty of care to prevent and limit environmental damage (addressed further in the chapter). A clause purporting to waive either is void.

The logic is consistent: purely commercial terms (fee structure, scope of services) are left to negotiation, but the protection against exploiting a party's distress, and the public interest in preventing environmental harm, are considered too important to be contracted away by the very party the rule protects against.

## Practical Takeaway

For foreign owners or salvors negotiating a Turkish-law salvage contract, this means fee and procedural terms are freely negotiable, but a court retains oversight power over fairness that cannot be excluded by contract language — and environmental due-care obligations apply regardless of what the contract says.
