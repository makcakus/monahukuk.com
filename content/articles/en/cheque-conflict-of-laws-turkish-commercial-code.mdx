TTK Art. 819-823 close out Turkey's entire kambiyo-instrument chapter with the conflict-of-laws regime for cross-border cheques. The cheque's regime is notably leaner than the ten-article bill-of-exchange version — only five articles — because a cheque has no acceptance or maturity mechanics to generate additional conflict-of-laws questions.

## Who Can Be Drawee

Art. 819 answers a cheque-specific question absent from bill-of-exchange law: which country's law determines who can validly be named drawee. The answer is the law of the place of payment — consistent with the "bank" definition rule in Art. 815. If that law would invalidate the cheque because of the drawee's identity, signatures on the cheque remain valid wherever the signing country's law does not recognize that same ground of invalidity — a holder-protective rule reflecting the independence-of-signatures principle in conflict-of-laws terms.

## Form, With an Alternative

Each cheque obligation is governed by the law of the country where it was signed (Art. 820/1) — but with a notable cheque-specific flexibility absent from bill-of-exchange law: compliance with the payment-place law's form requirements is also sufficient, even if the signing-place law would find a defect. Turkish courts require expert reports on both the signing-country and payment-country law when a foreign-language cheque's validity is disputed, though the expert supplies only the content of foreign law — the judge retains the legal characterization and final ruling.

## Effects of Obligations: A Single Rule

Where bill-of-exchange law splits liability effects between the acceptor (payment-place law) and other obligors (signing-place law), a cheque has no acceptor category at all — so Art. 821 applies one unified rule: every cheque obligation's effects follow the law of wherever it was signed.

## Payment-Place Law's Broad Reach

Art. 822 hands the payment-place law a strikingly broad list of nine questions unique to cheques: whether a cheque must be payable strictly at sight, the presentment period, whether acceptance-like notations have any effect, whether partial payment must be accepted, crossing and account-payee mechanics, the holder's rights over the cover, the drawer's revocation and stop-payment rights, measures for a lost or stolen cheque, and whether protest is required for recourse. In one recent case, a UK-payable cheque lost by the drawee bank was assessed under English law (the 1882 Bills of Exchange Act) to determine the holder's right to a replacement cheque.

## Unjust Enrichment

Claims against the drawee or a domiciled third-party payer for unjust enrichment follow the law of that party's domicile (Art. 823) — narrower than the bill-of-exchange equivalent, since the "cover" concept transferred to the holder (Art. 733) was never imported into cheque law via Art. 818.

## Practical Takeaway

Cross-border cheque disputes in Turkish courts typically turn on the drawee's identity and the cheque's formal validity; the deeper conflict-of-laws questions on payment mechanics and enrichment claims arise less often but can be decisive when a foreign bank or foreign payment location is involved.
