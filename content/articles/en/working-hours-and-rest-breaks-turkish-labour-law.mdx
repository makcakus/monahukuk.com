How many hours an employee owes their employer, and how those hours are distributed across the week, is one of the most basic building blocks of any employment relationship in Turkey. Labour Code No. 4857 caps ordinary weekly working time at 45 hours, but it also gives employers and employees considerable freedom to decide how that time is spread across the working week — a flexibility that Turkish practice calls "denkleştirme," or compensatory time arrangement. Because rest-break entitlements and overtime pay calculations are both derived directly from working time, getting the underlying arithmetic right is essential for employers operating in Turkey, particularly in sectors such as tourism, hospitality and manufacturing where shift patterns are rarely a flat eight hours a day.

## The 45-Hour Weekly Ceiling and the Equal-Division Default

Article 63 of the Labour Code measures working time on a weekly, not daily, basis: as a general rule, an employee may not be required to work more than 45 hours in a week. Unless the parties have agreed otherwise, that ceiling is applied by dividing it evenly across the days actually worked at the workplace — so in a business that runs six days a week, the default daily schedule works out to 7.5 hours, without anyone having to specify it. Underground mining is treated as a special case because of the physical demands and health risks involved: the law sets a stricter limit of 7.5 hours a day and 37.5 hours a week for employees working underground, a deliberate departure from the general regime.

## Compensatory Time Arrangements: Flexing the Weekly Limit

The second paragraph of Article 63 allows employers and employees to agree on a different distribution of weekly working time across the days actually worked, provided that no single day exceeds 11 hours. This is the compensatory arrangement, sometimes described as a "condensed work week." Its logic is straightforward: an employee may work more than 45 hours in some weeks and fewer in others, as long as the average across a defined reference period does not exceed the normal weekly limit. That reference period is ordinarily two months, but it can be extended to four months by collective bargaining agreement, and up to six months in the tourism sector (also by collective agreement). The practical consequence is significant: hours worked above 45 in a busy week are not treated as overtime as long as the average holds — the employee is compensated not with a premium wage but with lighter weeks or time off later on. The 11-hour daily ceiling, however, is mandatory and cannot be contracted around; any work beyond 11 hours in a single day falls directly under the overtime rules regardless of any compensatory agreement.

## Rest Breaks: Tiered Minimums Based on Daily Working Time

Article 68 of the Labour Code requires employers to grant a rest break roughly midway through the working day, timed to fit local custom and the nature of the job, with the length of the break scaled to how long the employee works that day: a shorter break for short shifts, and progressively longer breaks as the shift lengthens. In practice this produces three tiers — 15 minutes for shifts of four hours or less, half an hour for shifts of more than four hours up to and including 7.5 hours, and at least one hour for shifts exceeding 7.5 hours. These are minimum, uninterrupted periods, though contracts may allow them to be split, taking into account climate, season, local custom and the nature of the work; different employees at the same workplace may also take their breaks at different times. Crucially, rest breaks are not counted as working time.

The break itself responds to an obvious practical need — no employee can realistically work an entire shift without a pause for a meal, tea or simple rest. Court practice has gone a step further by reading Article 68 together with the 11-hour daily ceiling set out in Article 63: where daily working time reaches up to and including 11 hours, a one-hour break is treated as sufficient, but once daily working time exceeds 11 hours, case law requires a break of at least one and a half hours.

## What the Rest Break Actually Means in Practice

During the rest break the employee is genuinely free — they may spend the time inside or outside the workplace as they choose and cannot be compelled to work during it. If an employee remains on the premises but keeps working in substance, the break is treated as not having been granted at all. As a rule the break must be continuous, though climate, season, local custom and the nature of the work can justify a contractual arrangement allowing it to be split. Because the law expressly excludes rest breaks from working time, no separate wage is normally owed for that period — but if the employee is in fact kept working through what should have been a break, that time counts as ordinary working time, and any portion that pushes the week past 45 hours must be paid at the premium overtime rate.

## How Rest Breaks Interact With Overtime Calculations

The most common dispute in practice concerns whether rest breaks have been correctly deducted when calculating overtime pay. A recurring error in expert reports is deducting a flat half-hour rest break even where the calculated daily working time is well above 7.5 hours — when in fact at least one hour must be deducted for any shift beyond 7.5 hours, and at least one and a half hours for any shift beyond 11 hours. This tiered structure remains fully applicable even where a compensatory time arrangement is in place: compensatory arrangements only govern how the 45-hour weekly average is spread across the days worked, and do not shorten the rest break itself. A further condition applies before a compensatory arrangement can be relied on at all — there must be an actual agreement, written or implied, between the parties; where a contract merely refers to the statute in general terms without spelling out how the arrangement will operate, overtime pay must be calculated as if no compensatory arrangement existed. Getting these calculations right is equally relevant to workplace health and safety, since properly applied working-time and rest-break rules are a baseline condition for protecting employee wellbeing, including in special regimes such as night work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the 45-hour weekly limit mean I can never ask an employee to work more than 45 hours in a given week?**
No. If a valid compensatory time arrangement is in place, an employee can work more than 45 hours in some weeks, provided the average over the applicable reference period (normally two months, extendable by collective agreement) does not exceed 45 hours, and no single day exceeds 11 hours.

**Can a rest break be paid time, or does it always come out of the employee's working day for free?**
As a rule, rest breaks are excluded from working time and are unpaid as such. But if an employee is not genuinely relieved of duties and continues working through the scheduled break, that period must be treated as ordinary working time and paid accordingly, including at the overtime rate if it pushes the week over 45 hours.

**If my employment contract just says "the compensatory time arrangement under the Labour Code applies," is that enough to avoid paying overtime?**
Not necessarily. A general reference to the statute without specifying how the compensatory arrangement actually operates is treated as insufficient; in that situation overtime pay is calculated as though no compensatory arrangement existed at all.

**How long a rest break is required for a shift of around 9 or 10 hours?**
Turkish case law reads the rest-break tiers together with the 11-hour daily ceiling: for daily working time up to and including 11 hours, a one-hour break is generally regarded as adequate. Only once daily working time exceeds 11 hours does the required minimum rise to at least one and a half hours.
