TTK Art. 529-535 mark the point where a Turkish joint-stock company's active commercial life ends and winds down.

## General Grounds for Dissolution

Art. 529 lists six grounds: expiry of the company's term (unless business continued and the term became indefinite), completion or impossibility of the corporate purpose, an articles-specified event, a qualified-majority general assembly resolution, bankruptcy, or other statutory causes.

## Two Special Court-Driven Routes

Art. 530 lets shareholders, creditors, or the Ministry petition a commercial court for dissolution when a required organ (e.g., the board) has long been missing or the general assembly cannot meet — after giving the company a chance to correct the situation. Art. 531 introduces the "dissolution for just cause" action, available to shareholders holding at least 10% of capital (5% for listed companies). Importantly, this is not a withdrawal right: only the company can be sued for dissolution, and one shareholder cannot use it to force another shareholder's exit. Courts also have wide discretion here — instead of ordering dissolution, they may order the company to buy out the petitioning shareholders at fair value, avoiding the more drastic remedy where a lesser fix will do.

## What Happens on Dissolution

Once dissolved by any cause other than bankruptcy or court order, the board must register and publish the dissolution (Art. 532). The company then automatically enters liquidation (Art. 533) — it keeps its legal personality until liquidation ends, but must add "in liquidation" to its trade name, and its organs' powers narrow to liquidation purposes only. Bankruptcy is handled separately by the bankruptcy administration under insolvency law rather than the ordinary liquidation regime (Art. 534), and outside bankruptcy, the organs' surviving powers cover only what liquidators cannot themselves perform (Art. 535).

## Why This Matters

Foreign counterparties dealing with a Turkish company that has stopped normal operations should check its Trade Registry status before assuming it has ceased to exist — a company "in liquidation" retains full legal personality and can still be sued or sue, just within a narrower purpose.
