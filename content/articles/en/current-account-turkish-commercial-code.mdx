Articles 89 through 101 of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK) regulate the current account agreement — a contract structure commonly used in ongoing supplier and distributor relationships, under which two parties waive claiming individual debts as they arise and instead settle periodically for the net balance.

## Definition and the Strict Written-Form Requirement

Under Art. 89, a current account agreement lets two parties convert receivables arising from any legal relationship into debit and credit entries, to be settled at intervals for the resulting balance. Critically, the agreement is invalid unless made in writing (Art. 89(2)) — and Turkish courts apply this requirement strictly. Even a long-standing, regular trading relationship with invoices routinely logged as running entries does not prove a valid current account agreement absent a written contract. Where no written agreement exists, the relationship is treated instead as an "open account," and each individual receivable is subject to its own ordinary limitation period rather than the special rules discussed below.

## Key Effects and the Unity Principle

Entries recorded in the account do not, absent agreement otherwise, extinguish a party's underlying claims or defenses regarding that entry (Art. 90). Perhaps the most important structural feature is the "unity principle" in Art. 97: all entries in the account form an indivisible whole, and until the account is closed, neither party can be considered a creditor or debtor on any individual entry — their legal position is determined only by the final settlement. This means a party cannot pursue a single invoice in isolation before the relevant accounting period closes.

## Settlement, Interest, and the Five-Year Limitation Period

At the end of each accounting period (or each calendar year, absent agreement), the account is closed and the balance determined; a party receiving the balance statement who does not object within one month, using a prescribed formal method, is deemed to have accepted it (Art. 94). Claims relating to settlement of the account, an agreed or court-determined balance, interest, or errors are subject to a five-year limitation period running from termination of the agreement (Art. 101) — but this special period only applies where a valid written current account agreement is proven; otherwise, each underlying claim follows its own, often much shorter, limitation period.

Foreign suppliers with ongoing Turkish distributor relationships should ensure any intended current-account arrangement is documented in a signed written agreement — informal running-balance practices, however consistent, will not secure the longer five-year limitation period.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Must a current-account contract be made in writing?

Yes. Under Article 89/2 TTK a current-account contract must be made <b>in writing</b>; this is a condition of validity. A contract concluded without that form is void, and the parties cannot rely on the effects peculiar to a current account (waiver of the right to claim items individually, the principle of indivisibility, interest on the balance); the claims are then pursued separately under the general rules.

### What is the limitation period for a current account?

Under Article 101 TTK, actions concerning the liquidation of the current account, the remaining amount (balance) accepted or established by a court judgment, interest claims, errors and mistakes in the account, items that should have been kept outside the account or were wrongly entered in it, and repeated entries, are barred <b>five years after the end of the current-account contract</b>.

### In which cases does a current account come to an end?

Article 98/1 TTK lists three grounds: <b>expiry of the agreed term</b>; where no term was agreed, <b>notice of termination by one of the parties</b>; and <b>the bankruptcy of one of the parties</b>. In addition, under Article 99, where one of the parties dies or is placed under guardianship, the parties or their successors may terminate the current account on ten days' notice.
