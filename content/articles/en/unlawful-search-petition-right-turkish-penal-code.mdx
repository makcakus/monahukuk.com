A search of a person's body or belongings is one of the most intrusive contacts the Turkish state can have with an individual, and Turkish law confines it strictly to cases where a legal basis and a prescribed procedure exist. Article 120 of the Turkish Penal Code criminalizes the moment a public official's search loses that legal footing. The following provision, Article 121, addresses a related but distinct failure of state power: an official who, instead of overstepping authority, simply refuses to do what the law requires — declining to accept a petition a citizen submits to exercise a right. Both offenses sit in the part of the Penal Code dealing with crimes against personal liberty, and both can only be committed by a public official; that official capacity is a constituent element of each crime, not merely an aggravating factor.

## The Interests These Provisions Protect

The unlawful search offense protects several overlapping interests at once. Foremost is the constitutional guarantee of privacy and personal inviolability, alongside personal security and public confidence in the administration. The legislature does not criminalize the search power itself — searches conducted with proper authority remain lawful — but rather its exercise without or beyond that authority.

The petition offense protects a different, more specific interest: the constitutional right to petition, which is the administrative extension of the broader freedom to seek redress. It is the most basic channel of communication between an individual and the competent authority. An official who blocks that channel does not extinguish the underlying right itself, but does close off the path leading to it.

## The Offense of Unlawful Search (Article 120)

### What Conduct Is Covered

The offense applies only to a search of a person's body or personal belongings. A search of a residence, workplace, or their annexes falls outside this article entirely; if a public official conducts an unlawful search of those spaces, the provisions on violation of the inviolability of residence by a public official apply instead. The distinction matters in practice — if a single incident involves both a body search and a residence search, two separate offenses may be at issue.

The offense is a conduct crime rather than a result crime. No item needs to actually be seized and no particular outcome needs to follow; carrying out an unauthorized search is sufficient in itself.

### How "Unlawful" Is Determined

The unlawfulness element in this offense refers outward to procedural rules found outside the Penal Code itself. The Criminal Procedure Code's search provisions — the requirement of a judicial search order, the alternative of a written instruction from a prosecutor or a senior law-enforcement officer in urgent cases, the scope and duration limits of any such order, who must be present during a search, and the obligation to prepare a written record — supply the yardstick for this assessment. The search powers granted to police under the Police Duties and Powers Law are considered on their own separate terms as well.

Not every procedural shortfall automatically amounts to a crime, however. A meaningful line is drawn between a complete absence of search authority or a substantial exceeding of its limits, on one hand, and a merely formal defect such as an incomplete written record, on the other. The first category satisfies the material element of the offense; the second typically remains a matter of disciplinary responsibility or evidentiary weight rather than criminal liability.

### Consequences for the Use of Evidence

An unlawful search carries a consequence that is independent of criminal liability for the official involved. Reading the constitutional guarantee against self-incrimination and unlawfully obtained evidence together with the Criminal Procedure Code's evidence provisions, evidence obtained through an unlawful method cannot be used as the basis of a judgment; evidence gathered in violation of the law must be excluded. As a result, an allegation of unlawful search frequently plays out on two fronts simultaneously — it can trigger an investigation into the searching official, while also undermining the evidentiary foundation of the underlying case built on whatever was found during that search.

### Who Can Commit It, Who Is the Victim, and the Required Mental State

Only a public official can be the perpetrator; the victim is anyone whose body or belongings were searched. The offense can only be committed intentionally — the official must act knowing that the search authority is absent or that its limits have been exceeded. It cannot be committed through negligence. No special motive is required: an official's claim of acting in good faith or to protect public order does not negate intent if the official was aware the search lacked legal grounds. A genuine and substantial mistake about the scope of one's authority, however, may be examined under the general rules on mistake affecting intent.

## Obstruction of the Right to Petition (Article 121)

This provision punishes a public official who, without a legal reason, refuses to accept a petition that a person has submitted to a competent authority in order to exercise a specific right.

### Refusing to Accept Is Not the Same as Failing to Respond

The conduct this offense targets is the **refusal to accept** the petition — meaning it is never logged, registered, or is simply turned away at the counter. A petition that is accepted and formally processed, but never answered within the applicable time limit, is not covered by this article; in that scenario, administrative remedies, and, where the conditions are met, the offense of misconduct in office, may become relevant instead. This is the single most common point of confusion in practice.

A second limitation concerns the content of the petition. The text requires that the petition be submitted specifically "to exercise a particular right." Whether a submission that is merely a suggestion, a tip, or the provision of information without any accompanying request falls within this scope is debated; the prevailing view is that a concrete demand to exercise a right must be identifiable.

### The "Legal Reason" Defense

The operative phrase in this article is "without a legal reason." Turkish legislation on the exercise of the right to petition allows petitions to go unexamined if they lack a defined subject matter, are unsigned or carry no address, or fall within the jurisdiction of the courts rather than an administrative authority. Refusing to accept a petition for one of these reasons is lawful and does not constitute the offense. The same reasoning generally applies where a petition is submitted to the wrong authority rather than the one actually competent to receive it. This offense, too, requires intent — the official must act knowing that no valid legal ground exists for the refusal.

## Attempt, Participation, and Concurrence

Because the acts making up an unlawful search can be broken down into stages, an attempt is possible where the search has begun but is interrupted by an outside factor before completion. Refusing to accept a petition, by contrast, is treated as an instantaneous act, so attempt is not normally conceivable for that offense.

Both offenses can only be committed by a public official, so a person without that status cannot be a co-perpetrator; such a person could still be held liable as an instigator or an accomplice. Where an unlawful search is accompanied by unlawful deprivation of liberty, mistreatment, or an exceeding of the limits on the lawful use of force, the ordinary rules on concurrence of offenses apply and separate charges may be brought. Both Article 120 and Article 121 function as special provisions relative to the general offense of misconduct in office, so where their specific conditions are satisfied, these more specific articles apply rather than the general one.

## Penalties and Procedure

Unlawful search carries a prison sentence of three months to one year; obstruction of the right to petition carries a prison sentence of up to six months, with a statutory one-month minimum under the Penal Code's general rules. Because sentences under both articles can fall within the range for short-term imprisonment, conversion to alternative sanctions and suspension of the announcement of the verdict may both be available. A conviction against a public official can also result in disqualification from exercising certain rights.

Neither offense is subject to a complaint requirement; both are investigated and prosecuted ex officio, and because they are not listed among the offenses eligible for mediation, that avenue is not available either. Jurisdiction lies with the courts of first instance for criminal matters. Because the perpetrator is a public official, the special rules governing prior authorization to investigate may come into play depending on the connection between the act and the official's duties, and law-enforcement personnel are additionally subject to their own specific legislation on this point. Given the applicable sentence ranges, the statute of limitations for prosecuting either offense is eight years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a search of someone's home fall under Article 120?**
No. Article 120 covers only a search of a person's body or personal belongings. An unlawful search of a residence, workplace, or its annexes by a public official is instead addressed under the separate offense of violating the inviolability of residence.

**If a government office accepts my petition but never responds to it, has an offense under Article 121 been committed?**
No. Article 121 only covers the refusal to accept or register a petition in the first place. A petition that is accepted but left unanswered is a matter for administrative remedies, and potentially for the general offense of misconduct in office, not for Article 121.

**Can a public official avoid liability under Article 121 by pointing to a formal defect in the petition?**
Yes, where the defect is a genuine legal ground for non-examination — for example, the petition lacks a defined subject, is unsigned, carries no address, or falls within the courts' jurisdiction rather than the authority's own. Refusing acceptance on one of these recognized grounds does not constitute the offense.

**What happens to evidence obtained through an unlawful search under Article 120?**
Independently of any criminal liability the searching official may face, evidence obtained through an unlawful search cannot lawfully be used as the basis for a court's judgment. This can affect the evidentiary foundation of an entirely separate criminal case built on what the search produced.
