Work performed at night runs against the body's natural rhythm, and Turkish labour legislation treats this as a distinct risk category rather than an ordinary scheduling choice. Law No. 4857 (the Labour Code) does not simply cap how long an employee may work at night — it also layers on separate, largely non-negotiable prohibitions covering children, underground and underwater work, and the protection of women during pregnancy and after childbirth. For employers operating in Antalya's tourism, healthcare, construction and hospitality sectors, where shift work and night operations are common, these rules define not just a scheduling ceiling but a set of absolute boundaries on who may be rostered and when.

## What Counts as "Night" and the Eleven-Hour Window

Article 69 defines night, for labour law purposes, as the period beginning no later than 20:00 and ending no earlier than 06:00, and in any event lasting no more than eleven hours. This is a statutory bracket rather than a fixed clock: the exact start and end of the night period, including seasonal adjustments, can be fine-tuned by secondary regulation depending on the sector or region. For shift-based operations, a particular shift is only classified as "night work" if more than half of its duration falls within this night bracket — a shift that only marginally overlaps the night period, while being predominantly a day shift, is not treated as night work for these purposes.

## The 7.5-Hour Ceiling on Night Work

The most concrete protection in Article 69 is that an employee's night work may not exceed 7.5 hours. This is a separate and stricter ceiling than the general daily working-time limit of 11 hours, and it operates independently of the weekly 45-hour cap: night work beyond 7.5 hours in a single day is treated as overtime in its own right, even if the employee's weekly hours stay within the general limit. The law carves out a narrow exception for tourism, private security, healthcare, and petroleum exploration, prospecting and drilling activities under the Turkish Petroleum Law — in these sectors, night work beyond 7.5 hours is permitted, but only with the employee's written consent. For workplaces running continuous day-and-night shift rotations, the Code also requires that employees who work a night shift for one week be rotated to a day shift the following week (a two-week rotation cycle is also permitted), and an employee moving between shifts must be given an uninterrupted rest of at least eleven hours before starting the new shift. These rules work alongside the general regime on working hours and rest breaks to ensure that an employee's right to rest is protected in practice, not only on paper, even where night shifts are involved.

## Minimum Working Age for Children

Article 71 sets the general rule that children who have not yet turned fifteen may not be employed at all. The single exception is for children who have completed both their fourteenth year and compulsory primary education: they may be employed in light work that does not interfere with their physical, mental, social or moral development, or with continued schooling for those still in education. Placement of child and young workers is governed by safety, health and psychological-development criteria, and secondary regulation determines exactly which categories of work qualify as "light work" and under what conditions. This protection is not confined to night work, but read together with the night-work prohibition for minors under Article 73, it forms part of a broader, coordinated scheme protecting child labour.

## Underground, Underwater and Night-Shift Prohibitions

Two further provisions close off particularly hazardous work environments to children and, in part, to women. Article 72 imposes an absolute prohibition — with no statutory exception — on employing males who have not completed their eighteenth year, and women of any age, in underground or underwater work such as mining, cable laying, sewer construction and tunnel construction. Article 73 separately prohibits employing child and young workers under eighteen on night shifts in industrial work. For women who have turned eighteen, the Code does not impose an absolute ban on night-shift work; instead, it directs that the specific procedures and conditions for employing them on night shifts be set out in secondary regulation, prepared by the Ministry of Labour and Social Security in consultation with the Ministry of Health. Read against the Labour Code's equal-treatment principle, these restrictions are understood as protective in nature — a form of permissible differential treatment aimed at biological and social protection rather than unlawful discrimination.

## Maternity Leave, Nursing Breaks and Protection from Dismissal

Article 74 extends this protective framework to pregnancy and childbirth. As a general rule, a female employee may not be required to work for a total of twenty-four weeks — eight weeks before and sixteen weeks after childbirth — and in the case of a multiple pregnancy, the eight-week pre-birth period is extended by an additional two weeks. A physician's report can extend these periods further, paid leave is granted for periodic prenatal check-ups, and where medically appropriate the employee may be assigned lighter duties without any reduction in pay. Once she returns to work, a mother is entitled to a total of one and a half hours of paid nursing leave per day until the child turns one; she decides herself how this time is scheduled and split during the day, and it counts as part of her normal working hours rather than being deducted from it. This entire structure is reinforced by Article 18/3 of the Labour Code, which renders invalid any termination of an employee's contract during maternity leave — giving a woman protected under the night-work and hazardous-work restrictions an additional layer of job security precisely during this period. A 2026 amendment (Law No. 7578) added a further, narrower entitlement to Article 74: an employee who becomes a foster parent to one or more children, whether jointly with a spouse or individually, is entitled to ten days of unpaid leave upon request, following the date the child is placed with them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can an employer require night work beyond 7.5 hours if the employee agrees verbally?**
No. The exception for tourism, private security, healthcare and petroleum exploration/drilling activities under Article 69 requires the employee's written consent; a verbal agreement does not satisfy this requirement.

**Is a 22:00-06:00 shift automatically "night work" even if most of the hours fall after midnight?**
Classification depends on whether more than half of the shift's duration falls within the statutory night bracket (no later than 20:00 to no earlier than 06:00, capped at eleven hours). A shift that is predominantly within that bracket is treated as night work; one that is not, is not.

**Can a woman over eighteen simply be scheduled onto night shifts on the same basis as male colleagues?**
The Labour Code does not prohibit this outright, but Article 73 requires that the specific procedures and conditions be set out in secondary regulation prepared jointly by the labour and health ministries — an employer cannot assume unrestricted scheduling authority without regard to that regulation.

**Does the twenty-four-week maternity period run automatically, or does the employee have to request it?**
The eight weeks before and sixteen weeks after childbirth are the default statutory position, subject to extension by medical report; termination of the employment contract during this period is separately invalidated under Article 18/3, independent of any request by the employee.
