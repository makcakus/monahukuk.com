Two provisions round out the practical machinery surrounding the Art. 1256 liability regime: when exactly "carriage" begins and ends, and how the resulting liability is actually made good through insurance.

## Carriage Period for Passengers and Cabin Baggage

Art. 1258(1)(a) defines carriage period for the passenger and cabin baggage as the time aboard, or being boarded or disembarked — expressly *excluding* time spent in a passenger terminal, on a wharf, or at a quay or other port facility. An accident in a terminal before boarding falls outside this chapter's regime; it is governed by ordinary tort or port-operator liability instead. Carriage period is extended, though, to cover water transfer between shore and ship (e.g., by tender boat where a vessel anchors offshore), provided the fare covers it or the carrier furnished the transport.

## Cabin Baggage's Extended Period, and Other Baggage's Simple Rule

For cabin baggage specifically, the terminal-exclusion carve-out does *not* apply once the baggage has been handed to the carrier and not yet returned (Art. 1258(1)(b)) — an asymmetry that makes sense operationally: baggage already checked in stays "in carriage" even while the passenger is still standing in the terminal. For baggage other than cabin baggage, the rule is simplest of all: carriage period runs from receipt by the carrier ashore or aboard until return to the passenger (Art. 1258(1)(c)).

## Mandatory Liability Insurance

Art. 1259(1) requires every carrier — contracting and actual alike — undertaking carriage on a ship licensed for more than twelve passengers to insure against liability for passenger death or injury. Following a 2024 amendment, the minimum coverage is 250,000 SDR per passenger per incident for ships licensed for international voyages, and 100,000 SDR for ships without such a license — figures deliberately calibrated to match the Art. 1256(1) liability cap itself, ensuring carriers actually have the financial capacity to meet that exposure.

## No Insurance, No Sailing

Art. 1259(2) backs this with real teeth: a ship failing to meet these insurance requirements is not permitted to sail. This converts the protection from a post-accident civil claim into an administrative precondition for operating at all — port authorities can and do block uninsured vessels from departure. Turkish administrative courts have confirmed, however, that this regime does not extend to vessels used purely for tourism, sport, or entertainment rather than passenger transport.
