Intentional homicide sits at the top of the offences classified under the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) as crimes against life, and it carries the heaviest sentencing structure in the entire code. Articles 81 through 83 set out the base offence, a lengthy list of aggravating circumstances that convert the sentence into aggravated life imprisonment, and a separate regime for deaths caused by a failure to act. For international counsel advising clients exposed to Turkish criminal proceedings — whether as a suspect, a victim's family, or a corporate entity affected by a fatality — understanding how Turkish law distinguishes direct intent from possible intent, and how the aggravating grounds are applied in practice, is essential to assessing exposure and strategy.

## The Legal Interest Protected and Who Can Be a Perpetrator or Victim

The offence protects the right to life itself, which Turkish law treats as the precondition for every other legal right and therefore as a value the individual cannot dispose of by consent. A victim's agreement to be killed does not remove criminal liability, unlike in offences that protect interests a person may waive. Article 81 does not require the perpetrator to hold any particular status — anyone can commit the offence, making it a general rather than a specialised crime — though the relationship between perpetrator and victim can trigger the aggravated categories in Article 82. Any living person can be a victim; the unborn child falls outside this offence and is instead addressed through the separate offences relating to termination of pregnancy, while conduct directed at a body after death is dealt with under other provisions entirely.

## The Physical Act and the Causal Link to Death

Turkish law does not prescribe a specific method for intentional homicide — any active conduct capable of causing death can satisfy the offence, which makes it what Turkish doctrine calls a "free-form" crime rather than one tied to a particular manner of commission. What matters is the result: the offence is only complete once death actually occurs, and Turkish courts require both a factual causal chain between the perpetrator's conduct and the death, and a further finding that the death can fairly be attributed to the danger the perpetrator's own conduct created. In practice, this means the conduct must have been genuinely capable of producing a fatal outcome, and the death that results must be a realization of that specific risk rather than an unrelated intervening cause.

## Establishing Intent: Direct Intent, Possible Intent, and the Line With Negligence

Intentional homicide, as its name signals, requires intent as defined generally in Article 21 of the Penal Code — knowingly and willingly bringing about the elements of the offence. Turkish law recognises two forms of intent relevant here. Direct intent exists where the perpetrator foresees the death and wants it to happen. Possible intent (dolus eventualis) exists where the perpetrator foresees that death may result from the conduct and proceeds regardless, effectively accepting the outcome even without specifically wanting it.

The most contested line in Turkish criminal practice runs between homicide committed with possible intent under Article 81 and negligent homicide committed with conscious negligence under Article 85. In both scenarios the perpetrator foresees that death could result. The difference lies in the perpetrator's internal attitude toward that foreseen outcome: someone acting with possible intent accepts the risk and proceeds anyway, while someone acting with conscious negligence does not want the outcome and genuinely trusts — however unreasonably — that it will not occur. Turkish courts draw this distinction by weighing factors such as the instrument used, the part of the body or area targeted, the perpetrator's confidence in their own skill, and the concrete circumstances of the incident, because the classification determines whether the case proceeds as murder or as a substantially less severe negligence-based offence.

## Aggravating Circumstances That Raise the Sentence to Aggravated Life Imprisonment

Article 82 lists a series of circumstances that convert the base sentence for intentional homicide into aggravated life imprisonment, the most severe custodial sentence available under Turkish law. Premeditation is the first and most litigated ground: it requires the perpetrator to have formed a settled, unconditional decision to kill, to have let a period pass that would have allowed the initial impulse to cool, and to have carried out the killing according to a plan formed during that interval — a mere short interval between decision and execution, or the fact that the killing was carried out through a hired third party, does not by itself establish premeditation. Other aggravating grounds include killing carried out with cruelty or by torment, and killing by fire, flooding, destruction, sinking, bombing, or the use of nuclear, biological or chemical weapons.

A further set of grounds attaches to the relationship between perpetrator and victim or to the victim's vulnerability: killing an ascendant or descendant relative, a spouse, a divorced spouse, or a sibling; killing a child or a person unable to defend themselves physically or mentally; and killing a woman, a ground added to the code to address gender-based violence specifically. The remaining grounds cover killing carried out because of the victim's public duty, killing intended to conceal another offence, destroy its evidence, facilitate its commission, or avoid capture, killing driven by frustration at having failed to commit an offence, and killing motivated by blood feud or by so-called honor-based motives. Each of these grounds, once established, removes the case from the ordinary life-imprisonment tier and places it in the aggravated tier.

## Homicide by Omission

Article 83 addresses situations where death results not from an active act but from a failure to perform a duty the perpetrator was legally bound to carry out. Liability under this provision requires that the omission be treated as equivalent to an active act, which in turn requires that the person owed a duty to act — arising either from law or contract, or from the fact that their own prior conduct created a danger to others' lives — and failed to prevent the death despite that duty. Because an omission is considered a lesser form of wrongdoing than active killing, Article 83 provides for a reduced sentencing range: the court may impose twenty to twenty-five years where the aggravated-life tier would otherwise apply, fifteen to twenty years where ordinary life imprisonment would otherwise apply, and ten to fifteen years in other cases — though the judge retains discretion not to apply this reduction depending on the specifics of the case.

## Attempt, Complicity, and Concurrence of Offences

Where a perpetrator begins the acts of execution with intent to kill but death does not occur for reasons beyond their control, the offence remains at the attempt stage; what distinguishes an attempted homicide from a lesser injury offence is the perpetrator's intent at the time of the act, not merely the physical outcome. Multiple participants can be held liable under the general rules on complicity, whether as the principal actor, the instigator, or an accessory, with the allocation of liability turning on who made the decision to kill and the nature of each participant's contribution to carrying it out. Where a single act causes the death of more than one person, Turkish law treats the right to life as attaching individually to each victim, so a separate homicide offence arises for each person killed, and the ordinary rules on concurrence of offences apply accumulation of sentences accordingly.

## Sentencing Framework and Procedural Features

The base form of intentional homicide under Article 81 carries a sentence of life imprisonment, while any of the Article 82 aggravating grounds raises this to aggravated life imprisonment. General sentencing provisions still apply on top of this framework. Unjust provocation under Article 29 — where the perpetrator acts under the effect of anger or severe distress caused by an unjust act — can produce a significant sentence reduction. Where the requirements of self-defense under Article 25 are met, the act is rendered lawful and no punishment is imposed at all, though exceeding the limits of self-defense is assessed separately and can still result in a reduced sentence.

Given the severity of the offence, intentional homicide is prosecuted automatically by the public prosecutor rather than upon complaint by the victim's family, and it falls outside the scope of mediation available for lesser offences. Jurisdiction lies with the heavy penal courts (ağır ceza mahkemesi), and the gravity of the offence is reflected in the correspondingly long statutory periods for prosecution and for enforcement of the sentence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What separates homicide with possible intent from negligent homicide in Turkish law?**
Both require the perpetrator to have foreseen that death could result. The dividing line is attitude toward that foreseen risk: possible intent means the perpetrator accepted the risk and proceeded regardless, while conscious negligence means the perpetrator did not want the death and genuinely believed it would not occur. Courts examine the weapon or method used, the target area, and the surrounding circumstances to draw this line, since it determines whether the case is prosecuted as homicide or as a much less severely punished negligence offence.

**Does premeditation require a long time gap between the decision to kill and the act itself?**
Not necessarily a fixed length of time, but Turkish courts require evidence that the perpetrator reached a settled, unconditional decision, that enough time passed for the initial emotional impulse to subside, and that the killing was then carried out according to a plan formed in that interval. A very short gap between decision and execution, taken alone, has been found insufficient to establish premeditation, and using a third party to carry out the killing does not by itself prove it either.

**Can a victim's consent to be killed prevent a homicide conviction?**
No. The right to life is treated as a value the individual cannot waive, so the victim's consent does not remove criminal liability for the person who caused the death.

**Is intentional homicide ever resolved through mediation or withdrawn upon the family's request?**
No. Because of the severity of the offence, it is prosecuted automatically by the public prosecutor regardless of the victim's family's wishes, and it is excluded from the mediation procedures available for less serious offences.
