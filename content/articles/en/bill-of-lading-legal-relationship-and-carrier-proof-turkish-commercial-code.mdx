Having established that the bill of lading represents the cargo, the Code turns to its second core function: proof. Art. 1237 decides which document governs which relationship; Art. 1238 decides who counts as "carrier" when several parties (owner, agent, captain) could plausibly hold that role.

## The Governing Document Splits by Relationship

Under Art. 1237(1)-(2), the carrier-holder relationship is governed by the bill of lading itself, while the carrier-shipper relationship is governed by the underlying freight (voyage) contract. The logic: a holder — often a bank or a downstream buyer who acquired the bill by endorsement — typically never saw or negotiated the freight contract, so it would be unfair to bind them to terms they never had access to. Where the bill references a voyage charter, charter terms bind the holder only if a copy of the charter was actually handed over with the bill (Art. 1237(3)) — mere reference is not enough. Turkish courts apply this "production requirement" strictly: the Court of Cassation has both denied enforcement of an arbitration clause where the charter copy was never shown to the holder, and upheld one where it clearly was.

## Identifying the Carrier

Art. 1238 sets a cascading test. Primarily, whoever signs the bill "as carrier," or in whose name and account it is issued, is the carrier. If the bill fails to clearly name the carrier, the shipowner is deemed the carrier by default (Art. 1238(2)); if the bill was issued by the captain or another agent without naming the carrier, that agent is jointly deemed carrier alongside the owner (Art. 1238(3)) — unless, in either case, the true carrier's identity is disclosed and documented at the holder's request. If the identity given is wrong or given late, the carrier, owner, and agent are jointly and severally liable for resulting losses, and — critically — the one-year time-bar under Art. 1188 does not even start running until the correct identity is disclosed (Art. 1238(4)).

## Why This Matters for Foreign Parties

A cargo claimant should never assume that a signature block or a company letterhead settles who is liable. Turkish courts routinely have to determine carrier identity from indirect evidence — a stamp, a signature style, who issued the freight invoice, who received payment — precisely because Art. 1238's presumptions are triggered by ambiguity on the bill's face.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### If the bill of lading conflicts with the contract of affreightment, which prevails?

It depends against whom it is invoked. Under Article 1237/1 TTK, <b>the bill of lading governs the legal relations between the carrier and the holder of the bill</b>. By contrast, in the relationship between the carrier and the <b>shipper</b>, the contract of affreightment retains its force. A third party who acquires the bill is therefore bound by the document in their hands, not by the terms of a contract to which they were not a party.

### Who is treated as the carrier on a bill of lading?

Under Article 1238/1 TTK, <b>the person who signs the bill of lading as carrier, or in whose name and on whose account it is signed, is deemed to be the carrier</b>. In practice the bill is usually signed by the master or by an agent; that signature places the person on whose behalf it was given in the position of carrier. Where the identity of the carrier cannot be ascertained from the document, the law protects the holder by treating <b>the shipowner</b> as the carrier.

### Is the holder of the bill bound by an arbitration clause in the contract of affreightment?

As a rule no. It follows from the logic of Article 1237/1 that, in the relationship between the carrier and a holder who is not the shipper, <b>the bill of lading</b> governs; terms of the contract of affreightment may be invoked against the holder only where the bill contains a clear and intelligible reference to them. Moreover, under Article 1245/1, where a bill is issued under a voyage charter, the mandatory protection of Article 1243 applies between the carrier and a holder who is not the shipper.
