Articles 11 through 23 of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK) define who qualifies as a "merchant" (tacir) and what legal consequences flow from that status. Merchant status is far more than a label — it triggers bankruptcy exposure, bookkeeping duties, and a demanding standard of care, while also unlocking certain commercial presumptions in a merchant's favor.

## The Commercial Enterprise and the "Unity" Principle

Art. 11 defines a commercial enterprise as one pursuing income above the threshold set for a craftsman's business, operated continuously and independently. A key practical consequence is the "unity principle" in Art. 11(3): a commercial enterprise can be transferred as a single bundle — including its lease rights — without separately transferring each asset, and without the landlord's separate written consent that would otherwise be required under general lease law.

## Who Is a Merchant

A person who operates a commercial enterprise, even partially, in their own name is a merchant (Art. 12). Legal entities such as commercial companies, and certain public-interest associations and foundations that operate a commercial enterprise, are also merchants — but the State, municipalities, and other public bodies are expressly excluded, even where they run a commercial enterprise (Art. 16). This exclusion matters directly for jurisdiction: a dispute involving a municipality generally will not qualify as a commercial case unless it falls under an absolute commercial matter.

## The Prudent-Merchant Standard and the Commercial-Obligation Presumption

Art. 18(2) requires every merchant to act "as a prudent businessperson" across all commercial dealings — an objective standard judged against what a careful, foresighted merchant in the same line of business would do, not the individual merchant's personal capabilities. Turkish courts apply this standard broadly, including to reject contract-adaptation claims where a change was objectively foreseeable to a prudent merchant. Art. 19 further presumes that a merchant's obligations are commercial by default, and — critically — that a contract commercial for only one party is treated as commercial for the other as well, unless the law provides otherwise. This affects the applicable interest rate, limitation period, and evidentiary rules, though it does not by itself convert the matter into a "commercial dispute" for jurisdictional purposes.

## Invoices and the Eight-Day Objection Rule

Under Art. 21, a party receiving an invoice who does not object within eight days is deemed to have accepted its content — but Turkish courts limit this presumption strictly to the invoice's "ordinary content" (goods, quantity, price); complex or unverifiable items, such as profit-margin calculations, fall outside it.

Foreign companies dealing with a Turkish counterparty should note that failing to object to an invoice within eight days can create a binding evidentiary presumption, making prompt review of incoming invoices a genuine legal safeguard rather than mere bookkeeping housekeeping.
