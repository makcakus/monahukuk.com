Foreign residents, expatriate employees and visitors in Turkey are frequently surprised to learn how broadly Turkish criminal law protects private life against intrusion, recording and disclosure — protection that extends well beyond what many jurisdictions treat as a purely civil matter. Article 134 of the Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) makes it a criminal offense, not merely a tort, to invade another person's private sphere, to capture images or sound of that sphere without consent, or to disclose such material to others. Because the offense sits at the intersection of criminal procedure, digital evidence and constantly evolving technology — smartphones, messaging apps, cloud storage — understanding its scope, its limits and how Turkish courts have applied it is essential for anyone navigating a criminal complaint or investigation in Turkey, whether as a complainant or as a suspect.

## The Constitutional Basis and What the Offense Actually Punishes

Article 134 sits within the section of the Penal Code dedicated to offenses against private life and the confidentiality of personal life, and it gives criminal-law effect to the right to privacy guaranteed under Article 20 of the Turkish Constitution. The value protected is not a person's honor or reputation in the abstract, but their ability to keep a defined part of their life outside public exposure and under their own control. The provision distinguishes three distinct wrongs, each carrying its own penalty. First, simply violating the confidentiality of someone's private life is punishable by one to three years' imprisonment. Second, where that violation is carried out by recording images or sound, the penalty is increased by half again. Third, and separately, unlawfully disclosing images or sound relating to someone's private life is punished more severely — two to five years' imprisonment — and the same penalty applies where the disclosure takes place through the press or broadcast media. These three tiers reflect an increasing degree of harm: mere intrusion, intrusion made permanent through recording, and finally the act of spreading that material to others.

## Who Can Commit the Offense and What Counts as an "Intrusion"

Anyone can be held liable under Article 134; it is not restricted to a particular category of offender. Only a natural person can be a victim, since privacy in this sense is treated as a value attached to human existence — a company or organization cannot be the injured party. Acts directed at a deceased person's private life fall outside Article 134 altogether and are instead addressed, if at all, through separate legal concepts protecting the deceased's memory and the personal interests of surviving relatives. The Penal Code does not define what "violating" private life means in practice, leaving that task to case law and legal commentary. In general, the offense is committed when someone gains access to, or observes, a segment of another person's life that the victim did not consent to share and did not intend to expose to public knowledge — for example, information about someone's home, personal vehicle, medical condition or personal relationships. Where that same intrusion is accomplished by recording images or sound, the law treats the resulting permanent record as an aggravating factor, because the violation no longer ends with the act of observation but produces lasting material. Notably, no actual harm needs to be proven for the offense to be complete — it is treated as an offense of endangerment, meaning that the intrusion itself is punishable regardless of any subsequent damage, provided the specific conduct is shown to fall within the victim's private sphere on the facts of the case.

## Where Private Life Ends: Public Spaces, Public Figures and the Right to Report

The most contested question under Article 134 is defining the boundary of "private life" itself. As a general rule, capturing something that happens openly in a public place, visible to anyone present, is not treated as a violation of privacy. But even in a public place, a person can retain a reasonable expectation of privacy in specific circumstances — for instance, when images are obtained through sustained, systematic surveillance or long-range zoom photography rather than a single incidental shot. For public figures, Turkish courts strike a balance between a narrower right to report on matters of genuine public interest and the individual's protected private sphere: reporting on a matter that legitimately concerns the public can justify what would otherwise be an intrusion, but exposing intimate details unconnected to any public role or public duty does not benefit from that justification. A person's own valid consent also removes the unlawfulness of the act, but the scope of that consent matters — consent given for one purpose, such as a private, limited sharing, does not authorize use of the same material in a different context or through a different channel. Turkish legal commentary draws on comparative approaches, including the European Court of Human Rights' case law distinguishing an absolute public figure from a relative one and applying a "reasonable expectation of privacy" test; under this approach, a person's public recognition does not automatically mean that every image of them carries genuine public-interest value.

## Intent Requirement

Article 134 can only be committed intentionally. The perpetrator must be shown to have acted knowingly and deliberately, aware that the conduct amounts to an intrusion into the victim's private life. Turkish law does not provide for a negligent version of this offense — an accidental or careless intrusion, without the requisite awareness, does not give rise to criminal liability under this article.

## Unlawful Disclosure Under Article 134(2) and the Aggravated Form

The second paragraph of Article 134 criminalizes a distinct act from the first: sharing, spreading or otherwise making accessible to third parties images or sound relating to someone's private life, obtained unlawfully. This offense focuses on the act of disclosure itself, independent of whether the person disclosing the material is the same person who originally recorded it — someone who obtains a recording from a third party and then circulates it can equally be liable under this paragraph. The law separately captures disclosure carried out through the press or broadcast media, reflecting the greater harm caused when material reaches a wide audience, and imposes the same penalty in that scenario. The specific channel used to disclose the material — social media, messaging applications, cloud-sharing platforms, email — has no bearing on whether the paragraph applies; what matters is that images or sound have been made accessible to third parties without lawful basis. Even sharing with a limited number of recipients, such as within a closed messaging group, can amount to disclosure, provided those recipients gained knowledge of the material without the victim's consent. A related but broader provision, Article 136, separately punishes the unlawful transfer, dissemination or acquisition of personal data generally (not limited to images or sound) with two to four years' imprisonment; which provision applies in a given case depends on the nature of the data involved and where the substance of the wrongdoing lies.

## Distinguishing Article 134 From Neighboring Offenses

Article 134 must be distinguished from two related provisions that address overlapping but distinct conduct. Article 133 targets the interception or recording of non-public conversations — limited to sound — while Article 135 addresses the systematic unlawful recording of personal data more broadly. Article 134, by contrast, rests directly on the concept of "confidentiality of private life" and explicitly covers images as well as sound. Where the elements of more than one of these provisions appear to be satisfied on the same facts, Turkish courts apply the rules on the relationship between specific and general norms and on concurrence of offenses to determine which provision governs, with the aim of avoiding double punishment for what is substantively a single course of conduct. Turkish criminal procedure also allows for aggravating circumstances under Article 137: where any of these offenses is committed by a public official abusing the authority of their office, or by someone exploiting the special ease of access afforded by a particular profession or trade, the penalty is increased by half. The offense is capable of being attempted where the conduct can be divided into stages (for example, where a recording is begun but never completed), and the general rules on participation apply without any special provision needed for incitement to, or assistance with, disclosure. Where a person commits repeated violations or disclosures against the same victim over time, pursuant to a single criminal decision, the chained-offense rules under Article 43 of the Penal Code apply, resulting in a single sentence increased by between one quarter and three quarters — Article 134 is not among the offenses excluded from this mechanism. Prosecution of offenses under Article 134 is conditional on a complaint by the victim under Article 139, which also means that mediation can resolve the matter and lead to a decision that no further prosecution is warranted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does taking a photograph of someone in a public place automatically violate Article 134?**
No. A single, incidental photograph taken in a public space generally does not amount to a violation of privacy under Article 134, even if it could raise separate civil-law issues relating to personality rights. The analysis changes where the images result from sustained or systematic surveillance rather than a passing, incidental capture.

**If someone consents to being recorded, can the recording still be disclosed lawfully?**
Not automatically. Consent to being recorded for one purpose or context does not extend to disclosing that same material through a different channel or for a different purpose. Disclosure without the victim's consent to that specific use can independently trigger liability under Article 134(2), regardless of how the recording was originally obtained.

**Can a minor's consent to being recorded remove criminal liability?**
It depends on the minor's age. Turkish courts have held that children who have not completed the age of fifteen cannot validly consent in a way that removes the unlawfulness of the conduct, since privacy in that context is not treated as a right they can dispose of on their own. For minors between fifteen and eighteen who have sufficient discernment, consent may operate as a justification, subject to the facts of the case.

**Is it necessary for the victim to be identifiable for the recording offense to be complete?**
No, not for the initial recording. Turkish appellate case law has held that the initial recording offense under Article 134(1) is complete once the image or sound is captured without the victim's knowledge or consent, without any requirement that the person be recognizable. Identifiability becomes relevant for the separate disclosure offense under Article 134(2), where the person must be recognizable and the content must reach unauthorized third parties.
