Turkish criminal law treats interference with land, sea, rail and air transport as an offence against public order rather than a matter to be resolved purely through traffic regulation or civil liability. Article 223 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), together with the related offence in Article 224 covering fixed offshore platforms, criminalises obstructing or diverting a transport vehicle and — in a 2025 legislative revision — expressly carves out lawful protest activity from its scope. For businesses operating fleets, transport operators, and individuals involved in disputes that spill onto roads, waterways or airport premises, understanding where ordinary conduct ends and this offence begins is essential, because the penalties scale sharply with the type of vehicle involved and can reach well into double digits for offences against aircraft.

## The Interest Protected and the 2025 Reform

Article 223 sits within the section of the Penal Code dealing with offences against society, in the chapter addressing offences against transport vehicles and fixed platforms. The provision protects the safe and uninterrupted operation of land, sea, rail and air transport, and by extension the continuity of public transport and economic activity that depends on it. A law enacted in December 2025 rewrote both the heading and the substance of Article 223. Beyond restructuring the offence itself, the reform added a new paragraph that expressly removes acts committed during lawfully organised assemblies and demonstrations from the scope of the crime for most vehicle categories. The result is a provision that now does two things at once: it safeguards transport security, and it draws an explicit line between legitimate protest and criminal obstruction.

## The Two Distinct Acts: Obstruction and Diversion

The offence is built around two separate types of conduct, and the law treats them with different degrees of seriousness. The first is unlawfully preventing a transport vehicle from moving, or stopping it while it is already in motion. The second, more serious act is unlawfully diverting the vehicle away from its intended destination to another location. Anyone can commit this offence — no special status or relationship to the vehicle is required of the offender. The victim is typically the vehicle's owner, operator or passengers, but the offence also indirectly harms the public's general reliance on safe transport. The phrase "unlawful conduct" used in the statute is broad: it covers force and threats but is not limited to them, extending to any conduct that unlawfully obstructs a vehicle's movement. As to obstruction or stopping, the offence is a danger crime that is complete the moment the vehicle's movement is unlawfully interrupted — no actual harm needs to result, and it is enough that the vehicle was in fact prevented from moving or was stopped.

## Penalties Scaled to the Type of Vehicle

Article 223 sets out three tiers of punishment depending on the vehicle involved. For land transport vehicles, obstructing or stopping the vehicle carries a prison sentence of one to three years, while diverting it to another destination carries two to five years. Where the vehicle is a sea or rail transport vehicle, the sentence is three to seven years — notably, the law does not distinguish between mere obstruction and diversion for this category, applying the same range to both acts. Offences against aircraft are punished far more severely: obstructing an aircraft's movement carries five to ten years, and diverting it to another destination carries seven to twelve years. This graduated structure reflects a deliberate policy choice: the potential scale of harm and the risk to passenger and public safety rise sharply from land to sea/rail to air transport, and the legislature has calibrated the penalty range accordingly, with aviation security treated as a category of its own.

## Fault Requirement

This is an intentional offence only. The offender must know and want that their unlawful conduct is obstructing the vehicle's movement, or that they are knowingly diverting it elsewhere; while direct intent is the ordinary basis for liability, cases where the offender foresaw and accepted the obstructive consequences of their conduct may also support a finding of intent in its eventual (dolus eventualis) form. Obstruction caused by negligence falls outside this provision entirely — such conduct, where it meets the relevant conditions, may instead be addressed under separate negligence-based offences or administrative sanctions, not under Article 223.

## The Assembly and Demonstration Exception

The fifth paragraph of Article 223 provides that no offence arises under the first two paragraphs — covering land, sea and rail transport — where the conduct described occurs during an assembly or demonstration march that has been organised in accordance with the form, conditions and procedures set out by law. This functions as a ground of lawfulness, removing from criminal liability the inevitable disruption to land, sea or rail transport that can accompany a properly conducted exercise of the right to assemble. The exception is deliberately narrow in scope: it applies only to conduct falling within the first and second paragraphs, and does not extend to obstruction of aircraft under the third paragraph, meaning aviation security remains protected without exception even against conduct connected to an otherwise lawful demonstration. The exception is also conditional — it is available only where the assembly or march itself complies with the formal requirements the law imposes; demonstrations that are unauthorised or that fail to meet those formal conditions cannot rely on this ground of justification.

## Concurrence With Other Offences

The fourth paragraph provides that where another offence is committed for the purpose of, or in the course of, committing the acts described in Article 223, the offender is punished separately for that other offence as well. This is a straightforward application of the general rule on concurrence of offences (rather than treating the conduct as a single composite crime): if obstructing a vehicle is accompanied by assault, threats, insult or damage to property, those acts are punished in addition to the Article 223 sentence, not absorbed into it. In practice, this means violent or otherwise unlawful conduct that accompanies an obstruction is assessed independently, and the offender answers separately for each offence committed rather than facing a single combined penalty.

## Attempt and Participation

Where the acts of execution have begun but the obstruction of the vehicle's movement is interrupted before completion, the general provisions on attempt may apply. The Penal Code's general rules on participation also apply without modification, so liability as an inciter or an aider is possible alongside principal liability. Where several people act together to obstruct a vehicle's movement, joint principal liability may follow, and where the obstruction is organised through a chain of command, the liability of whoever instigated it is assessed separately.

## Seizure of Fixed Platforms on the Continental Shelf (Article 224)

Article 224 creates a distinct, maritime-specific offence: seizing, capturing or bringing under control a fixed platform established on the continental shelf or in the exclusive economic zone, whether by force or threat or by other unlawful conduct, carries a sentence of five to fifteen years' imprisonment. Where the offender restricts the personal liberty of individuals in the course of this offence, that conduct is punished separately, and the same applies where the aggravated consequences of intentional injury occur — the relevant injury provisions are applied in addition. Here too the legislature has treated the platform seizure as the base offence while separately sanctioning, through the concurrence rule, any accompanying restriction of liberty or aggravated injury that results. The concepts of the continental shelf and the exclusive economic zone are core institutions of international maritime law, and the provision reflects the strategic importance that fixed installations in these zones carry for sovereignty and security.

## Prosecution and Procedure

Offences under both Article 223 and Article 224 are prosecuted ex officio by the public prosecutor's office; they are not subject to a private complaint requirement, and mediation is not available given the seriousness of the danger to public security involved. Jurisdiction should be assessed with reference to the applicable sentencing tier — for the more serious categories, where the statutory maximum exceeds ten years (such as under the aircraft-diversion clause or the platform-seizure offence), that severity is a relevant factor in determining the competent court, while the general rules on jurisdiction apply to the less serious variants of the offence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a driver who simply blocks another car during a road dispute commit this offence?**
It depends on intent and effect. If the conduct amounts to unlawfully preventing the vehicle's movement or stopping it while in motion, and is accompanied by the required intent, it can fall within Article 223(1). Turkish appellate practice has treated deliberately blocking a vehicle's exit — for example from a car park, against the driver's clear objection — as capable of satisfying the offence, so this is not merely a civil or administrative matter.

**Is it a defence that the vehicle suffered no actual damage or delay?**
No. Article 223 is structured as a danger offence with respect to obstruction and stopping — it is complete once the vehicle's movement has actually been prevented or halted, regardless of whether any further harm materialises.

**Can a protest march ever lawfully block traffic without triggering this offence?**
Yes, but only within limits. The fifth paragraph removes liability for obstruction of land, sea or rail transport occurring during an assembly or demonstration organised in line with the legally required form and procedure. This exception does not apply to aircraft, and it is unavailable if the assembly itself does not meet the law's formal requirements.

**If someone assaults another person while obstructing their vehicle, are they punished for both offences?**
Yes. The fourth paragraph applies the general rule on concurrence of offences: any other offence committed for the purpose of, or during, the obstruction or diversion is punished separately and in addition to the Article 223 sentence.
