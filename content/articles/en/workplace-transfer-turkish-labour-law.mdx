Mergers, acquisitions, asset sales and business reorganisations are a routine part of commercial life, but in Turkey they carry a specific labour-law consequence that foreign investors often underestimate: when a workplace or part of it changes hands, the employees working there do not simply become dismissible or re-hirable at the buyer's discretion. Article 6 of Labour Law No. 4857 treats a change of employer as a continuation of existing employment relationships, not a break in them. For anyone structuring a share deal, asset purchase, franchise handover or outsourcing change involving staff based in Turkey, understanding how this rule allocates rights, seniority and liability between the seller and the buyer is essential before signing anything.

## When Does a Transaction Qualify as a "Workplace Transfer"?

Article 6 applies whenever a workplace, or a part of it, passes to another person "on the basis of a legal transaction" — a broad category that covers sales, leases, donations, mergers and demergers, among other voluntary arrangements. It is not limited to a single, clearly labelled "transfer agreement." Turkish Court of Cassation case law (9th Civil Chamber) looks at the substance of the transaction rather than its form: whether tangible and intangible assets actually changed hands, whether the customer base moved along with the business, whether the bulk of the workforce continued under the new employer, how similar the activities carried out before and after the change are, and — if operations were paused — for how long. This functional test means that even a change between contractors on the same project (for example, when a public tender moves from one subcontractor to another) can trigger workplace-transfer rules, provided employees continue working without a break, or after a gap short enough to be considered reasonable.

## Automatic Transfer of Employment Contracts and Continuity of Seniority

The core legal effect of a qualifying transfer is that every employment contract in force at the workplace on the transfer date passes to the new employer automatically, together with all related rights and obligations. No employee consent is required for this transfer, and no new contract needs to be signed — the employment relationship simply continues under a different employer. Critically, the law also requires the new employer to treat the employee's length of service as running from the date the employee originally started with the seller, for every entitlement that depends on seniority: pay increments tied to tenure, annual leave entitlement, notice periods and, most significantly, severance pay. In practice this means that when calculating severance pay after a workplace transfer, Turkish courts add together the periods of service before and after the transfer as a single, continuous period, and the one-year minimum service requirement for severance eligibility is likewise assessed on that combined total. A change of employer resulting from a workplace transfer therefore does not reset the clock on an employee's accumulated seniority — it is treated as a legally seamless continuation.

## Joint Liability for Debts Arising Before the Transfer

Article 6 also addresses what happens to employee claims that arose before the change of ownership but had not yet been paid — unpaid wages, overtime, accrued annual leave pay and similar debts. Both the seller and the buyer are jointly liable for these pre-transfer debts that fall due on or after the transfer date. To prevent the seller from being exposed indefinitely, the law caps the seller's liability at two years from the transfer date; once that period elapses, only the buyer can be pursued for those debts. This joint-liability rule does not apply, however, where the seller's legal entity ceases to exist through a merger, an absorption or a change of corporate form — in those cases, since the original entity no longer exists as a legal matter, liability is not split but passes entirely to the successor structure.

## Can the Transfer Itself Be Used to Justify a Dismissal?

A frequently misunderstood aspect of Article 6 is its effect on termination rights. The law is explicit that neither the seller nor the buyer may terminate an employment contract solely because the workplace transfer took place, and the transfer itself does not give the employee a valid reason to resign for cause either. Turkish Court of Cassation precedent consistently frames a workplace transfer as an exercise of the employer's management prerogative that does not, by itself, amount to a change in working conditions — so an employee cannot claim termination-related indemnities (such as notice pay) purely because ownership changed. That said, the law is careful not to freeze all termination rights: employers retain the right to terminate for economic or technological reasons, or because of a change in the organisation of work, and both employees and employers retain their rights to terminate immediately for just cause. The transfer only removes itself as an independent, standalone ground for dismissal — it does not shield a business from making legitimate structural changes, or protect against a genuine termination for cause. In disputes, this distinction is often decisive: courts look closely at whether the real reason for a dismissal following a transfer was the transfer itself, or an underlying business need that happened to coincide with it.

## The Bankruptcy Exception

The final paragraph of Article 6 carves out an important exception: where a workplace or part of it is transferred to another party as a result of the liquidation of assets in bankruptcy, the rules described above do not apply. The rationale is that a bankruptcy liquidation is a compulsory legal process rather than a voluntary commercial decision — if a buyer acquiring assets from a bankruptcy estate could be saddled with the bankrupt seller's pre-existing debts, this would discourage buyers and make it harder, not easier, to preserve jobs. As a result, a buyer acquiring a workplace out of a bankruptcy liquidation may take on the employment contracts without also inheriting joint liability with the seller for debts that arose before the transfer. This exception reflects a deliberate balancing act between protecting employees and recognising the practical realities of insolvency.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an employee need to agree to being transferred to the new employer?**
No. Where the transaction qualifies as a workplace transfer under Article 6, the employment contract passes to the new employer automatically and by operation of law, without requiring the employee's separate consent or a new contract.

**Does severance pay reset to zero when a company changes hands?**
No. Under Article 6, service time before and after the transfer is combined into a single continuous period for severance calculation purposes, and the same combined total is used to check whether the one-year minimum service requirement is met.

**Can we dismiss employees simply because we are acquiring the business?**
No. A workplace transfer cannot, on its own, serve as the ground for termination by either the seller or the buyer. Dismissals remain possible for economic, technological or work-organisation reasons, or for just cause, but the transfer itself is not a valid reason.

**Is the seller still liable for unpaid wages after selling the business?**
Yes, for a limited time. The seller and buyer are jointly liable for employee debts that arose before the transfer, but the seller's liability is capped at two years from the transfer date, after which only the buyer remains liable — unless the transfer occurred through a bankruptcy liquidation, in which case the joint-liability rule does not apply at all.
