Annual paid leave is one of the most fundamental employee protections in Turkish labour law, recognised as a constitutional right under Article 50 of the Turkish Constitution and set out in detail in Articles 53 through 60 of the Labour Code (Law No. 4857). It exists to let an employee recover physically and mentally from a year of work, and the legislator has made it a right that cannot simply be waived, with a use procedure that is tightly regulated to make sure leave is actually taken rather than quietly forfeited. For foreign employers operating in Turkey — particularly in the seasonal hospitality and tourism sector so common in the Antalya region — misjudging these rules is a frequent and costly source of disputes, since unused leave converts into a payable claim the moment employment ends.

## Who Is Entitled to Annual Leave, and Why It Cannot Be Waived

Under Article 53, the only condition for earning annual paid leave is having actually worked for the same employer for at least one year from the start date, and this qualifying period includes any probationary period. Leave cannot be claimed before that one-year mark is reached. Critically, the law treats this right as non-waivable: an employee cannot give it up by agreement or by unilateral declaration while the employment relationship continues, and any clause purporting to do so is void. The rationale is straightforward — because employees are economically dependent on their employer, they could otherwise be pressured into trading away a right meant to protect their health. The law carves out one exception: employees in seasonal or campaign-based jobs that by their nature last less than a year fall outside these leave provisions altogether, since their employment never reaches the one-year qualifying threshold. This exception is particularly relevant in seasonal hotel operations common around Antalya, where whether successive stints for the same employer across different seasons can be combined toward the one-year threshold is a frequently disputed question.

## Leave Duration by Length of Service

Article 53 sets a graduated scale tied to the employee's length of service: a minimum of fourteen days for service between one and five years (five years inclusive), twenty days for service beyond five years and up to fifteen years, and twenty-six days for fifteen years of service or more. Employees working underground receive an additional four days on top of these minimums, reflecting the more strenuous and hazardous nature of that work. The law also sets a separate, age-based floor: regardless of seniority, employees aged eighteen or younger and employees aged fifty or older must receive at least twenty days of leave. This reflects the view that the recovery needs of younger workers still in their developmental years, and of older workers, warrant protection independent of how long they have been employed. These are statutory minimums only — individual or collective employment agreements may extend them in the employee's favour, but may never reduce them.

## Calculating the Qualifying Service Period

Article 54 governs how the one-year qualifying period is calculated. Periods an employee has worked for the same employer, whether at one workplace or several, are added together as long as the employment relationship has been continuous — this even extends to time spent at workplaces of the same employer that fall outside the scope of the Labour Code. Where employment is interrupted for a reason other than those listed in Article 55 (which covers, among other things, time spent on annual leave itself, rest periods due to illness, and maternity leave), the resulting gap is added back onto the service period, effectively pushing back the date on which the next year of qualifying service is completed. As a rule, an employee takes the leave earned for a given service year during the following service year — a mechanism designed to prevent indefinite postponement of the right and to give concrete shape to the employer's obligation to actually grant the leave.

## Taking the Leave: The Non-Splitting Rule and Its Exception

Article 56 embodies a "non-splitting" principle meant to guarantee that leave is genuinely taken as rest rather than eroded into fragments: as a rule, the employer must grant annual leave in one uninterrupted block. By mutual agreement between employer and employee, however, the leave may be divided into portions, provided no portion is shorter than ten days — this flexibility exists only where both sides agree; the employer cannot split the leave unilaterally. Other paid or unpaid leave, and rest or sick leave granted during the year, may not be offset against annual leave, and any weekly rest day, national holiday or general holiday falling within the leave period is not counted as part of it. This last point connects directly to the rules on weekly rest and public holiday pay, underscoring that these are separate and non-substitutable entitlements.

## Payment of Unused Leave on Termination

Article 59 converts the leave right into a monetary claim once employment ends. Whatever the reason for termination — justified or unjustified, initiated by the employer or the employee — any annual leave the employee had earned but not used must be paid out, calculated on the wage rate in effect on the date employment ended. This is a separate labour claim, distinct from severance pay, and the statute of limitations on it runs from the termination date rather than from any date on which the leave could have been taken. In practice, the burden of proving that leave was actually granted rests on the employer, who must demonstrate this with a signed leave register or an equivalent written record; witness testimony alone will not satisfy this burden.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When does an employee first become entitled to annual paid leave?**
After completing one full year of actual service with the same employer, counted from the start date and including any probationary period. Leave cannot be requested before that year is completed.

**Can an employee agree to forgo their annual leave in exchange for extra pay while still employed?**
No. Article 53 makes the right to annual paid leave non-waivable during the employment relationship, and any agreement to give it up is void. It can only be converted into a cash payment once the employment relationship has ended and any accrued, unused leave is settled under Article 59.

**Can the employer unilaterally split an employee's annual leave into several short periods?**
No. The default rule under Article 56 is that leave must be given as one continuous block. It may only be split into portions — none shorter than ten days — if both employer and employee agree to it.

**If an employee resigns without notice, do they still get paid for unused leave?**
Yes. Under Article 59, payment for unused annual leave is owed regardless of how or by whom the employment relationship was terminated, calculated at the wage rate applicable on the termination date.
