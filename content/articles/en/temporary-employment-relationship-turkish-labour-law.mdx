Turkish labour law is built on the assumption that an employee works for, and only for, the employer who hired them. Businesses, however, regularly face seasonal peaks, unforeseen capacity spikes or short-term staffing gaps that a single fixed employer relationship cannot absorb efficiently. Article 7 of Labour Code No. 4857 addresses this need through the institution of "temporary employment" (commonly called agency work or loaned labour), allowing an employee to keep their original employer while actually working, for a limited period and under strict conditions, for another business. The provision, reshaped by Law No. 6715, sets out both the narrow circumstances in which such an arrangement may be created and firm limits on how long it may last, precisely to prevent temporary staffing from becoming a substitute for ordinary permanent employment.

## Two Routes Into a Temporary Employment Relationship

Article 7 recognises exactly two ways to establish a temporary employment relationship. The first runs through a private employment agency licensed by the Turkish Employment Agency (İŞKUR): the agency employs the worker under its own employment contract and, through a separate temporary labour supply agreement with a client business, assigns that worker to actually perform work for the client. This creates a three-party structure — the agency, the temporary worker, and the client business that directs the day-to-day work. The second route bypasses any agency entirely: an employer may temporarily second its own employee to another workplace within the same holding company or corporate group, but only with the employee's written consent given at the time of transfer. Turkish courts have made clear that this second route requires, as a precondition, a valid underlying employment contract between the worker and the original employer, and that a secondment carried out without the employee's genuine written consent does not qualify as a lawful temporary employment relationship under Article 7 at all.

## Agency-Based Temporary Employment: Permitted Grounds and Duration Limits

The law does not allow agency-based temporary staffing for any purpose an employer chooses — it is restricted to an enumerated list of situations: military service or other cases where the employment contract is suspended, seasonal agricultural work, domestic services, work that is not part of the business's ordinary daily operations and is performed intermittently, urgent occupational health and safety needs or compelling reasons that significantly affect production, an unforeseeable increase in the business's average production capacity that justifies temporary staffing, and periodic increases in work that are not seasonal in nature. Each category carries its own time regime: for suspension-related grounds, the agreement may run for as long as the underlying situation continues; for seasonal agricultural work and domestic services, there is no maximum duration at all; for the remaining grounds, the agreement is capped at four months and may be renewed no more than twice, up to a combined total of eight months (the periodic-increase category is excluded from this renewal allowance and cannot be extended beyond its initial four-month term). Once this period ends, the client business may not engage temporary staff for the same work again until six months have passed — a cooling-off period designed specifically to stop temporary staffing from displacing permanent hiring for the same role.

## Intra-Group Secondment and Who Is Responsible for Wages

The final paragraph of Article 7 governs the holding-company/corporate-group route, and its time limits differ from the agency model: this type of secondment must be agreed in writing and may last no more than six months, renewable up to two times. Under this model, the original employer that seconds the employee remains responsible for paying wages throughout the secondment. The host business where the employee actually works is held jointly liable with the seconding employer for unpaid wages accrued during the secondment period, for the duty of care owed to the employee, and for social security contributions. Turkish appellate case law consistently treats the seconded employee as remaining, in law, the employee of the original (seconding) employer, merely performing work temporarily for the host business — a distinction that matters directly when determining which employer is the correct party in a workplace accident notification, a disciplinary matter, or a reinstatement lawsuit.

## Operating Without an Agency Licence: The Risk of Unlawful Labour Brokering

One of the most common disputes in practice arises when a company that has never obtained an İŞKUR licence nevertheless supplies workers to third parties as if it were a licensed private employment agency. Turkish appellate courts have repeatedly held that labour supplied through such an unlicensed intermediary cannot be classified as a lawful temporary employment relationship under Article 7, nor can it be treated as a lawful principal employer–subcontractor relationship. Law No. 4904 on the Turkish Employment Agency exposes unlicensed temporary staffing to administrative fines and to revocation of any private employment agency licence the intermediary might otherwise hold. Businesses that source temporary labour should therefore verify that the supplying entity holds a valid İŞKUR authorisation for temporary employment activity, both to avoid administrative sanctions and to avoid unexpected liability for unpaid wages and other employment claims. This is also the point at which temporary employment is conceptually distinct from a [transfer of workplace](/articles/workplace-transfer-turkish-labour-law): in a transfer, the employer status changes permanently, whereas in a temporary employment relationship the original employer status is preserved and the arrangement is inherently time-limited.

## Equal Treatment and Automatic Conversion to an Indefinite-Term Contract

The law expressly requires that a temporary worker's working conditions may not fall below those that would apply if the same employer directly hired the worker for the same job — a specific application of the broader [principle of equal treatment](/articles/equal-treatment-principle-turkish-labour-law) to the temporary employment context. In addition, if the temporary employment relationship continues in practice after the agreed period has expired, the law treats an indefinite-term employment contract as having been formed between the client business and the worker, effective from the date the fixed term ended; the supplying agency's liability then remains limited to the original contractual period. This mechanism is an important safeguard against using successive or extended temporary arrangements as a permanent substitute for regular employment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a business simply extend a temporary staffing arrangement indefinitely by renewing it?**
No. Depending on the ground relied upon, renewals are capped (generally at two renewals up to a combined eight months for most agency-based grounds, or two renewals up to six months for intra-group secondment), and once the maximum is reached the client business must wait six months before hiring temporary staff again for the same work.

**Who is liable if a temporary worker is not paid?**
In agency-based temporary employment, the agency is the employer of record and bears primary responsibility for wages. In intra-group secondment, the seconding employer remains responsible for wages, but the host business is jointly liable for unpaid wages, the duty of care, and social security premiums accrued during the secondment.

**Is written consent from the employee required for a secondment within a corporate group?**
Yes. Turkish case law treats written consent obtained at the time of transfer as a mandatory precondition; a secondment imposed without the employee's genuine consent is not a valid temporary employment relationship under Article 7.

**What happens if a company supplies workers to others without an İŞKUR licence?**
The arrangement is not recognised as a lawful temporary employment relationship or a lawful subcontracting relationship, and the unlicensed intermediary faces administrative fines and potential revocation of any private employment agency licence, in addition to exposure for unpaid employment claims.
