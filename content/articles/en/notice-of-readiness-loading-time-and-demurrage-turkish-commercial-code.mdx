TTK Art. 1152-1157 regulate the economics of time during loading — every extra day a ship spends in port is a day it cannot earn freight elsewhere.

## Notice of Readiness Triggers the Clock

Art. 1152 requires the carrier to give the shipper a notice of readiness once the ship reaches its berthing point, in no particular form but only effective once it actually reaches the recipient. Art. 1153 starts laytime running the calendar day after that notice is received (or when loading actually begins, if earlier); absent a contractual figure, laytime defaults to what 24-hour continuous work would require, assessed against the port, ship, facilities, cargo type, and local custom.

## Demurrage: The Default Is Ten Days

Art. 1154 lets parties agree the carrier will wait beyond laytime — this extra period is "demurrage time," defaulting to ten days if the contract mentions demurrage without fixing a duration, and starting automatically (no notice needed) once laytime ends. Art. 1155 sets demurrage money as an unlimited-liability-style payment, not damages: a contractual cap binds if set, otherwise necessary/useful expenses are recoverable; the shipper is the debtor, the carrier need not sail without payment or security, and demurrage rules can never be applied to freight claims by analogy.

## Whose Risk Is a Delay?

Art. 1156 allocates fortuitous-event delays by whose "sphere" caused them: delays from the shipper's sphere still count against laytime (shipper's risk); delays from the carrier's sphere are excluded from laytime (carrier's risk); genuinely shared events (storms, ice, mobilization) extend laytime but the shipper still owes demurrage for those days. Demurrage time itself, once triggered, runs uninterrupted regardless of any of these events — unless the carrier's own fault caused them. Turkish courts have enforced this strictly: a contractually agreed uninterrupted-demurrage clause defeats even a bad-weather defense.

## Dispatch Money for Early Completion

Art. 1157 lets parties agree the carrier pays the shipper for unused laytime if loading finishes early — the mirror image of demurrage — though such an agreement is void if it is really a device to dodge mandatory freight-rate regulations at the port.

## Practical Takeaway

For carriers, Turkish courts have confirmed demurrage is a contractual payment independent of fault or proven loss — but it only attaches if the contract actually provides for it, and the consignee is only liable for demurrage if the bill of lading expressly says so.
