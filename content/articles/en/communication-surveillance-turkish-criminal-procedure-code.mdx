Foreign nationals facing a Turkish criminal investigation are sometimes surprised to learn that a case against them rests substantially on a wiretap transcript, a phone-location record, or the testimony of an undercover officer who spent months embedded in a group before an arrest was ever made. Turkish criminal procedure permits all three measures, but only within a framework designed to keep them exceptional: they may be used only for a defined list of serious offences, only after a judge (or, in urgent cases, a prosecutor subject to prompt judicial review) has authorized them, and only for a limited time. Because these measures operate without the suspect's knowledge, and because the resulting material so often forms the backbone of the prosecution's case, understanding how the rules work — and how they can fail — is essential for anyone defending against charges built on covert evidence.

## Three Distinct Measures, One Underlying Logic

Turkish law does not treat "surveillance" as a single tool. The Criminal Procedure Code (CMK) separates it into three regimes governed by different articles: the interception of telecommunications and related data (Article 135), the deployment of an undercover investigator (Article 139), and physical surveillance of a suspect using technical devices such as hidden cameras or listening equipment (Article 140). Each regime has its own list of qualifying offences, its own duration limits, and its own authorization procedure, and none of them substitutes for another — a decision permitting one measure cannot be stretched to justify a different one. What unites all three is a shared starting premise: because the suspect is deliberately kept unaware that they are being monitored, and because the interference reaches into communication privacy and personal liberty protected under the Turkish Constitution, each measure is treated as a last-resort tool rather than a routine investigative step.

## Interception of Communications (Article 135)

Article 135 actually covers four related but distinct techniques: real-time interception of the content of a communication, recording it, analysis of non-content "signal information" such as call logs and cell-tower data, and — under a separate paragraph of the same article — retrospective identification of who communicated with whom and when. Whichever variant is used, two conditions must be satisfied before it can be authorized: there must be strong suspicion grounded in concrete evidence that a specific offence has been committed, and it must be genuinely impossible to obtain the same evidence by any other means. That second condition operates as a subsidiarity requirement — if the evidence could realistically be gathered another way, interception is not permitted at all. Authorization is, as a rule, a judicial decision. Only where any delay would cause serious harm can a public prosecutor issue the order instead, and even then, the prosecutor must submit it to a judge immediately; the judge must rule within twenty-four hours, and if that deadline passes without approval, the measure must be lifted at once.

Crucially, interception, recording and signal-data analysis are restricted to a closed list of offences set out in the same article — matters such as homicide, kidnapping and human trafficking, drug production and trafficking, bribery, money laundering, offences against state security, and a number of similarly serious crimes defined by cross-reference to the Turkish Penal Code and related statutes. If the offence under investigation is not on that list, this form of surveillance cannot lawfully be used at all, and material obtained despite that restriction cannot be used to prove that offence. A frequent and heavily litigated scenario is the "incidental find": material intercepted during a lawful investigation into a listed offence turns out to reveal an entirely different crime that has nothing to do with the original investigation. Turkish law allows the incidental material to be preserved and reported to the prosecutor's office, but it can only be used as evidence of the newly discovered offence if that offence, too, appears on the closed list — even if the case later evolves so that the originally charged offence no longer applies. Whether evidence gathered this way survives judicial scrutiny is precisely the question addressed in Mona Hukuk's related discussion of the [assessment of unlawfully obtained evidence](/articles/evaluation-of-evidence-and-unlawful-evidence-turkish-criminal-procedure-code).

Additional safeguards apply regardless of which offence is involved. Communications between the suspect and family members who are legally entitled to refuse to testify against them may not be recorded, and if such material is captured before this is discovered, it must be destroyed immediately. Any authorization order must identify the offence, the person targeted, the specific communication line or device, and the precise scope and duration of the measure — interception may run for up to two months, extendable by one further month, with additional monthly extensions (capped at three months total) available where the offence is linked to organized criminal activity. Locating a suspect through mobile phone tracking to enable an arrest is treated as a related but separate measure under the same procedural logic. Records of who communicated with whom in the past, addressed in the article's sixth paragraph, are not subject to the closed-offence-list restriction and can in principle be sought for any offence, but everything obtained under Article 135 remains confidential for the duration of the measure, and monitoring another person's communications outside the article's specific procedure is strictly prohibited.

## Undercover Investigators (Article 139)

An undercover investigator is a public official — typically a police officer — assigned by judicial order to infiltrate a criminal enterprise using an altered identity, including forged supporting documents where necessary to maintain the cover. This tool is considerably narrower than communications interception: it applies only where the underlying offence is connected to organized criminal activity, and it is available only for a short, specific list of crimes — principally drug production and trafficking (which does not require an organizational link), establishing a criminal organization, and offences connected to armed organizations or supplying weapons to them, along with certain firearms-trafficking and cultural-heritage offences. Outside drug offences, the organizational connection is a mandatory element; deploying an undercover officer where no such connection exists is unlawful, and if the officer does not otherwise hold the status of a judicial law-enforcement officer, evidence they gather cannot be used to found a conviction.

The identity of an undercover investigator remains confidential even after the assignment ends, and if their testimony becomes indispensable at trial, they are heard away from those normally entitled to be present, or with voice and image disguised, by analogy to Turkey's witness-protection framework. Personal information gathered through the deployment can be used only in connection with the investigation for which the officer was assigned, and unrelated personal data must be destroyed immediately. An investigator cannot commit crimes in the course of the operation and is not held responsible for crimes committed by the organization under surveillance — but this protection has a mirror-image limit that matters enormously for the defence: if the officer's conduct crosses the line from passively observing an ongoing criminal enterprise into inducing a person to commit a crime they would not otherwise have committed, courts treat this as unlawful entrapment, and evidence obtained that way is excluded. Because it involves an ongoing, continuous method of investigation rather than a single act, an undercover deployment differs structurally from one-off protective measures like [search and seizure](/articles/search-and-seizure-turkish-criminal-procedure-code).

## Physical Surveillance with Technical Devices (Article 140)

Article 140 governs a different kind of monitoring: observing a suspect's activities in public places and at their workplace, and recording sound or image, using technical equipment such as hidden cameras or tracking devices. Because this measure targets physical conduct rather than communication content, it is subject to its own, narrower list of qualifying offences — one that notably excludes crimes such as torture, sexual assault and sexual abuse of a minor, which do appear on the Article 135 list. Authorization follows the same judge-first, prosecutor-in-urgent-cases structure as interception, with the same twenty-four-hour approval deadline, but the duration is shorter: up to three weeks, extendable by one additional week, or — where the offence is tied to an organized criminal enterprise — by further one-week increments up to a maximum of four weeks (or double those periods where combined with an undercover-investigator deployment). Article 140 explicitly cannot be used inside a suspect's home; extending covert surveillance into a private residence bypasses the separate constitutional protection for the inviolability of the home, and doing so renders the resulting recordings inadmissible. A recurring practical error is using an undercover-investigator authorization as if it also permitted technical surveillance under Article 140 — the two measures require entirely separate authorizing decisions, and one cannot stand in for the other.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a prosecutor order a wiretap without a judge's involvement?**
Only in urgent situations where waiting for judicial authorization would cause serious harm to the investigation. Even then, the order must go to a judge immediately, and the judge must decide within twenty-four hours. If that deadline lapses or the judge declines to approve it, the interception must stop immediately and any recordings are destroyed.

**If investigators intercept my communications while looking into one crime and discover evidence of an unrelated offence, can that evidence be used against me?**
Only if the newly discovered offence also appears on the closed list of offences that qualify for interception under Article 135. If it does not — for example, if the original investigation concerned an organized-crime offence but the incidental material only reveals a personal dispute or an offence outside the list — that material cannot be used to prosecute the new charge, even though it was lawfully captured for the original investigation.

**Does an undercover officer need court approval for every case they work?**
Yes, and the case must fall within a narrow list of offences generally tied to organized crime (with a specific exception for drug trafficking, which does not require an organizational link). If a law-enforcement officer operates covertly outside that framework — for instance, in a case with no organized-crime connection — they are not legally an "undercover investigator" under Article 139, and any conviction built primarily on their reports risks being overturned unless they are heard as an ordinary witness.

**Can the police use an undercover operation to persuade someone to commit a crime and then arrest them for it?**
No. Turkish courts distinguish between an officer who conceals their identity to observe and document a crime already being committed or planned, which is lawful, and one who actively creates a criminal intent that did not previously exist — commonly called entrapment or acting as an agent provocateur. Evidence obtained through the latter is treated as unlawfully obtained and excluded from the case.
