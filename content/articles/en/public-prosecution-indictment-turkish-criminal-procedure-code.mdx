When a criminal investigation in Turkey concludes, the outcome is not automatic. A Turkish public prosecutor (Cumhuriyet savcısı) does not simply hand the file to a court; the law gives the prosecutor sole authority to decide whether the evidence gathered justifies formal prosecution, and it sets out, in detail, what the resulting charging document — the indictment (iddianame) — must contain. For a foreign national under investigation in Turkey, or for family members and counsel trying to understand what happens after a police or prosecutorial inquiry, this stage is often the most consequential and the least visible part of the process, because it happens entirely outside the courtroom. This article explains, in plain terms, how a Turkish prosecutor decides whether to prosecute, what an indictment must look like, the alternatives to prosecution the law allows, and how a court may reject a defective indictment before the case ever reaches trial.

## Only the Prosecutor Decides Whether to Prosecute

Under Article 170 of the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (Ceza Muhakemesi Kanunu, "CMK"), the decision to bring a criminal case belongs exclusively to the public prosecutor. Neither the victim, nor a private complainant, nor any other authority can file criminal charges directly with a court — that gatekeeping function sits with the prosecution alone. The legal standard the prosecutor applies is described as "sufficient suspicion" (yeterli şüphe): a level of proof higher than a mere abstract possibility that a crime occurred, but well below the conviction-level certainty a trial court would eventually need. In practice, Turkish courts and legal commentary describe this as a filtering function — the prosecutor should only proceed to trial when the case, as investigated, is realistically capable of ending in a conviction without further evidence-gathering being necessary. Cases that do not meet this threshold are meant to be screened out before they ever reach a courtroom, sparing both the judiciary and the suspect the burden of an unwarranted trial.

## What an Indictment Must Contain

Article 170 lists, in detail, the information an indictment must set out: the identity of the suspect and defense counsel, the identity of the victim or injured party (and their legal representative, if any), the identity of any informant or complainant where disclosure is not harmful, the date a complaint was filed, the alleged offense and the applicable statutory provisions, the place, date and time the offense allegedly occurred, the evidence supporting the charge, and — where relevant — whether the suspect has been detained or arrested, and for how long. These are not mere formalities. Requiring the victim's identity and a concrete account of events tied to specific evidence is what turns the indictment from a vague accusation into a testable case the defense can actually respond to. The law also requires something that might surprise defendants used to adversarial systems elsewhere: the closing section of the indictment must set out not only the facts unfavorable to the suspect but also those in the suspect's favor. This reflects a broader principle running through Turkish criminal procedure — the prosecutor is expected to gather exculpatory evidence during the investigation, not just build a one-sided case, and that obligation carries through into the charging document itself.

## Alternatives to Prosecution: Effective Repentance and Deferred Prosecution

Sufficient suspicion does not automatically mean a case goes to trial. Article 171 carves out two situations where the prosecutor may decline to prosecute even though the evidentiary threshold is met. First, where the specific offense carries a statutory "effective repentance" provision (a personal ground reducing or eliminating criminal liability, common in offenses such as certain economic crimes) or a personal immunity from punishment applies, the prosecutor may issue a decision not to prosecute. Second, and more broadly used, the prosecutor may defer bringing a case for five years in offenses punishable by a maximum of three years' imprisonment or less (excluding offenses subject to pre-payment or mediation). This deferral is only available where several conditions are met together: the suspect has no prior conviction for an intentional offense, the investigation suggests the suspect will refrain from reoffending if given this chance, deferral serves the interests of both the suspect and society better than prosecution would, and any harm caused to the victim or the public has been fully repaired. If no intentional offense is committed during the five-year deferral period, the case is formally closed with a decision not to prosecute; if one is committed, prosecution proceeds. The limitation period is suspended for the duration of the deferral. Turkish appellate practice has repeatedly stressed that a deferral decision is only valid once served on the suspect together with a clear written warning of the consequences of reoffending and of the right to challenge the decision — a defectively served deferral decision is treated as not yet final, meaning the five-year clock has not actually started running. This mechanism is unavailable for organized-crime offenses, offenses committed by or against public officials in connection with their duties, military offenses, and sexual offenses.

## Decisions Not to Prosecute and the Right to Object

Where the evidence gathered does not reach the sufficient-suspicion threshold, or prosecution is otherwise not possible, Article 172 requires the prosecutor to issue a decision not to prosecute (kovuşturmaya yer olmadığına dair karar). This decision must be communicated to the victim and to any suspect who was questioned during the investigation, and it must expressly state the right to object, the deadline, and the competent authority. Importantly, this is not a final acquittal — if genuinely new evidence later emerges, and a criminal judgeship of peace (sulh ceza hâkimliği) approves reopening the matter, the same conduct can be investigated again. Article 173 sets out how a victim can challenge a decision not to prosecute: an objection must be filed within two weeks of notification, with the criminal judgeship of peace in the jurisdiction of the relevant high criminal court, and the objection must identify concrete facts and evidence capable of justifying prosecution. If the judgeship finds the objection well-founded, the prosecutor must draft an indictment and submit the case to the trial court; if not, the objection is dismissed with reasons and the objecting party bears the costs. The same objection procedure applies to a prosecutor's discretionary decision to defer prosecution under Article 171, not only to a straightforward finding of insufficient evidence.

## The Court's Power to Return a Defective Indictment

Even after a prosecutor formally decides to prosecute, the trial court performs one further check before the case can proceed. Under Article 174, within fifteen days of receiving the indictment and investigation file, the court may return the indictment to the prosecutor's office, specifying the defects, in four situations: the indictment does not comply with the mandatory content requirements of Article 170; it was drafted without gathering any evidence directly bearing on whether the offense actually occurred; the case is clearly one that should have gone through pre-payment, mediation, or the expedited trial procedure but did not; or the offense required prior permission or a request (for certain categories of offense) that was never obtained. An indictment cannot be returned merely because the court disagrees with how the prosecutor legally classified the offense — reclassification is the trial court's own job to resolve during the case, not a ground to reject the charging document outright. If the court does not act within the fifteen-day window, the indictment is deemed accepted by operation of law, which makes this deadline procedurally decisive. Where an indictment is returned, the prosecutor may only cure the specific defects identified and resubmit — a second return citing a new, previously unmentioned defect is not permitted. The prosecutor may object to a return decision. Once an indictment clears this review, the case formally moves into the trial-preparation and trial stage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a victim force a prosecutor to bring charges in Turkey?**
Not directly. Only the prosecutor can file an indictment. A victim who disagrees with a decision not to prosecute can object within two weeks to the competent criminal judgeship of peace, which can order the prosecutor to proceed if it finds the objection justified.

**If a Turkish prosecutor decides not to prosecute, is that final?**
Not necessarily. A decision not to prosecute can be revisited if new evidence emerges and a criminal judgeship of peace approves reopening the investigation into the same conduct.

**What is "deferred prosecution" and does it mean the case disappears?**
It is a discretionary mechanism available for offenses with a maximum sentence of three years or less, under specific conditions including no prior intentional-offense conviction and full repair of any harm caused. If no new intentional offense is committed within five years, the case ends with a decision not to prosecute; if one is, the case proceeds.

**Can a court reject an indictment just because it disagrees with the charge?**
No. An indictment cannot be returned solely because the court would classify the alleged offense differently — that determination is made by the trial court itself once the case proceeds, not through a return of the indictment.
