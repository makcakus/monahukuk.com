Foreign nationals facing criminal charges in Turkey often reach the trial stage without understanding what has actually changed: the investigation is over, an indictment has been accepted by a court, and a formal, courtroom-based process now governs what happens to them. This transition — from a prosecutor-led inquiry to a judge-supervised hearing where evidence is tested in the open — is the point at which a defendant's right to be heard, to know precisely what they are accused of, and to respond to the evidence against them becomes concrete. The Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (CMK) sets out, in considerable procedural detail, how this stage must unfold: when the court schedules the hearing, in what order the defendant is addressed, when questioning may be skipped, and when a defendant can be excused from personally attending every session. Understanding this sequence matters because departures from it are among the most common grounds on which Turkish appellate courts overturn convictions.

## From Indictment to Hearing Date

Under CMK Article 175, the trial phase (kovuşturma) formally begins the moment a court accepts the indictment submitted by the prosecutor. Everything before that point — detention, statements taken by police or the prosecutor, evidence-gathering — belongs to the investigation phase and does not yet constitute a trial in the legal sense. Once the indictment is accepted, the court's first tasks are administrative but consequential: it must set a hearing date and issue summonses to everyone who needs to be present, including the defendant, defence counsel, witnesses and expert witnesses. These notifications are not a formality to be glossed over. A hearing conducted without the defendant having been properly and lawfully summoned exposes the resulting judgment to challenge on appeal, and Turkish courts take this seriously — as the case law discussed below illustrates. During this preparatory window the court also checks whether the file is genuinely ready for trial, including whether any additional evidence needs to be gathered or any notification gap needs to be closed before the first hearing takes place.

## How the Hearing Opens and the Fixed Order of Questioning

CMK Article 191 lays out, step by step, how a criminal hearing must open. The presiding judge first confirms who is present — the defendant, defence counsel, and any summoned witnesses or experts — and formally declares the hearing open by reading out the decision accepting the indictment. Witnesses are then asked to leave the courtroom, so that later testimony is not shaped by what they hear from others. What follows is a fixed sequence that the law does not leave to the court's discretion: the defendant's identity, along with their personal and financial circumstances, is established first; the acts and evidence underlying the accusation, together with its legal classification, are then explained to the defendant; the defendant is reminded of the right to remain silent and of other statutory rights; and only if the defendant indicates willingness to speak does the formal questioning (sorgu) take place. This order is not incidental — it is designed to ensure the defendant actually understands the accusation before being invited to respond to it.

Each step in this sequence serves a distinct protective function. Establishing identity first allows a case of mistaken identity or a mixed-up criminal record to surface at the earliest possible moment. Asking about financial circumstances matters later, when the court considers a judicial fine or the allocation of trial costs. The step requiring the accusation to be explained was substantially strengthened by a legislative amendment: it is no longer enough for the court to simply read the indictment aloud. The judge must now explain, in terms the defendant can actually follow, the specific acts alleged, the evidence said to support them, and how the law characterizes the offence. The purpose is straightforward — a defendant cannot mount an effective defence against an accusation they have not genuinely understood.

## Why Questioning the Defendant Is Almost Always Mandatory

CMK Article 193 states the default rule bluntly: except in the narrow situations the law itself carves out, a hearing cannot proceed against a defendant who is not present, and a defendant who fails to appear without valid excuse can be compelled to attend. The exception to this rule is deliberately narrow. A case can be concluded without the defendant ever being questioned only where the evidence gathered points toward an outcome favorable to the defendant — a decision to acquit, a decision that no penalty is warranted, or an outcome other than the imposition of a security measure. Outside that narrow exception — in other words, whenever a conviction is contemplated — the defendant's questioning is not optional. This is one of the clearest procedural guarantees of the right to a defence in Turkish criminal practice, and as the Court of Cassation decisions summarized below show, skipping it is treated as reversible error in its own right.

## When a Defendant Can Be Excused From Attending, and Remote Questioning

The strict presence rule of Article 193 is softened, to a limited degree, by CMK Article 196. A defendant who has already been questioned once may, at their own request or through defence counsel specifically authorized to make that request, be excused from personally attending later sessions — a meaningful relief in lengthy, multi-session trials. Separately, for offences carrying a minimum sentence below five years' imprisonment, a defendant may be questioned through a different court by way of judicial assistance (istinabe) rather than in person before the trial court; the record of that questioning is then read aloud at the actual hearing. Where the minimum sentence is five years or more, this route is not available, and questioning must take place before the trial court itself. Turkish procedure has also adapted to technology: where a judge or court considers it necessary, a defendant present within Turkey may be questioned, or may participate in hearings, through simultaneous audio-visual communication (the SEGBİS system) rather than in person.

## Presenting Evidence, Courtroom Discipline, and the Right to Question Witnesses

Once questioning is complete, the hearing moves to the presentation of evidence, and the law regulates this stage closely as well. The court retains the power, at any point, to compel the defendant's attendance through a summons or an arrest order (Article 199). If there is concern that an accomplice or witness will not tell the truth in the defendant's presence, the defendant may be removed from the courtroom during that person's questioning — but must be brought back afterward and informed of what was said through the minutes taken in their absence (Article 200), a rule that balances establishing the truth against the defendant's right to know what evidence has been given against them. Article 201 governs cross-examination: the prosecutor, defence counsel, and any lawyer appearing as a representative may put questions directly — subject to courtroom discipline — to the defendant, the victim/intervening party, witnesses, experts and other participants, while the defendant and the intervening party must channel their own questions through the presiding judge; objections to a particular question are resolved by the presiding judge. Finally, Article 202 addresses language and accessibility: a defendant or victim who cannot follow proceedings adequately in Turkish is entitled to a court-appointed interpreter for the essential points of the claims and defence, and a defendant or victim with a disability is entitled to have those essential points explained in a manner they can understand.

## How Procedural Errors at This Stage Affect the Outcome

The hearing phase concludes with closing statements from the parties on the merits, after which the court issues its judgment. Every procedural misstep along the way — a conviction handed down without questioning, a defective summons, or an unlawful restriction on the right to question witnesses — can become grounds for the judgment to be overturned on appeal. For this reason, the way trial preparation and the hearing itself are conducted is not a mere technical formality; it is what makes the process a genuine safeguard for everyone involved, including the victim and any intervening party. In practice, notification failures during trial preparation can lead to defendants being convicted without ever having learned that a hearing was taking place — an outcome that is very difficult to undo later — which is why courts are expected to take particular care with notification periods and address accuracy when setting a hearing date. Likewise, in cases involving multiple defendants or witnesses, disciplined management of the questioning order, the manner in which evidence is presented, and the exercise of the right to ask questions has a direct bearing not only on the lawfulness of the eventual judgment but on how quickly the case can reasonably be resolved. One of defence counsel's most important tasks at this stage is to monitor, session by session, whether these procedural rules are actually being followed, and to place any objection on the record immediately — an objection raised later, after the fact, is generally far less likely to succeed than one recorded at the time it arose.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a Turkish court convict a defendant who was never personally questioned?**
As a rule, no. Under CMK Article 193, a conviction requires that the defendant be questioned, and Turkish appellate courts have repeatedly overturned convictions reached without it. The only exception is where the evidence points toward an outcome favorable to the defendant, such as acquittal.

**If I am facing charges but living abroad, can I avoid attending every hearing session in Turkey?**
Once you have been questioned at least once, you or your specifically authorized defence counsel may request to be excused from attending later sessions in person. For lower-level offences, questioning itself can sometimes take place through a different court via judicial assistance, with the record read into the trial file. For offences with a five-year-or-higher minimum sentence, this route is unavailable and personal attendance before the trial court is required for questioning.

**What happens if I do not speak Turkish well enough to follow my own hearing?**
The law entitles a defendant who cannot adequately follow proceedings in Turkish to a court-appointed interpreter, who conveys the essential points of the accusation and the defence. The same protection extends to defendants or victims with disabilities, who are entitled to have those essential points explained in an understandable way.

**Does the failure to read out the decision accepting the indictment automatically invalidate a hearing?**
Not necessarily on its own. Turkish case law has treated the omission of this reading, by itself, as insufficient to overturn a judgment, though it can strengthen an appeal when combined with another genuine procedural defect, such as a defendant being questioned without having first been properly informed of the charge.
