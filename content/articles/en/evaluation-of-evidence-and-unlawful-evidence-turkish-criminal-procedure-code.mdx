Foreign nationals facing criminal charges in Turkey often assume that if the prosecution has a document, a recording, or a witness statement pointing to guilt, that material will simply be used against them at trial. Turkish criminal procedure does not work that way. A criminal court is not free to rely on any material that happens to land on the file — it may only build its judgment on evidence that was gathered lawfully and then properly presented and discussed at the hearing. This principle, anchored in Articles 206 and 217 of the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (CMK), is one of the most important protections available to a defendant, and it is frequently decisive in cases involving searches, seized devices, wiretaps, or covertly recorded conversations.

## The Order in Which Evidence Is Presented at Trial

Turkish trial procedure follows a deliberate sequence: evidence is presented to the court only after the defendant has been questioned. The logic behind this order is protective — it lets the accused give their own account of events before the court works through the material gathered against them, so that each piece of evidence can be met with a specific, informed response rather than a generic denial made in the dark. The law does build in a practical exception: if a defendant who was properly summoned fails to appear without a valid excuse, the trial does not grind to a halt merely because questioning could not take place — the court may proceed to examine the evidence, provided that the defendant is informed of what was presented once they do appear. A further, party-driven flexibility exists: if the public prosecutor and the defendant (or defense counsel) jointly agree, the court may dispense with hearing a particular witness or examining some other item of evidence, sparing the proceedings unnecessary delay when neither side actually contests the point.

## Grounds on Which a Court Must Refuse to Admit Evidence

The law does not leave it to a judge's unstructured discretion to decide what evidence enters a Turkish criminal trial. Article 206 sets out three specific grounds on which an item of evidence offered by either side must be rejected. The first, and by far the most significant for defense strategy, is that the evidence was obtained in violation of the law — this is the operative mechanism through which the broader unlawful-evidence prohibition is enforced at the hearing stage, discussed in detail below. The second ground applies where the fact the evidence is meant to prove would have no bearing on the outcome of the case; here the court is expected to articulate a concrete reason why the evidence is irrelevant, not simply assert it. The third ground guards against procedural abuse: a request to introduce evidence made solely to drag out the proceedings can be refused outright. Taken together, these three grounds strike a balance between two competing risks — a trial that never ends because either side keeps introducing peripheral material, and a trial that becomes unfair because material obtained through improper means is allowed to influence the verdict.

## The Constitutional Foundation: Why Unlawfully Obtained Evidence Cannot Support a Conviction

The prohibition in Article 206 does not stand alone. Article 217 of the CMK governs how a judge weighs evidence more generally, and it does so subject to two firm limits. First, a judge may base a decision only on evidence that was actually brought before the court and discussed at the hearing — material sitting in the file that was never presented and debated in open court cannot quietly support a conviction. Second, and more fundamentally, the article makes clear that a charge may only be proven through evidence that was obtained by lawful means. Read together with Article 206's exclusion rule, this creates what is often described as a two-layer safeguard: Article 206 stops unlawfully obtained evidence from being introduced at the hearing in the first place, while Article 217 confirms that even if such evidence somehow makes it onto the file, it cannot be the basis of a judgment. This dual structure directly implements the constitutional guarantee, found in Article 38 of the Turkish Constitution, that findings obtained unlawfully cannot be treated as evidence — and it mirrors the fair-trial guarantee under Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which Turkey has ratified and applies directly in criminal proceedings.

## Where This Comes Up in Practice: Searches, Seizures, and Communication Surveillance

In real cases, the unlawful-evidence question most often surfaces around [search and seizure operations](/articles/search-and-seizure-turkish-criminal-procedure-code) conducted without a properly issued judicial order, or executed beyond the scope that order allowed. If the authority that issued the search order lacked jurisdiction to do so, if the search went beyond its authorized limits, or if the seizure of items did not follow the procedure the law requires, the resulting evidence is tainted from the outset. The same sensitivity extends to more invasive measures such as [communication interception and the use of undercover investigators](/articles/communication-surveillance-turkish-criminal-procedure-code), which are only lawful when both the underlying offense falls within the specific catalog the law enumerates and every procedural condition attached to the measure has been met. Turkish appellate case law consistently treats the unlawfulness inquiry as reaching not just how a search was physically carried out, but also the validity of the decision or order that authorized it in the first place — a defect in either can taint the resulting evidence. Courts are also expected to record, expressly and separately in the reasoning of a conviction, not only the evidence relied upon but also any evidence on the file that was excluded because it was unlawfully obtained, so that a higher court reviewing the case can verify what was actually used.

## Consequences of Exclusion: Grounds for Reversal and the "Fruit of the Poisonous Tree" Question

A judgment built on unlawfully obtained evidence is treated as one of the categories of absolute procedural defect, which by itself provides grounds for [reversal on appeal](/articles/appeal-to-court-of-cassation-turkish-criminal-procedure-code). In practice, this means trial courts are expected to scrutinize the origin of every piece of evidence they rely on, exclude anything whose lawfulness is genuinely in doubt, and explain clearly why any contested item was or was not excluded. This oversight matters not only to the individual defendant but to the broader credibility of the justice system — tolerating unlawfully gathered evidence, over time, weakens the incentive for police and investigative authorities to follow proper procedure at all.

A related and more technical question is what happens to a second piece of evidence that was only discovered because of an earlier unlawful act — for instance, a document found only because of an unlawful search that, in turn, pointed investigators toward further material. This is essentially the same problem addressed in American law under the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine. Turkish courts assess this on a case-by-case basis, and the prevailing approach recognizes that evidence which could independently have been obtained through a separate, lawful route need not automatically be treated as tainted simply because an unlawful act happened to lead investigators to it. Where that line falls in a given case is often the single most consequential argument defense counsel can make when building an evidentiary challenge.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If the police searched my home or seized my phone without a proper warrant, can that evidence be used against me?**
Not automatically, and often not at all. If the search order was defective, issued by an authority without power to do so, or the search or seizure exceeded what the order permitted, the resulting evidence should be excluded under Article 206, and a conviction resting on it can be reversed on appeal.

**Does this exclusion rule mean the case against me automatically falls apart?**
No. Exclusion applies to the specific tainted item, not necessarily to the whole case. If the prosecution has other, independently and lawfully obtained evidence sufficient to prove the charge, the case can proceed on that basis. The practical effect depends heavily on how central the excluded evidence was.

**Can a private recording made secretly by another individual, rather than the police, be excluded as unlawful evidence?**
Yes, this is a live issue in Turkish practice, particularly for covert recordings made by private individuals as part of a planned setup. Courts are expected to examine the lawfulness of how such material was obtained just as carefully as they would examine a police-gathered item, rather than assuming private origin automatically makes it admissible.

**What should I do if I believe evidence used against me was gathered unlawfully?**
This should be raised as a specific, documented objection at trial — identifying exactly which procedural step was defective (the authorization, the execution, or both) — since a general complaint that "the evidence is unfair" is far less effective than a precise legal challenge tied to Articles 206 and 217.
