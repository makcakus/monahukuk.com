A vote is only meaningful if the shareholder casting it actually knows what is happening in the company. TTK Art. 434-437 pair the mechanics of voting power with the information rights that make that power exercisable in an informed way.

## Voting Power Follows Paid-Up Capital

Art. 434 ties voting rights to the nominal value of shares held, though every shareholder gets at least one vote regardless of holding size, and the articles may cap the votes available to large holders — a tool for preventing a controlling shareholder from completely sidelining the rest. Art. 435 fixes the trigger: voting rights arise only once the legally or contractually required minimum has actually been paid on the share, tying governance influence directly to capital actually contributed.

## Narrow Conflict-of-Interest Bans

Art. 436 disqualifies a shareholder from voting on matters involving a personal transaction, dispute, or litigation between themselves (or close family, or entities they control) and the company, and separately bars board members and authorized signatories from voting their own shares on their own discharge. Turkish courts treat these bans as strict exceptions to the general rule of voting freedom — not to be extended by analogy. Case law has, however, read the discharge ban to also cover votes on whether to file a liability lawsuit against a board member (as a heavier consequence of non-discharge), while treating a board member's vote cast as legal guardian for another shareholder as outside the ban's scope — a boundary the courts continue to police carefully.

## The Right to Information and Inspection

Art. 437 requires financial statements, the board's annual report, audit reports, and the profit distribution proposal to be available for inspection at least fifteen days before the meeting, with financial statements staying accessible for a full year afterward. During the meeting itself, shareholders may question the board about company affairs and the auditor about audit results — and crucially, if one shareholder receives information outside the meeting, any other shareholder can demand the same information on request; the board cannot then invoke the trade-secret defense. Requests refused, ignored, or left unanswered can go to the commercial court within ten days of a refusal (or a reasonable time otherwise), and the right to information cannot be curtailed by the articles or by any corporate body's decision. Turkish courts have also held that inspecting the company's books requires the request to first have been properly raised at the assembly or with the board — a procedural prerequisite before litigation.
