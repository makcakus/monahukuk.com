When a workplace accident or occupational disease occurs in Turkey, the employer's exposure is rarely a single claim. A single incident can trigger a compensation lawsuit from the employee or their family, a recovery action from the state social security institution, and — where the employer's fault is severe enough — criminal proceedings. Understanding how these three tracks fit together, and why Turkish courts treat the employer's duty of care as a *contractual* obligation rather than an ordinary tort, is essential for any business operating in Turkey. This article explains the legal architecture behind employer liability once an accident has already happened, building on the general preventive OHS duties covered separately on this site.

## The Contractual Basis: Article 417 of the Code of Obligations

The starting point is not the Labour Code but Article 417 of the Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098). This provision obliges the employer to protect the employee's personality rights and to take every measure necessary to ensure occupational health and safety at the workplace, while employees are correspondingly required to comply with the safety measures put in place. The third paragraph of Article 417 then makes the decisive move: where the employee's death, bodily injury or violation of personality rights results from the employer's breach of a statutory or contractual duty, the resulting compensation claim is governed by the rules on liability for breach of contract, not by the rules on tort.

This classification is not a technicality. Turkish courts have consistently held — including the Court of Cassation's General Assembly of Civil Chambers — that because the duty of care (in Turkish, *gözetme borcu*) arises from the employment relationship itself, an employee injured in a workplace accident sues under contract law. In practice this shifts the burden of proof: once the employee shows the damage and the causal link between the accident and that damage, it falls to the employer to prove it was not at fault. This is considerably more favourable to the injured employee than the fault-based standard that generally applies in tort claims, and it also affects which limitation periods apply to the claim.

## Filling the Duty of Care with OHS Legislation

Article 417 sets out a general contractual obligation, but it does not by itself tell an employer which concrete precautions to take. That content comes from occupational health and safety legislation. The OHS provisions that used to sit in Articles 77–89 of the Labour Code (Law No. 4857) were repealed as of 20 June 2012 by Article 37 of the Occupational Health and Safety Law (Law No. 6331), and the employer's detailed obligation to take precautions is now set out primarily in Article 4 of Law No. 6331. When a court assesses an employer's fault in an accident or occupational-disease case, Article 417 supplies the general contractual framework, while Law No. 6331 and its implementing regulations supply the technical benchmark for what the employer should actually have done. Because the underlying rules changed in 2012, the Court of Cassation first identifies whether the accident is governed by the former Labour Code provisions or by Law No. 6331 before determining the employer's specific obligations.

## Defining "Workplace Accident" and "Occupational Disease" Under Law No. 5510

Before any compensation claim can proceed, the incident must qualify as a workplace accident or occupational disease under Article 13 and Article 14 of the Social Security and General Health Insurance Law (Law No. 5510). Article 13 defines a workplace accident broadly: an event that occurs while the insured person is at the workplace, in the course of work carried out by or on behalf of the employer, while travelling for work duties outside the workplace, during time set aside for a nursing mother to breastfeed her child, or during transport provided by the employer to and from work — and which leaves the insured person physically or mentally impaired. Article 14 defines an occupational disease as a temporary or permanent illness, or physical or mental impairment, suffered because of the nature of the work performed or the conditions under which it is carried out, with the determination made by the Social Security Institution's Health Board.

These definitions matter well beyond terminology. Qualification as a workplace accident or occupational disease under Law No. 5510 is the precondition for the insurance benefits the Social Security Institution provides — temporary incapacity allowance, permanent incapacity income, and survivors' income in the event of death — and it is equally the precondition for both the direct compensation claim against the employer under Article 417 and the Institution's separate recovery action described below.

## Fault, Causation and the Institution's Recovery Action

Where the Social Security Institution has paid benefits or granted income to the insured person or their survivors following a workplace accident or occupational disease, and that event resulted from the employer's intent, or from conduct contrary to occupational health and safety legislation that could be classified as an offence, the Institution recovers those amounts from the employer. The amount recovered is calculated according to the employer's degree of fault. This recovery action is a distinct, public-law relationship separate from the employer's direct contractual liability under Article 417, but the determination of fault is central to both. Importantly, the absence of an adequate causal link between the employer's fault and the accident does not undo the fact that a workplace accident occurred — it can, however, remove or reduce the employer's liability. Courts must therefore examine the degree of fault and the causal link between that fault and the damage as two separate questions.

## How the Compensation, Recovery and Criminal Tracks Interact

Put together, an employer facing a workplace accident or occupational disease in Turkey can be exposed on three fronts simultaneously: a material and non-pecuniary (moral) compensation claim from the employee or their heirs under Article 417 of the Code of Obligations; a recovery claim from the Social Security Institution under Law No. 5510 for benefits it has already paid, calculated according to the employer's degree of fault; and, where the conduct is serious enough to be classified as an offence, criminal liability. None of these three is a substitute for the others, and a favourable outcome on one does not resolve the other two — each requires an independent assessment of fault and causation on its own record.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does it matter that workplace accident claims are treated as contractual rather than tort claims?**
Because Article 417/3 of the Code of Obligations routes these claims through contractual liability, the employee benefits from a more favourable evidentiary position: once damage and causation are shown, the burden shifts to the employer to prove the absence of fault. This also affects the applicable limitation period compared to an ordinary tort claim.

**Does the Social Security Institution's recovery action replace the employee's own compensation claim?**
No. The Institution's recovery action under Law No. 5510 is a separate public-law mechanism to recoup the benefits and income it has paid out; it does not substitute for, or reduce, the employee's own direct compensation claim against the employer for material and moral damages under Article 417.

**Can an employer be liable if there is no clear causal link between its fault and the accident?**
The absence of a proper causal link between the employer's faulty conduct and the accident does not change the fact that a workplace accident occurred, but it can eliminate or reduce the employer's liability. Courts examine the degree of fault and the causal connection as two distinct issues.

**Is every workplace injury automatically a "workplace accident" for legal purposes?**
No. The event must fall within the specific circumstances listed in Article 13 of Law No. 5510 (at the workplace, in the course of assigned work, during authorised travel, during breastfeeding breaks, or during employer-provided transport, among others) and result in physical or mental impairment. Occupational disease additionally requires a determination by the Social Security Institution's Health Board under Article 14.
