Foreign nationals who become involved in Turkish criminal proceedings — whether as suspects, witnesses, victims, or through counsel following a case from abroad — often assume that only the outcome of a case matters. Turkish law takes a different view: the integrity of the process itself is protected by a distinct cluster of offenses in the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), Articles 285 through 288, all found in the chapter dealing with offenses against judicial administration. These provisions criminalize leaking details of an ongoing investigation, recording court and investigative proceedings without authorization, subjecting a person to a genital examination without judicial approval, and publicly pressuring judges, experts or witnesses to reach a particular outcome. Together they exist to ensure that a Turkish criminal case is decided on its evidence and its law, not on premature publicity, unauthorized recordings, unsanctioned bodily intrusions, or outside pressure on the people responsible for deciding it.

## Breach of Investigation Confidentiality (Article 285)

Article 285 protects two related interests: the presumption that a suspect is not to be treated as guilty before conviction, and the effectiveness of the investigation itself, which can be undermined if its contents leak before the facts are established. The offense is not triggered by disclosure alone. Turkish law requires two elements to be present together — the disclosure must interfere with the presumption of innocence, the confidentiality of communications, or personal privacy, and it must also be capable of hindering the investigation's ability to establish the truth. This dual test is what separates ordinary news reporting about a case from a punishable leak, and the article explicitly states that reporting on investigative or trial proceedings, so long as it stays within the bounds of legitimate news coverage, is not an offense at all.

The article separately criminalizes several related acts: disclosing the content of an investigative step, revealing a decision that by law must be kept confidential from a party to the investigation, disclosing statements or images from a hearing that was legally required to be closed to the public, and publishing a person's image in a way that creates the impression they are guilty before any determination has been made. Most of these carry a prison term of one to three years or a judicial fine, while publishing images that create a false impression of guilt carries a separate penalty of six months to two years' imprisonment. Notably, where the confidentiality order exists specifically to protect a witness's identity, the law does not require that the disclosure reach the general public — revealing the witness's identity to even a single unauthorized person can be enough. If a public official commits any of these acts by exploiting the ease their position provides, the penalty can be increased by up to half.

## Unauthorized Recording of Judicial Proceedings (Article 286)

This provision addresses a narrower and more technical concern: preserving the orderly conduct of investigative and trial proceedings and protecting the people involved in them — parties, witnesses, officials — from being recorded without authorization. The offense covers both making an unauthorized audio or video recording of a judicial proceeding and transmitting such a recording; either act on its own is sufficient. The absence of authorization is central to the offense — recordings made through an officially sanctioned channel, such as an authorized court recording, fall outside the article entirely. The maximum penalty is capped at six months' imprisonment, a comparatively light sanction that signals this is a procedural, order-preserving offense rather than one aimed at punishing serious harm.

## Genital Examination Without Judicial Authorization (Article 287)

Article 287 addresses one of the most invasive procedural measures available in a criminal case: a genital examination of a suspect or another individual connected to an investigation. Turkish law treats this measure as lawful only when a judge or prosecutor has specifically authorized it in advance — without that authorization, both the person who refers someone for such an examination and the person who actually carries it out can be held criminally liable, each independently. The penalty ranges from three months to one year in prison. A specific exception preserves examinations carried out under public-health rules for the legitimate purpose of controlling contagious disease, which makes clear that the provision targets arbitrary use of this measure, not medically necessary examinations conducted through the proper channel.

## Attempting to Influence a Fair Trial (Article 288)

Article 288 protects judicial independence from outside pressure exerted through public statements. The offense consists of making a public verbal or written statement, in connection with a pending case or an ongoing investigation, intended to steer a judge, an expert witness, or a witness toward an unlawful decision, act, or false statement. A key feature of this provision is that the attempt itself is the completed offense — Turkish law does not require that the pressure actually succeed in changing anyone's decision or testimony. Because the statement must be made with the specific intent of steering the recipient toward an unlawful outcome, ordinary criticism or genuine academic commentary on a case, made without that intent, does not meet the threshold. The penalty is a judicial fine with a statutory minimum but no stated maximum, leaving the exact amount to the court's discretion within general sentencing rules.

## Common Threads: Intent, Complaint, and Practical Handling

All four offenses require intent — none of them can be committed negligently. Because Article 285 defines several distinct acts across its paragraphs, each paragraph must be assessed on its own facts, with the public-official aggravating factor considered separately where relevant. Article 288 stands apart from the other three in using a fine-only penalty rather than imprisonment, reflecting a legislative choice to deter interference with judicial proceedings through a financial sanction rather than a loss of liberty. Comparing the four articles, the harshest penalties attach to breaching investigation confidentiality (Article 285), the lightest and most technical penalty to unauthorized recording (Article 286), while unlawful genital examination (Article 287) sits in between, reflecting its character as a direct intrusion on bodily integrity. Because the evidence underlying these offenses — recordings, published images, documentation of an unauthorized medical procedure — can be lost or become harder to establish over time, anyone considering a complaint should act promptly while the relevant material and records are still available.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a foreign national be a victim of one of these offenses in Turkey, and can they file a complaint?**
Yes. These offenses protect anyone involved in a Turkish investigation or prosecution — suspect, defendant, witness or victim — regardless of nationality. A foreign national affected by an unauthorized leak, recording, examination, or public pressure campaign connected to their case can file a criminal complaint through the same channels available to Turkish nationals, and is generally well advised to retain local counsel to manage the process.

**Does a journalist commit an offense simply by reporting on an ongoing criminal case?**
Not necessarily. Article 285 expressly excludes reporting that stays within the limits of legitimate news coverage. Liability arises only where the disclosure both harms a protected interest — such as the presumption of innocence or the confidentiality of the proceedings — and is capable of impeding the investigation's search for the truth. Reporting that does not create an impression of guilt and does not compromise the investigation generally falls outside the offense.

**Is every audio or video recording made during a police or court proceeding illegal?**
No. Article 286 only criminalizes recordings and transmissions made without authorization. A recording created through the proper official channel, such as an authorized court recording system, does not fall within the offense.

**Can a genital examination ever lawfully be carried out without a judge's or prosecutor's order?**
As a rule, no — prior judicial or prosecutorial authorization is required. The one exception is an examination conducted under public-health regulations for the purpose of controlling a contagious disease, which is treated as a separate, lawful category of medical necessity rather than an investigative measure.
