Having covered how the salvage award is earned, calculated, and split, this article closes out the salvage-award sub-section with its one negative check: Art. 1311, which lets a court or arbitral tribunal take the award back from a salvor who earned it the wrong way.

## The Three Grounds for Forfeiture

Art. 1311 allows the award to be withheld, in whole or in part, on three grounds: (i) the salvor's own fault made the salvage operation necessary in the first place; (ii) the salvor's own fault made an already-necessary operation more difficult; or (iii) the salvor engaged in fraud or other conduct inconsistent with good faith. The first two grounds are negligence-based — the salvor created or worsened the very danger it then purported to rescue the property from, for example through a careless maneuver or a poor technical decision. The third ground is qualitatively different: not carelessness but deliberate bad faith, such as overstating the danger involved or misrepresenting the scope of work performed to inflate the claim.

## A Discretionary, Proportionate Sanction

The statute's "may be deprived" language makes forfeiture discretionary rather than automatic, and the "in whole or in part" wording gives courts a full spectrum rather than a fixed percentage. This ties directly back to Art. 1304's "useful result" principle: a salvor who achieves a useful result is, in principle, entitled to an award, but Art. 1311 makes that entitlement defeasible where the salvor's own conduct fell short of the due-care standard Art. 1303(1)(a) demands. Art. 1304 governs how the right to an award arises; Art. 1311 governs how that same right can later be reduced or withdrawn. Without such a check, salvage law's reward-for-success structure would create a perverse incentive to chase a useful result by any means, including reckless or dishonest shortcuts — Art. 1311 ties the reward to how the result was reached, not only to the result itself.

## Court of Cassation Track Record

No Court of Cassation decision has been found deciding a case on the merits under Art. 1311 — that should be said plainly. One 11th Civil Chamber ruling (E.2016/8081, K.2018/1811, T.08.03.2018, arising from a fire and near-sinking aboard the vessel Tibil) shows the provision being raised defensively: the vessel owner argued that the responding tugboat's use of pressurized water worsened the ship's list and increased the risk of sinking, and invoked Art. 1311 to defeat the salvage claim entirely. The court upheld the salvor's entitlement to an award calculated under Art. 1305 based on expert evidence, without separately analyzing Art. 1311 in its reasoning — the defense did not prevail, but the ruling stops short of a reasoned merits treatment of the provision. An earlier interim-attachment ruling in the same dispute (E.2014/11224, K.2014/13369, T.08.09.2014) confirmed only the claim's status as a maritime lien, not the forfeiture question.
