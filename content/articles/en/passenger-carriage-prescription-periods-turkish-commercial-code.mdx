Every substantive protection in this Chapter operates within a time limit. TTK Art. 1270 sets that clock, and calibrates it very differently depending on what kind of harm is at stake.

## Ten Years for Death and Bodily Injury

Art. 1270(1) gives all compensation claims arising from a passenger's death or bodily injury a ten-year prescription period — dramatically longer than the one-year periods that apply to cargo claims (Art. 1188 and Art. 1246) or the two-year period this same article sets for other passenger-carriage claims. The rationale tracks the nature of the harm: unlike cargo loss, which is typically assessable shortly after delivery, the extent of a permanent disability, ongoing care needs, or lost future support often cannot be determined until years of treatment and rehabilitation have passed. A short period would be fundamentally inadequate for claims involving human health and life.

## Two Years for Everything Else

Art. 1270(2) subjects all other claims arising from the passenger carriage contract — including baggage loss or damage — to a two-year period. For baggage claims, the clock starts from whichever is *later*: the date the passenger actually disembarked, or the date disembarkation was due — a claimant-protective mechanic that avoids an artificially early start date. For all other claims, the period runs from the date the claim became due.

## Extending the Period: An Asymmetric Mechanism

Art. 1270(3) allows these periods to be extended — after the claim has already arisen — by either the carrier's unilateral written declaration or a written agreement between the parties. This creates a notable asymmetry compared to Art. 1264(1)'s requirement of mutual written agreement to *raise* a liability cap: since raising a cap increases the carrier's exposure and needs the carrier's genuine, documented consent, but extending a prescription period only benefits the claimant, requiring the claimant's separate consent serves no protective purpose. As with similar rules elsewhere in the Code, this extension can only be granted after the claim has already arisen — a boilerplate waiver embedded in a ticket at purchase time does not count.

## Case Law

A 2014 Court of Cassation decision (11th Civil Chamber, 2014/9100-2014/15966) confirmed the ten-year period applies to death/injury claims even under the predecessor Code, citing both a 1971 unification-of-case-law decision and TTK Art. 1270 itself as confirming the current rule. The 2026 Istanbul Regional Court of Appeal decision (12th Civil Chamber, 2026/420-2026/757, not yet final) applied the ten-year period concretely, rejecting a limitation defense where only eight years had passed between a 2014 accident and the lawsuit.

## Practical Takeaway

Claimants and their counsel should calendar the ten-year period for bodily-harm claims separately from the much shorter two-year period governing baggage and other claims — misapplying the shorter period to a bodily-injury claim, or vice versa, can be litigation-critical.
