Foreign companies operating in Turkey, and their local managers or advisers, increasingly encounter Article 257 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") when a public official's decision — a permit refusal, an inspection outcome, a delayed approval, a procurement ruling — is challenged as criminal rather than merely wrong. Article 257 criminalizes misconduct in public office, but it is deliberately built as a last-resort provision: it only applies once every more specific offense has been ruled out, and even then only if the misconduct actually produced a defined harm. Understanding this structure is essential for anyone assessing exposure, filing a criminal complaint, or defending a public official caught up in a Turkish criminal investigation.

## A Residual Offense, Not a General Catch-All

The protected interest behind Article 257 is the public's confidence that state functions are carried out honestly, impartially and effectively. The provision punishes a public official who, in connection with their duties, either acts contrary to what their office requires or improperly fails or delays to act, where that conduct produces victimization of a person, loss to the public, or an unjust advantage to someone.

The single most important interpretive feature of Article 257 is its opening qualifier: it applies only "in cases not otherwise defined as a separate offense by law." This turns the article into a subsidiary, backstop norm rather than a freestanding prohibition. If the same conduct already satisfies the elements of a more specific crime — embezzlement of public funds, extortion by a public official, bribery, unlawful deprivation of liberty, or forgery of official documents, among others — that specific provision governs, and Article 257 has no role to play. The analysis therefore always proceeds in a fixed order: first test the facts against every applicable specific offense; only if none of them fit does Article 257 become relevant. Skipping this sequence creates real risk in practice — conduct that in substance amounts to embezzlement or bribery can be wrongly downgraded to a misconduct charge, which understates both the wrongdoing and the applicable penalty, and can distort the entire prosecution.

## Who Can Commit the Offense, and Who Counts as a Victim

Article 257 is a special-capacity offense: only a public official can be its perpetrator. Turkish law defines a public official broadly, under Article 6/1(c) TCK, as anyone who participates in carrying out a public function — whether through appointment, election, or any other means, and whether on a permanent, fixed-term or temporary basis. This definition reaches well beyond career civil servants to cover elected officials and persons temporarily assigned to a public task.

The victim can be a private person who suffers direct harm, or the state itself where the relevant consequence is loss to the public purse. Because the offense can injure either an individual or the public administration as such, it carries both a personal and a collective dimension, and complaints can originate from either angle.

## Two Ways the Offense Can Be Committed

The statute recognizes two distinct forms of conduct, carrying different penalties:

**Active misconduct (first paragraph).** This covers a public official who acts contrary to the requirements of their office — that is, who exercises their authority in a way that actually breaches a statute, regulation or administrative procedure. The official is not passive; they take an affirmative step that oversteps or violates the boundaries of their function, such as knowingly issuing a decision that the applicable rules clearly prohibit.

**Neglect or delay (second paragraph).** This covers a public official who neglects or delays performing a duty they were required to perform. This is a passive failure — an obligation to act that is not fulfilled, or is fulfilled too late. The legislature treats this passive form as less serious than active misconduct, and the sentencing range reflects that: imprisonment from six months to two years for active misconduct, against three months to one year for neglect or delay. This gap shows that Turkish law does not equate the culpability of actively breaching a duty with that of simply failing to meet it.

A third paragraph, which originally addressed certain improper gains falling short of extortion, was repealed by Law No. 6352 in 2012. That repeal reinforced the backstop character of Article 257: conduct of that kind is now assessed under extortion or other specific offenses rather than under the general misconduct provision.

## The Result Requirement: Where Administrative Error Ends and Criminal Liability Begins

Neither form of the offense is complete on the strength of a rule breach alone. The law requires a defined consequence — victimization of a person, loss to the public, or an unjust advantage to someone — before liability attaches. This turns Article 257 into a result crime rather than a pure conduct crime, and the distinction matters enormously in practice. A public official's decision that departs from applicable regulations can, by itself, be a matter for administrative discipline; it only becomes a criminal matter once a concrete victimization, public loss or unjust gain is established as flowing from that decision. Establishing the causal link between the official's conduct and that specific result is the backbone of any indictment or judgment under this article — an abstract allegation of procedural irregularity, without proof of an actual harm or benefit, cannot support a conviction. This is also why the mere fact that an administrative court found a decision unlawful is not, on its own, sufficient grounds for a conviction under Article 257.

## Fault, Attempt, Participation and Overlap With Other Offenses

The offense requires direct or possible intent: the official must act knowing that their conduct departs from — or neglects — the requirements of their office, and that it may cause victimization, public loss, or unjust gain. Turkish law does not recognize a negligent version of this offense, so carelessness or inexperience alone falls outside Article 257 and remains within the realm of administrative or disciplinary responsibility. Whether the official knew the applicable rule in advance, and any documented prior warnings, are typically central to proving intent.

Because it is a result crime, an attempt is conceptually possible where execution begins but the required result never materializes, though this is assessed case by case given how divisible the underlying conduct is. Private individuals who are not public officials can participate as instigators or accomplices under the general rules on special-capacity offenses. Where the same act also satisfies a separate offense — forgery of a document, for instance — the case is resolved under the ordinary rules on concurrence of offenses, precisely because of the backstop nature of Article 257; and where a single course of conduct victimizes multiple people, the provisions on continued/serial offenses may come into play.

## Penalties and Procedure

Active misconduct under the first paragraph carries imprisonment from six months to two years; neglect or delay under the second paragraph carries imprisonment from three months to one year. The offense is, as a rule, investigated and prosecuted ex officio, though the official's public-service status can trigger special permission and authorization procedures before an investigation proceeds. Because the conduct is tied to the exercise of a public function, these cases typically turn on documentary evidence from the relevant administrative file and on witness testimony about how the office in question actually operated, with expert reports and administrative records often forming the evidentiary core of the case. A conviction can also trigger the general consequences under Turkish law regarding disqualification from exercising certain rights, depending on the nature of the act — a consequence aimed at restoring public confidence in the office concerned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If a public official's decision is later annulled by an administrative court, does that automatically mean they committed misconduct in office under Article 257?**
No. Administrative unlawfulness alone is not enough. A conviction additionally requires proof that the decision caused victimization of a person, loss to the public, or an unjust advantage — the criminal case and the administrative annulment are assessed under different standards.

**Can a public official be charged under Article 257 for conduct that also fits a more specific offense, such as bribery or embezzlement?**
No, not under Article 257 for that conduct. Because the article is subsidiary, the more specific offense takes precedence, and the misconduct provision only applies to conduct that does not fall within any other specifically defined crime.

**Does simple carelessness or an honest mistake by a public official amount to misconduct in office?**
No. The offense requires direct or possible intent. Turkish law does not punish a negligent version of this offense, so unintentional errors from carelessness or inexperience remain a matter for administrative or disciplinary proceedings rather than criminal law.

**Is delay by a public official in issuing a decision or ruling enough, by itself, to constitute misconduct in office?**
Not by itself. Delay can fall under the neglect/delay form of the offense, but a conviction still requires proof that the delay caused victimization, public loss, or unjust gain — victimization can include harm such as being unable to obtain a timely legal outcome, but it must still be established on the facts.
