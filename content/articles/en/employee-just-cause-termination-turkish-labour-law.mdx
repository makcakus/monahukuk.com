Turkish employment relationships are built on an ongoing exchange of trust between employer and employee, and the Turkish Labour Code (Law No. 4857) recognises that this trust can break down badly enough to justify ending the contract on the spot. Article 24 gives the employee — regardless of whether the contract is for a fixed or indefinite term — the right to walk away immediately, without waiting out a notice period, whenever the employer's conduct falls into one of a closed list of categories: danger to health, breaches of wages or dignity, and certain force-majeure situations. Because the legislator treats the employee as the structurally weaker party, exercising this right does not cost the employee their severance pay or other statutory claims; it is treated as if the employer itself had dismissed them. For foreign employers operating in Turkey, understanding exactly which situations trigger this right — and how quickly an employee must act on it — is essential to managing termination risk correctly.

## Termination for Health Reasons

The first category under Article 24 covers situations where continuing to perform the job has become dangerous to the employee's health or life. This is not about risks that were already known and accepted when the job was taken; it is about a new, concrete danger that develops during the employment relationship and makes continued performance unsafe. A second, related situation arises where a person the employee is in constant, close and direct contact with at work — whether the employer or a co-worker — contracts a contagious illness, or an illness incompatible with the nature of the work. In that scenario the law is not protecting the employee's own health directly, but their legitimate interest in not being forced to remain in contact with the risk. In both cases, Turkish courts require the danger to be real and specific, not a vague or speculative worry.

## Non-Payment of Wages as Just Cause

In practice, the most frequently invoked ground under Article 24 is the employer's failure to calculate or pay wages in accordance with the law or the contract. The Turkish Court of Appeals has consistently held that "wages" in this context must be read broadly: bonuses, premiums, fuel and clothing allowances, overtime pay, and weekly and public holiday pay are all wage-related entitlements, and withholding any of them can support a just-cause termination. It does not matter that the employer is in financial difficulty, or that part of the unpaid amount could theoretically be recovered from the state wage guarantee fund — the law protects the employee's right to be paid in full and on time, not the source of the payment. The same logic extends to social security contributions: failing to pay them, underpaying them, or declaring a wage lower than what the employee actually earns has repeatedly been treated by the courts as just cause, even though this touches the employee's social security standing rather than their take-home pay. A closely related ground is the failure to apply agreed "working conditions" — Turkish case law has treated underreporting of the insurance base salary as a breach of working conditions in its own right, and has recognised this type of breach as a continuing violation that is not cut off by the short time limits discussed below.

## Other Employer Conduct Contrary to Good Faith and Morality

Beyond wage issues, Article 24 also covers a cluster of situations centred on the employer's personal conduct: misleading the employee about essential terms when the contract was formed; words or behaviour that offend the honour or dignity of the employee or their family; sexual harassment by the employer; assault, threats or incitement to unlawful conduct directed at the employee or their family; and — importantly — a failure by the employer to take action after being notified that the employee was sexually harassed by a co-worker or a third party at the workplace. These grounds exist to protect the employee's personality rights and human dignity, and they mirror the employer's own right to terminate for cause under Article 25 where the employee behaves in a comparably dishonest or disloyal way. The system is designed to run in both directions: whichever side breaches the basic duty of good faith gives the other an immediate exit.

## Termination Due to Compelling Reasons

A separate, narrower ground applies where a compelling reason outside the control of either party — a natural disaster, an epidemic or similar event — makes it objectively impossible to keep working, and this state of affairs causes work at the employee's workplace to stop for more than a week. The employee cannot invoke this right before that one-week period has actually elapsed; the clock starts running from the date work factually stopped, not from the date the triggering event occurred.

## The Time Limits for Exercising the Right

Article 26 imposes strict time limits on the "good faith and morality" grounds described above (though notably not on the health or force-majeure grounds, which are not subject to these deadlines). The employee — or the employer, since Article 26 applies symmetrically — loses the right to terminate for cause if more than six working days pass from the day the breach became known, or in any case more than one year from the date the conduct occurred. The one-year limit does not apply where the employee derived a material benefit from the incident — an exception the statute ties specifically to the employee, not to whichever party is terminating. Turkish courts treat these two periods as independent and mandatory: expiry of either one extinguishes the right to terminate for cause, and a judge must take this into account even if neither party raises it. The important exception is for continuing breaches, such as ongoing non-payment of wages: because the violation persists day after day, the time limit never starts running, and the employee may invoke just-cause termination at any point until payment is actually made.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an employee who terminates for just cause under Article 24 receive severance pay?**
Yes. A termination that genuinely falls within Article 24 is treated the same way as a dismissal by the employer for the purposes of severance pay, provided the employee otherwise meets the seniority requirement. The employee also retains any other compensation claims arising from the employer's breach.

**Can an employee terminate immediately over a single late salary payment?**
It depends on the facts, but Turkish case law reads "wages" broadly and treats non-payment or short payment of wage-related entitlements — including bonuses, overtime, and holiday pay — as sufficient grounds, regardless of the employer's financial difficulties.

**What happens if the employee waits too long before terminating?**
If the ground relied upon falls under the "good faith and morality" category, the right is lost once six working days have passed after the employee learned of the breach, or one year after the breach occurred, whichever applies — unless the breach was a continuing one, such as ongoing unpaid wages.

**Is proof required, or is the employee's word enough?**
The employee bears the burden of demonstrating that a ground under Article 24 genuinely existed. Written termination notices that clearly state the relied-upon ground, together with supporting documentation such as payslips or correspondence, are critical to withstanding a later legal challenge from the employer.
