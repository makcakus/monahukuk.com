TTK Art. 1061-1063 open the Code's chapter on shipowners (donatan) and joint shipping ownership, defining who counts as a shipowner and what liability that status carries.

## A Functional, Not Purely Titular, Definition

Under Art. 1061(1), a "donatan" is a ship's owner who operates it commercially for profit. But Art. 1061(2) goes further, extending the label to anyone who operates *someone else's* ship for profit in their own name — whether personally or through a captain. In practice this captures bareboat charterers above all: a charterer running a vessel under its own commercial organization is treated, vis-à-vis third parties, exactly like the title owner. This functional approach exists because a party dealing with a ship at sea — after a collision, cargo loss, or port accident — has no realistic way to research title; what matters to them is who is actually running the ship. Turkish courts have applied this broadly, recognizing an "implied shipowner" status even for a party formally described as a mere agent, where the underlying facts show it was really operating the vessel. The registered owner is not left exposed to unauthorized use, though: it can block claims by a ship-lien creditor arising from operation that was both unauthorized *and* known to be so by the claimant.

## Liability for Crew Fault

Art. 1062 makes the shipowner liable, on a classic respondeat-superior basis, for damages caused to third parties by the fault of crew members and pilots performing their duties. Toward passengers and cargo interests specifically, though, this general rule steps aside in favor of the more detailed carrier-liability regime found later in the Code's carriage-of-goods provisions — reflecting that those parties already have a contractual relationship with the shipowner. The shipowner's right to limit liability under international conventions Turkey has ratified is expressly preserved.

## An Additional Forum for Claims

Art. 1063 gives claimants a convenient alternative: any claim against a shipowner in that capacity can also be brought in the courts of the ship's home port, in addition to the owner's normal domicile — useful given how often evidence and administrative records cluster there.

## Practical Takeaway

For foreign investors chartering Turkish-flagged or Turkey-based vessels, Art. 1061(2) means charter structuring should anticipate being treated as the operative "shipowner" for liability purposes, regardless of who holds registered title.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is a shipowner; can someone who does not own the vessel be a shipowner?

Under TTK Article 1061/1, a shipowner is <b>the owner of a vessel who uses it on water for the purpose of profit</b>. However, under Article 1061/2, <b>a person who, in their own name, either personally or through a master, uses on water for the purpose of profit a vessel that does not belong to them</b>, is treated as the shipowner in their relations with third parties. In this way, a person who operates a vessel under charter is also subject to the shipowner's liability regime as against third parties. Under the same paragraph, the owner cannot prevent a person claiming a right as a ship creditor from pursuing their claim, unless the operation is unlawful against the owner and the creditor is in bad faith.

### Is the shipowner liable for the fault of the crew?

Yes. Under TTK Article 1062/1, the shipowner is liable for damage caused <b>to third parties</b> as a result of fault committed by <b>the crew, the compulsory advisory pilot, or the optional pilot</b> in the performance of their duties. This is a liability that arises without any need to establish fault on the part of the shipowner personally, and it cannot be avoided by adducing exculpatory evidence. The shipowner's right to limit liability for maritime claims (Article 1328 et seq.) remains reserved.

### Where can an action be brought against a shipowner?

Under TTK Article 1063/1, an action against a shipowner, in respect of any claim arising from that capacity, may <b>also</b> be brought in the court of the place where the vessel's port of registry is located. The word 'also' is significant: this jurisdiction does not exclude the general jurisdiction rules under the Code of Civil Procedure, but establishes an <b>additional</b> optional jurisdiction alongside them. The claimant may choose between the court of the defendant's domicile and the court of the port of registry.
