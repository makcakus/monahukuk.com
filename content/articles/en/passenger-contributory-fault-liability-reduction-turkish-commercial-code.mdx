The passenger-protective regime built around TTK Art. 1256 could, left alone, insulate a passenger entirely from the consequences of their own reckless conduct. Art. 1261 prevents that by giving courts a discretionary tool to rebalance liability when the passenger shares the blame.

## Two Thresholds, One Discretionary Remedy

Under Art. 1261, if the carrier proves that the passenger's own intent or negligence *caused* the death, injury, or baggage loss/damage, or merely *had an effect on it*, the court "may" — not must — decide that the carrier is partly or wholly not liable. The two-tier standard (causing versus contributing) lets the rule flex across the full range of real-world scenarios, from a passenger solely responsible for their own harm to one whose carelessness was just one factor among several. Critically, the burden of proving the passenger's fault sits with the carrier, not the passenger — consistent with the principle that the party closer to the evidence (accident details, safety procedures, crew instructions) should carry that burden.

## Why It Matters: Balancing Art. 1256's Protective Framework

The clearest illustration involves a passenger's duty under Art. 1251 to obey the captain's instructions for order aboard ship. A passenger who ignores a safety instruction — where to sit, where not to stand, which route to take — and is injured as a result gives the carrier grounds to invoke Art. 1261. Since Art. 1256 can also impose a fault presumption on the carrier (particularly for cabin baggage in a shipping incident), a court may in theory need to weigh both a presumed carrier fault and a proven passenger fault in the same case.

## Case Law: Contributory Fault in Practice

A 2025 Court of Cassation decision (11th Civil Chamber, 2025/117-2025/4906) illustrates the mechanism concretely, even though it was decided under general tort law (TBK Art. 49) and TTK Art. 1256 rather than Art. 1261 by name. A passenger drowned crossing from a ferry to a pier by an unsafe route instead of the designated gangway, ignoring announcements. The court apportioned fault — 30% to the off-duty captain, 30% to the deceased passenger, 30% to the carrier, and 10% to the municipality — and reduced the damages award proportionally to the passenger's own share. A related 2023 Criminal Chamber decision reached a parallel result: passengers who stood on stairs against a captain's audible and visual warnings during rough weather were held primarily at fault for the resulting injuries, with the captain only secondarily responsible.

## Practical Takeaway

For carriers, documenting safety announcements and passenger non-compliance is essential to invoking Art. 1261 successfully. For passengers and their heirs, understanding that any own-fault contribution — however partial — can proportionally reduce recovery is equally important when framing a claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the passenger's own fault affect the compensation?

Yes. Under TTK Article 1261/1 <b>if the carrier proves that the passenger's intent or negligence caused, or contributed to, their death, injury or the loss of or damage to their luggage, the court may decide that the carrier is wholly or partly not liable.</b> The provision recognises two thresholds: fault that <b>causes</b> the loss and fault that merely <b>contributes</b> to it.

### Who must prove this fault?

The carrier. The wording of the article is clear: the court may reduce liability <b>if the carrier proves</b> it. This is consistent with Article 1256/1: since the carrier's liability in a shipping incident is strict, the burden of proof falls on the party seeking to lighten it. In practice, safety announcements, the master's instructions, camera recordings and witness statements are the means of that proof.

### Can the court remove liability altogether?

Yes. Article 1261/1 empowers the court to decide that the carrier is <b>'wholly or partly not liable'</b>. Where the passenger's intent or gross negligence is the sole and decisive cause of the loss (for example, entering a closed part of the deck despite a clear prohibition), liability may fall away entirely. Where the fault has merely contributed, the compensation is reduced proportionately.
