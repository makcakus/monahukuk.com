Foreign nationals and their families are often unfamiliar with how the first hours after an arrest in Turkey unfold, and the terminology can be confusing: an "arrest" (yakalama) and "police custody" (gözaltı) are two distinct legal stages under the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (CMK), each with its own conditions and, crucially, its own strict time limits. Unlike pre-trial detention ordered by a judge, arrest and custody are short, on-the-spot measures applied before any judicial decision is made. Because they restrict personal liberty without prior judicial review, the law fixes their duration almost to the minute, and any deviation from those limits can expose the state to a compensation claim. This article explains, in plain terms, who may make an arrest, when custody may follow, how long it may last, and what rights a detained person — including a foreign national — is entitled to during this process.

## Arrest by Ordinary Citizens and Arrest by Law Enforcement

Turkish law distinguishes between two very different sources of arrest authority. The first is genuinely exceptional: under Article 90 of the CMK, any private individual — not just a police officer — may briefly apprehend someone caught in the act of committing an offense, or someone being pursued immediately after a flagrant offense where there is a risk of flight or no immediate way to establish identity. This "citizen's arrest" power is narrowly confined to the moment of the act or the immediate pursuit that follows it; it does not authorize a private person to seize someone over an offense allegedly committed in the past. Law enforcement officers have a broader arrest power: where a situation calls for a detention order or an arrest warrant and any delay would be prejudicial, and there is no time to contact a prosecutor or a superior, police may arrest on their own initiative. As a general rule, arrest is not available for offenses that can only be prosecuted upon a private complaint, but an exception applies to flagrant offenses committed against children or against people who cannot protect themselves because of illness, disability or infirmity — in those cases, an arrest may proceed without waiting for the victim's complaint.

## Conditions for Custody and the 24-Hour Limit

If the prosecutor's office does not release a person who has been arrested, custody may be ordered so the investigation can be completed. This is not left to the prosecutor's discretion: Article 91 requires two conditions to be satisfied at the same time — custody must be necessary for the investigation, and there must be concrete evidence indicating that the person committed an offense. A 2014 legislative change deliberately raised this threshold, replacing a looser standard based on mere "indications" with a requirement of concrete evidence; a custody decision resting on hunch or assumption no longer meets the legal standard. The time limit is strict: custody cannot exceed twenty-four hours from the moment of arrest, excluding the time genuinely needed to bring the person before the nearest judge or court, and that transfer time itself cannot exceed twelve hours. The twenty-four-hour clock starts running at the moment of the actual arrest — not when the person is logged in at a police station, a distinction that matters in practice because custody periods are sometimes miscalculated from the later point.

## Extensions for Collective Offenses and Flagrant Serious Crimes

The general 24-hour rule allows for two narrow extensions. The first applies to offenses committed collectively, where evidence-gathering is difficult or there are many suspects: the prosecutor may issue a written order extending custody by up to three days total, in increments of no more than one day at a time, and this extension order must be communicated to the detained person immediately. The second is a special regime, added in 2015, that applies only to flagrant (in-the-act) offenses — violent offenses committed during public disturbances, and a defined list of serious crimes under the Turkish Penal Code such as intentional homicide, intentional injury, sexual assault, sexual abuse of children, theft, robbery, and drug trafficking, among others. In these cases, senior law enforcement officers designated by the local governor may order custody of up to twenty-four hours, extended to forty-eight hours during public-order disturbances involving widespread violence. Even under this exceptional regime, the person must be brought before a judge within forty-eight hours, or within four days for collective offenses, and the prosecutor must be informed immediately once the grounds for custody disappear or the processing is complete.

## Arrest Warrants

Separate from the on-the-spot arrests described above, an arrest warrant is a written order issued in advance, typically used when a suspect fails to respond to a summons or cannot be reached. During the investigation stage, the warrant is issued by a magistrate judge (sulh ceza hâkimi) at the prosecutor's request; during trial, the court itself may issue it, either on its own initiative or at the prosecutor's request. Prosecutors and police may also issue a warrant for a suspect who escapes from custody or a convicted person who escapes from a detention facility. The content of a valid warrant is fixed by law: it must describe the person's physical characteristics, identity if known, the offense charged, and where the person is to be taken once apprehended — a warrant missing these elements cannot be executed.

## Notification of Relatives and Appearance Before a Judge

Notifying a detained person's relatives is one of the most basic safeguards against arbitrary or unrecorded detention: when a person is arrested, placed in custody, or has their custody extended, the prosecutor must ensure that a relative or another person designated by the detainee is informed without delay, and where the detainee is a foreign national, the relevant consulate must also be notified unless the person objects in writing. Where the arrest follows a judicial warrant, a separate 24-hour rule under Article 94 requires the person to be brought before the competent judge or court within that time; if that is not possible, a 2014 reform allows the interrogation to be conducted remotely through the national audio-video communication system (SEGBİS) at the nearest courthouse. A person arrested outside working hours solely for the purpose of taking a statement may also be released on a written undertaking to appear on a set date — a relatively recent mechanism aimed at reducing unnecessary custody.

## Consequences of Unlawful Arrest or Custody

Article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights, as interpreted by the European Court of Human Rights, tests the lawfulness of any arrest or custody against three criteria: a legal basis for the interference, a concrete and reasonable suspicion, and proportionality in duration. Turkish law incorporates these guarantees domestically through the strict time limits of Articles 90-91, but exceeding those limits, miscalculating the custody period, or failing to inform the detainee of their rights can give rise to a compensation claim under Article 141 of the CMK, which governs claims for damages caused by protective measures. In practice, the most common disputes involve an incorrect starting point for the custody period, a magistrate's failure to rule promptly on an objection, and a failure to clearly explain the reason for the arrest to the person concerned. Once the case moves to the interrogation stage, the suspect's defense rights and access to legal representation become a separate and broader set of protections.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a private citizen in Turkey legally detain someone?**
Yes, but only in narrow circumstances. Under Article 90, anyone may briefly detain a person caught committing an offense, or someone fleeing immediately after a flagrant offense when identity cannot be established on the spot. This power does not extend to detaining someone over a past, non-flagrant offense.

**How long can the police hold someone in custody before release or referral to a judge?**
As a general rule, no more than twenty-four hours from the moment of arrest, excluding the time reasonably needed to transfer the person to the nearest judge or court (capped at twelve hours). Custody for collective offenses can be extended to a maximum of three days by written prosecutorial order, and a narrower category of flagrant serious offenses allows up to forty-eight hours, with mandatory appearance before a judge within forty-eight hours (four days for collective offenses).

**If I am a foreign national arrested in Turkey, will my embassy or consulate be told?**
Under Article 95 of the CMK, the authorities must notify the consulate of a detained foreign national's home country, unless the detainee objects in writing. A relative or a person the detainee designates must also be informed without delay.

**What can I do if I believe custody time limits were violated?**
Exceeding the statutory time limits, failing to inform the detainee of their rights, or omitting the required notification to relatives can support a compensation claim under Article 141 of the CMK for damages arising from protective measures. The detainee, their lawyer, or close family members can also apply to a magistrate judge to challenge the custody order itself while it is ongoing.
