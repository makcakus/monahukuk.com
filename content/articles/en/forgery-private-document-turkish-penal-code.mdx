Foreign businesses operating in Turkey frequently rely on private paperwork — invoices, receipts, contracts, letters, promissory notes — that never passes through a public official or notarial procedure. When such a document is fabricated, altered, or knowingly put to use in its falsified form, Turkish criminal law addresses it through Article 207 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), a provision distinct from the offense covering official documents. A closely related but conceptually different risk arises when someone hands over a page bearing a genuine signature but left blank or partially blank, intending it to be completed on agreed terms, only to have it filled in with different content — a scenario governed by Article 209. Understanding where these rules diverge, what triggers criminal liability, and how the complaint procedure differs is essential for any party relying on privately executed paperwork in Turkey.

## What the Law Protects and Why "Private" Documents Are Treated Differently

The forgery offenses under the TCK sit within the chapter protecting public trust, and the interest safeguarded by Article 207 is society's confidence in the authenticity and content of documents used as evidence in dealings between private parties. The dividing line from the offense covering official documents (Article 204) is not the paper's subject matter but who produced it and how: an official document is one drawn up by a public official acting within an official procedure, while a private document is any written instrument capable of proving a fact or a legal relationship between individuals, produced outside that official channel. Because a private document does not carry the presumption of reliability attached to something issued through state procedure, the legislature has set a comparatively lighter penalty for tampering with it. The category is read broadly in practice: promissory notes, contracts, invoices, receipts, letters and similar written instruments all qualify, provided they are capable of proving a legal relationship or fact and were not produced before an official authority.

## The Two Ways to Commit the Offense, and Why Use Matters

Article 207 criminalizes two alternative acts: fabricating a private document from scratch so that it appears genuine, or altering a genuine private document in a way designed to mislead others. Critically, the provision does not stop there — it also requires that the falsified or altered document actually be used. Fabrication or alteration alone, without any subsequent use, does not complete the offense as defined; the act only becomes criminally complete once the document is put into circulation or relied upon. This differs meaningfully from the offense involving official documents, where the moment of completion is analyzed differently. The second paragraph of Article 207 extends liability further: a person who knowingly uses a forged private document — even someone who played no part in creating or altering it — is punished under the same terms. Use is therefore treated as an independent alternative act capable of generating liability on its own, separate from the act of fabrication.

Anyone can commit this offense; no special status or capacity is required of the offender. The party harmed is either the person who suffers a loss from the document's impaired evidentiary value, or, more broadly, the public's general trust in documentary evidence. No separate showing of actual damage is required — the offense is a danger crime that is complete once a document capable of deceiving is put to use.

## The Deception Standard That Applies Across the Offense

Whether the document was freshly fabricated or altered from a genuine original, Article 207 requires that it be capable, in its actual manner of use and in the context of the transaction involved, of misleading a reasonably attentive observer. A document whose falsity is obvious on its face — one that could not realistically deceive anyone — falls outside the scope of the offense. This deception threshold applies uniformly to fabrication, alteration, and use, and is assessed case by case according to the concrete circumstances in which the document was presented.

## Destroying, Damaging or Concealing a Document: A Different Kind of Attack

Article 208 addresses an entirely different way of undermining confidence in private documents. Here the content of the document is neither invented nor changed — instead, the physical or legal existence of a genuine private document is eliminated. Damaging the document renders it unusable; destroying it eliminates it outright; concealing it prevents its production or retrieval by the person entitled to it. The value protected remains the reliability of documentary evidence, but the point of attack shifts from the document's authenticity to its very availability. Article 208 is therefore a distinct offense from forgery, targeting the practical elimination of a party's ability to produce or rely on evidence that genuinely exists.

## Misuse of a Blank Signed Page: Article 209 and the Betrayal of a Narrower Trust

Article 209(1) protects a different and more personal kind of trust. Here, the offender has been handed a page that already carries a genuine, voluntarily given signature — left entirely or partially blank — with the understanding that it will be completed in a specific, agreed way. At the moment it changes hands, the document is not forged: the signature is authentic and given with full consent. The offense arises when the person entrusted with the page fills it in with content that departs from what was agreed, thereby betraying the purpose for which it was handed over. Because the wrongdoing lies in exceeding an entrusted authority rather than fabricating something from nothing, this offense sits doctrinally close to breach of trust under Article 155 of the Penal Code — both punish someone for turning a power or confidence placed in them to an unintended use. Article 209 is treated as the more specific rule within the forgery chapter precisely because the misuse here takes the form of completing a document with legal consequences, rather than a general misappropriation of property or value.

Article 209(2) addresses a materially different situation: a signed blank page that was unlawfully obtained or is unlawfully held by someone who then completes it in a way that produces legal consequences. Because no relationship of trust exists between that person and the original signatory, the legislature has removed this scenario from the lighter regime of Article 209(1) and instead directs that it be punished under the ordinary forgery provisions — Article 204 or Article 207, depending on whether the resulting document is treated as official or private. The same physical object, a signed blank page, is thus subject to two entirely different penalty regimes depending solely on whether a relationship of trust existed between the parties.

## Intent, Complaint Procedure, Attempt and Concurrence

All three offenses discussed here require intent; Turkish law does not recognize a negligent version of document forgery. For Article 207, the offender must act with awareness that using or causing the document to be used will mislead others — a further motive such as obtaining a benefit is not required. Under Article 209(1), the offender must knowingly complete the page in a manner contrary to the signatory's actual instructions; this awareness is what separates the offense from an innocent mistake or misunderstanding about what was agreed.

The complaint regime differs sharply across these provisions. Article 207 and Article 208 are prosecuted automatically by the public prosecutor, without any complaint requirement. Article 209(1), by contrast, is explicitly a complaint-based offense — reflecting the view that misuse of a blank signed page is fundamentally a dispute of personal trust between the signatory and the person entrusted with the page, rather than an affront to public confidence generally. If the injured party does not file a complaint within the applicable statutory period, prosecution becomes barred. Article 209(2) breaks from this exception and follows the prosecution regime attached to whichever forgery provision it redirects to — meaning automatic prosecution, since it defers to Articles 204 or 207.

Because the Article 207 offense is only completed upon use, a document that has been fabricated but not yet used can raise questions about attempt, given that the underlying conduct is divisible into separate steps. The same is true for Articles 208 and 209 where the conduct can similarly be broken down into distinct stages. General rules on complicity apply without modification; because a person who knowingly uses a forged document can be held liable through an alternative act independent of the person who created it, instigation and aiding must be assessed separately for each participant. Where a forged private document served as the tool for committing a separate offense, the specific facts may call for cumulative punishment under the general concurrence rules, since document forgery carries its own distinct wrongfulness apart from whatever other offense it facilitated. Whether using the same forged document against multiple different people generates a separate offense for each use is likewise a fact-specific question.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is simply creating a fake private document already a crime, even before anyone uses it?**
Under Article 207, fabrication or alteration alone does not complete the offense — the provision requires that the document actually be used. Depending on how far the preparatory conduct has progressed, this can raise attempt-related questions, but liability for the completed offense turns on actual use.

**Can someone be prosecuted for using a forged document they did not create themselves?**
Yes. Article 207(2) makes knowing use of a forged private document a basis for liability in its own right, independent of who fabricated or altered it.

**Why does misuse of a blank signed page get treated more leniently than ordinary forgery?**
Because the signature itself is genuine and was voluntarily given — the wrongdoing lies in exceeding the agreed purpose for completing the page, a breach of a personal trust relationship rather than an attack on public confidence generally. This is also why Article 209(1) requires a complaint, unlike Article 207 or 208.

**Does it matter whether the document that was destroyed or concealed, rather than forged, was official or private?**
Article 208 applies regardless of that distinction in terms of its own conduct — what matters is that a genuine document's physical or legal existence, not its content, has been eliminated or hidden, which is a different kind of attack on evidentiary reliability than fabricating or altering a document's content.
