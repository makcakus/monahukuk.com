A witness statement or a sworn declaration is only as useful to a Turkish court as it is truthful, and the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) devotes a cluster of provisions — Articles 272 through 275 — to punishing false testimony and false oaths precisely because the reliability of the fact-finding process depends on it. These offenses sit in the code's chapter on crimes against the administration of justice, meaning the law treats the harm as directed first at the judicial system itself, and only secondarily at any individual who is wrongly implicated or disadvantaged by the false statement. For foreign parties, witnesses, or counsel encountering a Turkish criminal or civil proceeding, understanding how these articles are structured — and how differently the law treats a witness who lies from a mistaken witness, or an early confession from a late one — can materially affect both exposure and defense strategy.

## What the Law Protects and Who Can Be Affected

The rationale behind Articles 272-275 is straightforward: investigating authorities and courts largely rely on what witnesses and sworn parties say, and a knowingly false statement corrupts that evidentiary foundation regardless of whether anyone is ultimately harmed by it. This is why Turkish law frames the primary victim as the judicial process itself rather than a specific person. That said, a person who is investigated, prosecuted, detained, or convicted as a direct result of someone else's false testimony is also recognized as affected by the offense, and the statute scales the penalty according to the severity of what that person actually suffered. The result is a two-layered protective scheme: one that safeguards the integrity of fact-finding as a public interest, and one that responds proportionally to the concrete damage inflicted on an identifiable individual.

## Perjury by a Witness (Article 272)

Article 272 punishes a person who gives false testimony while formally being heard as a witness, and it grades the penalty by reference to the forum in which that testimony is given and to what happens to the person the testimony was directed against. Testimony given to an investigating authority (before charges reach a court) carries the lightest penalty range; testimony given before a court, or given after having been placed under oath, is punished more heavily; and testimony given in the investigation or prosecution of an offense that itself carries a sentence of more than three years' imprisonment is punished more heavily still. Beyond these baseline tiers, the article layers on a series of aggravating circumstances tied to the actual consequences suffered by the person the false testimony targeted: if a protective measure short of detention or arrest was imposed on that person and they were later acquitted or the case against them was discontinued, the applicable penalty is increased; if that person was actually detained or arrested under the same circumstances, the false witness additionally faces liability as an indirect perpetrator of unlawful deprivation of liberty; and if the targeted person was convicted of aggravated life imprisonment or life imprisonment, the false witness faces a substantially higher prison term, increased further still if enforcement of that sentence had already begun. Where the consequence for the targeted person was a judicial or administrative sanction other than imprisonment, a separate, elevated penalty range applies. Taken together, this structure reflects a clear legislative design: the more severe and irreversible the harm caused by the lie, the heavier the punishment for telling it.

## The Mental Element: Knowing Falsity, Not Mere Error

Perjury under Turkish law requires direct intent — the witness must know that what they are saying is untrue and choose to say it anyway. A witness who gives an inaccurate account because of forgetfulness, a mistaken perception, or a genuine misremembering of events has not committed perjury, however wrong their statement turns out to be, because the falsity that the law targets is a *subjective* one: knowingly misrepresenting what the witness actually believes to be true. There is no negligent or reckless version of this offense — the code contains no provision punishing careless false testimony. In practice, this makes perjury cases turn heavily on proof of the witness's state of mind, which prosecutors typically have to establish indirectly, by comparing the witness's account against the rest of the case file and other evidence to show the statement could not plausibly reflect an honest mistake.

## Reduced Penalty or No Penalty for Testimony Involving Close Relatives (Article 273)

Turkish criminal procedure recognizes a witness's right to decline to testify where doing so could incriminate the witness or certain close relatives, and Article 273 gives that procedural right a substantive counterpart in the perjury framework itself. Where a person gives false testimony concerning a matter that could expose themselves, an ascendant, a descendant, a spouse, or a sibling to investigation or prosecution — or gives false testimony because they held the right to decline to testify but were not reminded of that right before testifying — the court may reduce the sentence or may forgo imposing any penalty at all. The policy behind this leniency is an acknowledgment that placing a person in the position of either incriminating a close relative or lying carries a level of moral pressure the law is willing to excuse, at least in part. This leniency is deliberately narrowed, however: it does not apply to false testimony given in private-law disputes, since the balance of interests and the weight of family loyalty are viewed differently outside the criminal context.

## Effective Remorse: Rewarding an Early Retraction (Article 274)

Article 274 builds in a graduated incentive for a witness who initially lies but later corrects the record, with the size of the benefit depending on how early the correction comes. If the truth is told before any decision restricting or depriving the targeted person of a right has been issued, no penalty is imposed at all. If the truth comes out after such a decision but before the final judgment, the sentence may be reduced by between one-half and two-thirds. If it comes out after conviction but before that conviction becomes final, the reduction available is smaller — between one-third and one-half. The logic is proportional: the earlier the retraction, the more damage it prevents, and the more generously the law rewards it. This structure gives defense counsel in a perjury case a genuinely practical lever — timing a client's retraction correctly can be the difference between full exposure and no penalty whatsoever.

## False Oath in Civil Proceedings (Article 275)

Article 275 addresses a distinct scenario confined to civil litigation: a plaintiff or defendant who swears a false oath in the course of a civil case faces imprisonment of one to five years. The person punished here is not a witness but the litigant personally, which is what separates this offense from Article 272. The article carries its own effective-remorse mechanism, modeled on the same logic as Article 274: telling the truth before judgment is rendered eliminates the penalty entirely, while telling the truth after judgment but before it becomes final or is enforced results in a fifty percent reduction.

## Procedural Notes and Related Conduct

Perjury is treated as an offense completed by a single act — the false statement itself — so questions about an attempt that is interrupted before the statement is finished arise mostly in theory rather than in practice. Encouraging or assisting someone else to give false testimony is assessed under Turkey's general rules on complicity rather than under a separate offense. Perjury is prosecuted automatically once discovered; it is not conditional on a private complaint, and depending on the gravity of the underlying case, jurisdiction may sit with either a court of first instance or a heavy penal court. Two further practical points matter for anyone assessing exposure: where the same witness testifies in more than one proceeding, each statement is assessed independently, since each case carries its own distinct legal interest; and false reports or translations given by expert witnesses or court interpreters are governed by separate provisions of the Penal Code, not by Article 272, so correctly identifying the capacity in which a person spoke is essential to knowing which offense — if any — actually applies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a witness be punished for perjury if they honestly misremembered the facts?**
No. Perjury requires knowing that the statement is false at the time it is made. An inaccurate statement caused by forgetfulness, misperception, or an honest mistake does not satisfy the mental element the offense requires, even though the statement is objectively wrong.

**Does retracting a false statement always eliminate the penalty?**
Not always, but it always helps. The benefit depends on timing: a retraction before any adverse decision against the affected person wipes out the penalty entirely; a later retraction, made before the judgment becomes final, still earns a substantial but smaller reduction.

**What happens if a witness lies to protect a close family member from prosecution?**
The court has discretion to reduce the sentence, or to impose no penalty at all, where the false testimony concerned exposing the witness themselves or a close relative (ascendant, descendant, spouse, or sibling) to investigation or prosecution — but this leniency does not extend to false testimony given in civil disputes.

**Is lying under oath in a civil lawsuit the same offense as perjury by a witness?**
No. Article 272 punishes witnesses who give false testimony in an investigation or prosecution, while Article 275 punishes a plaintiff or defendant who personally swears a false oath in a civil case. The two provisions apply to different actors in different types of proceedings, although both include a version of the effective-remorse discount for an early retraction.
