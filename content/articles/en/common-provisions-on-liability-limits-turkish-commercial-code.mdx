TTK Art. 1264 closes out the numeric side of the passenger-liability regime with two short but practically important rules: how the Art. 1262/1263 caps can be raised, and what those caps actually measure.

## Raising the Caps: Express and Written, One-Way Only

Art. 1264(1) lets carrier and passenger agree on higher liability limits than those set in Art. 1262 and 1263 — but only if they do so *expressly* and *in writing*. This is a heightened formality compared to the equivalent cargo-context rule (Art. 1186(8)), and it exists for a reason: raising the cap increases the carrier's exposure, so the law requires unambiguous, documented consent rather than an implied or oral understanding. As with the cargo-context pattern, this is a one-way ratchet — the statutory caps function as a floor, and parties cannot contract *below* them.

This also resolves an earlier open question in the series: Art. 1260's reference to "unless a higher limit is agreed under Art. 1264(1)" for entrusted valuables now has a concrete answer — such an agreement is valid only if made expressly and in writing.

## What the Caps Do Not Include

Art. 1264(2) clarifies that the Art. 1262 and 1263 caps do not include interest or litigation costs. This matters in practice: complex bodily-injury cases can take years to resolve, and if accrued interest and court costs counted against the cap, a slow-moving case would paradoxically punish the claimant by eating into an already-fixed recovery — creating a perverse incentive favoring delay. The caps measure only the principal amount of the loss; interest and litigation expenses run separately, under their own rules.

## Practical Takeaway

Carriers relying on the Art. 1262/1263 caps should confirm no informal understanding with a passenger could be construed as raising those limits — only a written, express agreement does so. Passengers and their counsel should factor in that interest and costs are recoverable on top of any capped principal amount, not absorbed within it.

A 2026 Istanbul Regional Court of Appeal decision (12th Civil Chamber, 2026/420-2026/757, not yet final) references Art. 1264(1) only indirectly, confirming it falls within the scope of Art. 1267's forfeiture rule without applying it to a contractually raised limit in that case.
