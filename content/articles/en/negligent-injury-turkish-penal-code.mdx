Foreign nationals involved in a traffic accident, a workplace incident, or a medical mishap in Turkey are often surprised to learn that the person who caused their injury may face criminal liability even though nobody intended to hurt anyone. Article 89 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") criminalizes bodily injury caused through carelessness rather than through intent, and it is one of the most frequently applied provisions in Turkish criminal practice because it covers exactly this kind of everyday harm — a driver who misjudges a lane change, an employer who skips a safety measure, a doctor who deviates from a treatment protocol. Unlike most criminal offenses, negligent injury also carries a procedural feature that can decide the outcome of a case before the facts are even argued: in most circumstances, the case can only proceed if the injured person files a formal complaint, and that complaint must be filed within a strict time limit. This article explains how the offense is structured, what raises its severity, and how the complaint requirement works in practice.

## What the Law Protects and Why Intent Does Not Matter

Article 89 protects the same interest as intentional-injury offenses — a person's bodily integrity and physical or mental health — but it reaches a different kind of wrongdoing. The person who causes the injury has not chosen to hurt anyone, nor accepted that risk as the price of achieving some other goal. What triggers liability is the failure to exercise the standard of care that social life reasonably expects, in a situation where that failure foreseeably results in someone getting hurt. This is why the offense matters so much in areas of everyday risk — road traffic, industrial work, healthcare — where some risk is inevitable and lawful, but liability attaches once a person crosses the line from an acceptable risk into carelessness that a reasonable person in the same position would have avoided.

## Who Can Be Liable and Who Can Be a Victim

Any person can commit this offense; the law does not limit it to people in a particular profession or role. That said, a person's occupation or position directly shapes how much care the law expects of them. A driver is judged by traffic rules and driving practice, an employer by occupational safety regulations, a doctor by accepted medical standards, and a construction supervisor by building and safety codes. This professional or positional status does not change whether the offense exists — it changes how serious the departure from the expected standard of care is considered to be.

Anyone can be a victim as well, with one structural exception: a person who injures only themselves does not commit this offense, since the law is concerned with harm to others. Where a single act of carelessness injures several people at once, the law treats that as a distinct scenario with its own sentencing range, discussed below. Harm to an unborn child of a pregnant victim is treated separately too, as one of the aggravating outcomes described in the next section.

## Establishing the Offense: Conduct, Result, and Causation

Negligent injury is what Turkish criminal theory calls a "result crime" with no fixed form of conduct — the law does not specify a particular act; it simply requires that a careless act or omission actually cause pain to another person, or impair their health or their sense perception. The careless conduct can be an active act, such as an unsafe overtaking maneuver, or a failure to do something the person was legally required to do, such as an employer's failure to install a mandated safety guard.

The injury must actually occur — without a real physical or psychological result, there is no completed offense. There must also be a genuine causal link between the careless conduct and that result, and the result must be one that could reasonably be attributed to the person's conduct. Foreseeability is the key test here: an unusual, unforeseeable chain of events that goes beyond what the person could reasonably have anticipated in the circumstances is not attributed to them. The fact that the victim or a third party also behaved carelessly does not, as a general rule, break the chain of causation entirely, though shared fault is taken into account in assessing each person's degree of responsibility.

## Basic Fault Standard and the Distinction Between Simple and Conscious Negligence

The mental element of this offense is negligence, defined in Article 22 of the Penal Code as acting in violation of a duty of care and attention, such that the result described in the offense occurs without having been foreseen. Turkish law distinguishes two forms of this. In ordinary (simple) negligence, the person did not foresee the harmful outcome at all, even though a reasonably careful person in their position would have. In conscious negligence, the person actually foresaw that the harmful outcome could occur, but did not want it, and proceeded anyway trusting — wrongly — that it would not materialize. This distinction is not academic: conscious negligence increases the sentence within the range set for negligent offenses, and, as explained below, it can also remove the complaint requirement for more serious injuries.

Because liability for negligence tracks personal fault, when several people each act carelessly and jointly contribute to the same accident, each is assessed and sentenced according to their own individual degree of fault, not as a collective. Turkish law also allows a court to forgo imposing any sentence at all where the negligent act has left the person who caused it, given their personal and family circumstances, so severely affected that punishing them further would serve no purpose — though this leniency is not available where the offender acted with conscious negligence.

## Aggravated Outcomes and Injury to Multiple Victims

The base offense covers ordinary pain or ordinary impairment of health or perception. Where the injury is more serious, the law increases the sentence in tiers tied to specific medical outcomes, broadly mirroring the aggravating-outcome structure used for intentional injury under Article 87, with one notable difference: a bone fracture is placed in the lower aggravating tier here rather than treated separately.

The first, less severe tier — carrying a sentence increased by half over the base range — covers outcomes such as permanent weakening of a sense or organ function, a bone fracture, a lasting speech impediment, a permanent mark on the face, a condition that endangers life, or premature birth caused to a pregnant victim's child.

The second, more severe tier — carrying a doubled sentence — covers outcomes such as an incurable illness or a vegetative state, complete loss of a sense or organ function, loss of the ability to speak or to procreate, permanent disfigurement of the face, or the loss of a pregnant victim's unborn child.

Where a single careless act injures more than one person, the law does not simply multiply the sentence by the number of victims; instead it sets an independent sentencing range of nine months to five years' imprisonment, with no option for a monetary fine — unlike the base offense, which allows a fine as an alternative to imprisonment.

## Attempt, Complicity, and How the Offense Combines With Other Crimes

Because a negligent offense requires no intent toward the result, it cannot be attempted: if the injury never actually occurs, there is no offense to speak of, since attempt requires acting toward a result the person actually wants to bring about. Complicity in the sense of instigating or knowingly assisting another person's crime is likewise unavailable, because Turkish law's rules on participation require an intentional, unlawful underlying act; a negligent act does not qualify. Where several people each contribute carelessly to the same harmful result, each is held liable as an independent principal to the extent of their own fault, rather than as accomplices to one another's conduct.

Turkish law also does not treat a single incident that injures several people as multiple separate crimes to be counted individually — the dedicated sentencing range for injury to multiple victims applies instead. The rules on continuing offenses (repeated acts carried out in execution of a single criminal decision) do not apply to negligent crimes at all, since they presuppose a plan the offender intended to carry out. Where a single accident causes both a death and an injury, Turkish courts resolve this by applying the negligent-homicide provision (Article 85) as a unified sentence rather than sentencing the death and the injury separately.

## The Complaint Requirement: The Rule and Its Exception

The feature of Article 89 most likely to determine how a case unfolds is its complaint requirement. As a rule, investigation and prosecution of negligent injury depend on the injured party filing a complaint — without one, the authorities will not pursue the matter, and if a case is opened anyway and no valid complaint follows, it will be discontinued. There is a narrow exception: the complaint requirement falls away only where two conditions hold together — the offender acted with conscious negligence, and the injury goes beyond the basic, first-tier harm covered by the base offense. In other words, ordinary (non-aggravated) injuries always require a complaint, even where the offender is found to have acted with conscious negligence; only aggravated injuries (the tiers described above, including the multiple-victim scenario) caused through conscious negligence are prosecuted regardless of any complaint.

Where a complaint is required, the injured person must file it within six months of learning both the act and the identity of the person responsible. Turkish courts treat this deadline strictly, and it runs from actual knowledge rather than from the date of the accident itself. Withdrawing a valid complaint leads to a decision not to prosecute if made before charges are filed, or to a discontinuance of the case if made afterward, though a withdrawal generally does not bind a defendant who does not accept it. Because the six-month period, the conscious-negligence exception, and the scope of "basic" injury frequently intersect in the same case — particularly where a victim delays before deciding to pursue the matter — getting the complaint analysis right is often the single most consequential step in a negligent injury file.

## Sentencing, Settlement, and the Competent Court

In its basic form, the offense carries imprisonment of four months to two years or a judicial fine, with the court choosing between the two. The first aggravating tier increases that sentence by half; the second aggravating tier doubles it. Where multiple victims are injured, the independent range of nine months to five years' imprisonment applies instead, without a fine option. Within these ranges, the degree of fault — whether the offender's negligence was the primary or a contributing cause of the accident, and whether the victim shares some responsibility — plays a decisive role in the sentence actually imposed.

Jurisdiction over negligent injury cases lies with the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi). Because the offense generally requires a complaint, it also falls within the mandatory mediation (uzlaştırma) procedure under the Criminal Procedure Code, meaning the file must be referred to a mediation bureau at the investigation stage before proceeding further. A mediation reached during the investigation stage, once its terms are carried out, leads to a decision not to prosecute; a mediation reached during the trial stage leads to a discontinuance. If mediation fails, the case proceeds and ordinary sentencing tools — such as suspension of the sentence or conversion of a short prison term into an alternative penalty — may become relevant depending on the circumstances. The statute of limitations follows the general rules for criminal offenses, calculated by reference to the maximum sentence available for the specific outcome involved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the victim always have to file a complaint for a negligent injury case to proceed?**
Not always. A complaint is required for the basic (non-aggravated) form of the offense in every case. It is also required for more serious, aggravated injuries unless the offender acted with conscious negligence — in that combination alone, the case can proceed without any complaint from the victim.

**How long does a victim have to file a complaint?**
Six months from the date the victim learns both what happened and who is responsible for it, not from the date of the accident itself. Turkish courts apply this deadline strictly, and a complaint filed after it has expired will not revive the case.

**Can the responsible party be prosecuted if the victim withdraws the complaint?**
Generally, no. A withdrawal made before charges are filed leads to a decision not to prosecute, and a withdrawal made during trial leads to the case being discontinued, unless a defendant who has not accepted the withdrawal is affected by rules to the contrary.

**Why does a bone fracture count as a lesser aggravating outcome here compared to intentional injury cases?**
Turkish law generally mirrors the aggravating-outcome structure of intentional injury (Article 87) for negligent injury, but with one deliberate difference: a bone fracture is grouped into the lower, half-increase tier for negligent injury rather than any separate or higher category, reflecting the different policy judgment applied to careless as opposed to intentional harm.
