Foreign parents going through a divorce or custody dispute in Turkey are often surprised to learn that a parent who is denied custody and then takes the child from the custodial parent's home can face criminal charges, separate from and in addition to any family-court proceedings over custody itself. Turkish law addresses this scenario, together with two related situations — abusive treatment within a shared household and the failure to meet family-law support obligations — in a single cluster of provisions, Articles 232 through 234 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, TCK). These three articles do not protect the general public against violent crime in the way most of the Penal Code does; instead, they police the internal power imbalances of family and household relationships, criminalizing conduct that would otherwise be difficult to reach through ordinary assault or restraint offenses. This article explains how each provision works, how the offenses are distinguished from one another and from related crimes, and what parents and other affected parties should understand about the practical mechanics of a case.

## What These Provisions Protect and Why They Exist as a Group

Articles 232, 233 and 234 sit together in the Penal Code because they share a common structural feature: each requires a specific relationship of proximity, authority or responsibility between the offender and the victim before the conduct becomes criminal at all. Article 232 addresses mistreatment among people who share a home, or between someone who holds a position of educational or custodial authority over another and that person. Article 233 addresses the practical failure to provide care, education or support that family law otherwise imposes as an abstract duty — most visibly, non-payment of court-ordered alimony or child support. Article 234 addresses a parent, having lost custody, or certain close relatives, taking or keeping a child away from the parent or guardian who currently has lawful care of that child. What unites the three is that ordinary criminal law would either not reach this conduct at all, or would reach it only awkwardly, because the relationship between offender and victim is itself part of what makes the conduct wrongful. Correspondingly, the basic form of each offense carries a relatively modest maximum sentence — none exceeding one year at the base level — with the legislature reserving heavier consequences for aggravated circumstances, such as when force or threats are used to take a child, or when a parent's neglect creates serious danger to a child's health or safety.

## Mistreatment Within a Household or an Authority Relationship (Article 232)

Article 232, paragraph 1, criminalizes mistreatment of a person by someone with whom that person shares a home. The relevant connection is not blood relation but cohabitation: spouses, siblings, a parent and child, or even unrelated housemates can all fall within its scope, provided they live under the same roof. The conduct covered is behavior that does not rise to the level of physical violence but that repeatedly humiliates, isolates or demeans the other household member. Turkish courts have applied this provision, for example, to a parent who habitually sends a young child out alone at night to sell goods on the street, treating this as conduct incompatible with ordinary parental compassion and care, and to a parent who binds a child to a bed to stop them from running away again after an earlier escape attempt — even where the underlying motive is to prevent the child from leaving home. Where the same conduct becomes systematic and inflicts sustained psychological suffering, it can shift into the more serious offense of torment (Article 96); where it causes actual bodily harm, it shifts into intentional injury (Article 86). A separate paragraph within the same article, Article 232(2), addresses a different kind of relationship: someone who has disciplinary authority over a person they are responsible for raising, educating, caring for or training in a trade — for instance a teacher over a student, or a master over an apprentice — and who abuses that disciplinary authority beyond its legitimate limits. Prosecution under either paragraph proceeds without the need for a formal complaint from the victim, and the offense can be committed with either direct intent or the lesser mental state of possible intent.

## Breach of Family-Law Duties: Support Obligations, Abandonment and Endangering a Child (Article 233)

Article 233 addresses a different problem — not abusive behavior as such, but the failure to perform obligations that family law imposes on a person by virtue of their family relationship. The first paragraph penalizes someone who fails to provide the care, education or support they are obligated to provide under family law; in practice, this provision is invoked most often against a person who has stopped paying court-ordered alimony or child support after divorce. Because the offense depends on an underlying legal obligation already having been established — typically by a court decision or a formal agreement — and because it consists of a failure to act rather than an affirmative act, this is treated as an offense of omission, and prosecution requires a complaint from the person affected. The second paragraph covers a narrower and more specific scenario: abandoning a spouse known to be pregnant, or a woman with whom the offender has been living and by whom the offender knows they have fathered a child outside of marriage, in a state of helplessness — the victim's resulting helplessness being itself a necessary element of the offense. The third paragraph addresses parents whose habitual drunkenness, drug use, or generally degrading conduct results in a serious neglect of a child's moral upbringing, safety or health, creating a real and concrete danger to the child; notably, this applies even to a parent whose custody rights have already been legally withdrawn, since the loss of custody does not erase the underlying responsibility the law is enforcing. Unlike the first paragraph, the second and third paragraphs are prosecuted without requiring a victim's complaint.

## Parental Child Abduction and Retention (Article 234)

Article 234 is the provision most relevant to international custody disputes. Its first paragraph applies to a parent whose custody rights have been withdrawn, or to a blood relative up to the third degree, who takes or keeps a child under sixteen away from the guardian or custodian who currently has lawful care of that child, without using force or threats. In practical terms, this is the provision that applies when a parent who was not awarded custody after a divorce simply takes the child from the custodial parent's home or from wherever the child is being cared for, without resorting to violence or intimidation — the fact that the child is the offender's own son or daughter does not put the parent outside the reach of this offense. The victim, in the eyes of the law, is not the child but the custodial parent, guardian or caregiver, since what is protected is both the child's stable care environment and that caregiver's right of custody. The penalty is increased where force or threats are used, or where the child has not yet turned twelve, reflecting both the greater seriousness of coercive means and the heightened vulnerability of younger children. This offense is distinguishable from the general offense of unlawfully depriving a person of their liberty (Article 109) because Article 234 requires a specific category of offender (a parent stripped of custody, or a qualifying relative) and a specific category of victim (the guardian, custodian or caregiver, rather than the child), and because the base offense does not involve force or threats at all — features that place it in a special relationship to Article 109 rather than in competition with it. A child's own apparent consent to being taken does not prevent the offense from being committed, given the limited legal weight the law attaches to a minor's consent in this context. A third paragraph, added to Article 234 in 2006, extends this logic further: a person who keeps a child who has left home without a parent's or guardian's knowledge or consent — even where the child left of their own free will — without informing the family or the relevant authorities, can also be prosecuted, though only on complaint. This confirms that even a child's voluntary departure does not, by itself, excuse someone who then keeps that child without notifying the people responsible for the child's care.

## Complaint Requirements, Participation and How the Provisions Interact

The three articles are not uniform when it comes to whether prosecution requires a formal complaint. Article 233(1) and Article 234(3) require one; Article 232, Article 233(2)-(3) and Article 234(1)-(2) do not, meaning prosecutors can act on their own initiative once informed of the conduct. As for participation by third parties, both the mistreatment offense under Article 232 and the support-duty offenses under Article 233 are limited to people who hold the qualifying relationship described in each provision, but instigating or assisting someone who does hold that relationship remains possible under the Penal Code's general rules on complicity; participation in an abduction under Article 234 is assessed under those same general complicity rules as well. Where the same set of facts appears to satisfy both Article 234 and the general unlawful-restraint offense under Article 109, the more specific provision — Article 234 — takes precedence, since a specialized rule displaces a general one covering the same ground. Because these cases frequently involve a child as the person most affected, Turkish courts are expected to apply the child's best-interest principle and any applicable procedural safeguards for minors throughout the proceedings.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a parent be prosecuted for taking their own child?**
Yes, under Article 234, a parent whose custody rights have been legally withdrawn can be prosecuted for taking or keeping the child away from the parent or guardian who currently has lawful custody, even without using force. The child being the offender's biological son or daughter does not remove this exposure.

**Does the child's consent to being taken or kept prevent prosecution?**
No. Turkish courts give limited legal weight to a minor's consent in this context, and both the abduction offense under Article 234(1)-(2) and the retention-of-a-runaway offense under Article 234(3) can apply even where the child went along willingly or left home on their own.

**Is failing to pay court-ordered child support or alimony a criminal offense in Turkey?**
It can be, under Article 233(1), but only once the underlying obligation has already been established by a court decision or formal agreement, and only on the affected party's complaint. This is distinct from — and can run alongside — enforcement remedies pursued in the family-law or execution proceedings.

**What is the difference between mistreatment under Article 232 and the more serious offense of torment?**
Article 232 covers repeated demeaning or humiliating conduct within a household or authority relationship that falls short of systematic, sustained suffering. Once the conduct becomes intense and continuous enough to amount to deliberate, ongoing torment, it is treated instead under the separate and more serious torment offense in Article 96.
