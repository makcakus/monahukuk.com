Not every shipping document is a bill of lading, and not every rule governing carriage can be contracted away. TTK Art. 1242 and 1243 close out the bill of lading chapter and open the mandatory-provisions framework that governs the whole of Turkish carriage-of-goods-by-sea law.

## Other Shipping Documents: A Rebuttable Presumption Only

Art. 1242 covers documents such as sea waybills — issued to acknowledge receipt but not designed to circulate or transfer title the way a bill of lading does. Such a document creates a presumption that the carriage contract was made and that the cargo was received as described, but unlike a bill of lading, this presumption is always rebuttable, even against a good-faith third party. The reason: these documents do not circulate to a wide universe of relying strangers, so the extraordinary (near-conclusive) protection built around negotiable bills of lading is simply unnecessary here.

## Mandatory Provisions: The Default Rule Is Voidness

Art. 1243(1) answers the question underlying the entire Chapter: are the carrier's and shipper's statutory duties mandatory, or can parties contract around them? The answer is mandatory. Any clause in a freight contract, bill of lading, or other shipping document that directly or indirectly eliminates or narrows liabilities arising from the carrier's core duties (seaworthiness, deviation, deck-cargo rules, the full liability regime), the shipper's disclosure duties, or the bill of lading provisions is void.

## Indirect Evasion Is Caught Too

Art. 1243(2) closes two specific loopholes: clauses assigning the cargo interest's insurance rights to the carrier, and clauses reversing the statutory burden of proof in the carrier's favor, are treated exactly like a direct exclusion clause — void. The rule targets substance, not drafting cleverness.

## Severability, and a One-Way Ratchet

A void clause does not poison the rest of the document (Art. 1243(3)) — it is simply struck, and everything else remains enforceable. And parties remain entirely free to agree to *more* protective terms than the statute requires; only clauses that go below the statutory floor are void (Art. 1243(4)).

## Practical Note

Turkish courts have confirmed that a disclaimer for deck-cargo damage, even when it tracks the statutory deck-carriage provision on its face, cannot override the carrier's basic duty of care — courts look past the label to the practical effect of the clause.
