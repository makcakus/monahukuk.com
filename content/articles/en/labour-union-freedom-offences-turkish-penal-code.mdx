Article 117 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") is one of the more unusual provisions in Turkish criminal law: a single article title covers conduct of dramatically different severity. It criminalises coercing a shopkeeper into closing his premises through threats in exactly the same section that criminalises exploiting a destitute worker's dependency to make them work for a pittance or for nothing at all. The first is an interference with personal liberty; the second is an assault on human dignity that Turkish courts and commentators often describe, informally, as a form of modern servitude. A companion provision, Article 118, moves the same basic protection out of the individual employment relationship and into the collective sphere, safeguarding a person's freedom to join, avoid, or take part in a trade union. For any foreign business operating in Turkey, or any counsel advising on a labour dispute that has escalated into a criminal complaint, understanding where these two articles diverge — and where they overlap with the much more severe offence of human trafficking — is essential.

## A Protected Interest That Shifts From Liberty to Dignity

The values these provisions protect are not uniform. Under the first paragraph of Article 117, what is protected is a person's freedom to choose, continue, or abandon an occupation — the criminal-law counterpart of the constitutional guarantee of freedom to work and to contract. The second and third paragraphs move past liberty altogether and protect human dignity directly: here, the law is not punishing the restriction of someone's free will, but the exploitation of a will that is already compromised by circumstance. Article 118 protects something different again — a collective interest, namely the freedom to join or not join a union, to participate or not participate in union activity, and the union's own institutional functioning.

## The Basic Offence and the Problem of "Other Unlawful Conduct"

Under Article 117(1), a person who uses force or threats, or otherwise engages in unlawful conduct, to interfere with another person's freedom to work commits an offence carrying six months to two years' imprisonment or a judicial fine, prosecuted only on the victim's complaint. No special status is required of the offender — an employer, a competing shopkeeper, or a complete stranger with no connection to the employment relationship can all commit it. Force denotes physical compulsion; a threat is the communication of a future harm.

The most contested element is the third alternative act. Force and threats are relatively bounded concepts, but "other unlawful conduct" opens a much wider field. Consistent with the principles of legality and precision that Turkish criminal law demands, this phrase should be read narrowly, limited to conduct of comparable intensity to force or threats that actually affects the victim's will to work. Mere competition, ordinary economic pressure, or an expression of displeasure does not qualify. The offence requires a genuine result: the victim's working or not working must actually have been affected, not merely threatened in the abstract.

## Exploitative Employment: The Article's More Serious Face

Article 117(2) targets a materially different wrong: making a person work for free, or for wages plainly disproportionate to the service rendered, by exploiting that person's destitution, homelessness, or dependency — or subjecting a person already in such a position to living or working conditions incompatible with human dignity. This form carries six months to three years' imprisonment or a judicial fine of no fewer than one hundred days. Force or threats are not required at all; what distinguishes this offence is that the offender takes advantage of a vulnerability that already exists. Consequently, a defence that the victim "consented" ordinarily fails, because that apparent consent is itself a product of the very destitution being exploited. Victims who are children, or migrants without secure status, are typical examples of people whose vulnerability the provision is designed to reach.

Article 117(3) extends liability further by criminalising, with the same penalty, the act of procuring, dispatching, or transporting a person with a view to placing them in such a situation — conduct that would ordinarily be no more than a preparatory step is elevated into a stand-alone offence.

### The Boundary With Human Trafficking

This is the most consequential distinction in practice. Human trafficking, punished far more severely under Article 80 of the Penal Code, requires the offender to use some means that corrupts the victim's will — threats, pressure, force, or deception — in order to procure, transport, or harbour the person. Article 117(2)–(3) contains no such means requirement: the offender simply takes advantage of a destitution, homelessness, or dependency the victim is already in. This distinction also explains the large gap in sentencing between the two offences: where the investigation can identify a specific means that corrupted the victim's will, Article 80 applies rather than Article 117. As a result, the central question in these investigations is almost always whether the method the defendant used actually vitiated the victim's free will, or merely exploited a pre-existing condition.

## Coercion Directed at Collective Labour Disputes

Article 117(4) addresses a further scenario: using force or threats to compel an employee or an employer to reduce or increase wages, to accept terms different from those already agreed, or to cause a work stoppage, its termination, or its continuation. This carries six months to three years' imprisonment. The provision runs in both directions — it covers both forcing work to stop and forcing a stopped operation to continue — and the victim can equally be an employee or an employer.

## Interference With Union Rights and the Requirement of Specific Intent

Under Article 118(1), a person who uses force or threats to compel someone to join or not join a union, to participate or not participate in union activities, or to resign from a union or from a position within its management, faces six months to two years' imprisonment. The decisive phrase is "for the purpose of compelling" — general intent is not enough; the offender must act with the specific intent of achieving that particular outcome. The offence is complete once force or threats are used with that intent; it is not necessary for the offender to actually succeed in compelling the victim.

Article 118(2) separately punishes obstructing a union's activities through force, threats, or other unlawful conduct, carrying one to three years' imprisonment. The first paragraph protects an individual's union-related choices; the second protects the union's own institutional operation.

## Aggravating Circumstances: Scope and Limits of the Shared Provision

Article 119 provides that several offences — including obstruction of education, obstruction of public institutions or professional bodies, obstruction of political rights, obstruction of the exercise of religious or ideological freedom, violation of the inviolability of the home, and interference with freedom to work — carry a sentence increased by half again where committed with a weapon, by someone who has disguised their identity, through an anonymous letter or a special sign, by more than one person acting together, by exploiting the intimidating power of an actual or presumed criminal organisation, or by abusing the influence conferred by a public office. Critically, Article 118 is not among the offences listed in Article 119, so interference with union rights cannot be aggravated on this basis. Article 119(2) also provides that where any of the listed offences results in an aggravated form of intentional injury, the provisions on intentional injury apply in addition.

## Attempt, Participation, and Concurrence of Offences

Because Article 117(1) and Article 118(2) require an actual result, an attempt is conceptually possible for both; attempt may also be considered for Article 117(3) to the extent its acts of execution can be divided into stages. Article 118(1), by contrast, is completed once force or threats are used with the specific intent to compel, so the scope for attempt there is narrow. None of these offences require a special status in the offender, so all forms of participation are possible. As for concurrence, a threat used to commit the offence is not separately punished, since it is already an element of the crime; where force is used, an ordinary battery is generally treated as absorbed into the offence, though the aggravated consequences of intentional injury under Article 119(2) are assessed separately. Where the same victim is targeted repeatedly, at different times, pursuant to a single criminal design, the rules on continuing offences come into play.

## Penalties, Complaint Requirement, and Procedure

Only Article 117(1) is subject to a victim's complaint; without one, no investigation may proceed, and the general six-month complaint period applies. By contrast, the conduct described in Article 117(2), (3), (4), and the whole of Article 118 are prosecuted ex officio, regardless of whether the victim complains. This distinction also governs whether mediation is available: because Article 117(1) depends on a complaint, it falls within the scope of mediation, while the remaining paragraphs and Article 118 are excluded from that route. Given the applicable sentencing ranges, these cases are tried before the courts of first instance for criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an employer have to use physical force for Article 117 to apply?**
No. A threat alone is sufficient, as is other unlawful conduct of comparable intensity that actually affects the victim's ability to work. Ordinary competitive pressure or an expression of dissatisfaction is not enough on its own.

**If a worker appears to consent to poor pay or conditions, does that prevent liability under Article 117(2)?**
Generally not. Where the apparent consent flows from the worker's own destitution, homelessness, or dependency, the law treats that consent as a product of the very vulnerability being exploited, not as a valid waiver.

**How is this offence distinguished from human trafficking?**
The key question is whether the offender employed some means — threats, pressure, force, or deception — that corrupted the victim's free will (which points to trafficking under Article 80), or instead merely took advantage of a vulnerability the victim was already in (which points to Article 117). The sentencing consequences of that distinction are substantial.

**Can a victim withdraw a complaint to end proceedings under Article 117?**
Only for Article 117(1), which requires a complaint and can be mediated. The other paragraphs of Article 117 and all of Article 118 are prosecuted ex officio and are not affected by a victim's decision to withdraw a complaint.
