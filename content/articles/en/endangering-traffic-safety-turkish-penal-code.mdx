Foreign drivers and businesses operating in Turkey are sometimes surprised to learn that dangerous driving and driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs are not merely administrative traffic offenses in Turkey — under certain conditions, they can amount to a criminal offense carrying a prison sentence. Article 179 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), together with the neighboring Article 180, sits in the chapter of the Code dealing with offenses that create general danger. It punishes tampering with transport signaling systems, driving in a manner that endangers others, and driving while unfit to do so because of alcohol, drugs, or another impairing condition. Understanding how these provisions work — and, crucially, where the line falls between a routine traffic ticket and a criminal charge — matters for anyone facing a prosecution arising from a road traffic incident in Turkey.

## What the Law Actually Protects

Article 179 is not simply a rule that punishes breaking traffic regulations. Although it appears in the part of the Code aimed at safeguarding the orderly functioning of land, sea, air and rail transport, the offense is not complete merely because a transport rule was broken in the abstract. The provision requires that the conduct in question actually created a concrete danger to the life, health or property of identifiable people. In other words, Article 179 operates as a hybrid: it is framed around the safety of the transport system, but its true target is the protection of individuals who could realistically be harmed by that conduct. This dual character — general transport safety on one side, an individual's concrete safety on the other — is the reason the offense is so frequently litigated: courts must decide, case by case, whether a given act of dangerous driving crossed the line from a regulatory infraction into an act that genuinely endangered someone.

## Three Distinct Forms of the Offense

Article 179 does not describe a single act. It sets out three separate categories of conduct, each carrying its own sentencing range:

- **Article 179(1) — interfering with transport signaling or infrastructure.** This covers altering, disabling, or removing traffic or transport signals, giving false signals, placing an obstacle on a route used for passage, arrival, departure or landing, or tampering with a technical operating system, where the act creates a danger to the life, health or property of others. This branch can be committed by anyone, not just a driver, and carries a sentence of one to six years' imprisonment — the heaviest range in the article, reflecting the potential for infrastructure interference to endanger a large and undefined group of people.
- **Article 179(2) — driving in a manner that is capable of endangering others.** Anyone who actually operates a vehicle — land, sea, air or rail — in a way that creates a danger to the life, health or property of others faces a sentence of four months to two years' imprisonment. This is the provision most often invoked in ordinary road traffic cases involving reckless driving, such as running a red light or dangerous overtaking, but — as discussed below — the mere breach of a traffic rule is not enough on its own.
- **Article 179(3) — driving while unfit due to alcohol, drugs, or another impairing condition.** A person who drives despite being in a state where they cannot safely operate a vehicle — whether due to alcohol, narcotics, or any other cause — faces six months to two years' imprisonment.

Although each branch is triggered through a different kind of conduct, all three share the same underlying requirement: a real, demonstrable danger, not simply a rule violation.

## The Concrete Danger Requirement and the Line With Administrative Offenses

The most sensitive question in practice is whether ordinary traffic violations — speeding, running a red light, unsafe overtaking — are automatically criminal under Article 179(2). The prevailing view among Turkish legal scholars and, as discussed below, in Court of Cassation case law, is that they are not. A rule violation by itself does not constitute the offense; the prosecution must additionally show that the driver's conduct created a real and immediate danger to the life, health or property of specific, identifiable people in that particular situation. Without this additional showing, Article 179 would effectively convert every administrative traffic citation into a criminal charge — an outcome the concrete-danger requirement is specifically designed to prevent. As a result, phrases describing driving "in a manner capable of" causing danger must be read as requiring an observable, case-specific danger that actually materialized, not a hypothetical or statistical risk.

## Driving Under the Influence: Does Danger Still Need to Be Proven?

Article 179(3), dealing with alcohol and drugs, is worded slightly differently from Article 179(2): it does not explicitly refer to a resulting danger, focusing instead on the fact that the person drove while unfit to do so safely. This textual difference has generated debate over whether this branch requires proof of an actual danger or criminalizes the state of impairment itself, regardless of outcome. Given the overall structure and heading of the article — "endangering" — the more consistent reading is that a concrete danger should still be shown alongside the loss of safe driving capacity: the blood alcohol level or the impairment itself is not automatically sufficient on its own. In practice, a high level of intoxication functions as strong evidence pointing toward such a danger, but it does not replace the need to establish that the driver's conduct in the specific circumstances put others at risk.

## A Recent Legislative Change to the Sentencing Ranges

The prison ranges under Article 179 are not static. Law No. 7550, dated 4 June 2025, raised the minimum sentence under Article 179(2) from three months to four months. More significantly, it removed the earlier drafting under which Article 179(3) was punished merely by reference to the sentence for Article 179(2), and replaced it with its own independent range of six months to two years. This is a meaningful shift: driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs is no longer treated as a variant of ordinary dangerous driving borrowing its punishment from that provision, but stands as its own offense with a higher statutory minimum than Article 179(2). This can reasonably be read as the legislature treating impaired driving as inherently more serious than dangerous driving in general.

## Intent: The Mental Element Required

Article 179 is an intentional offense. The driver (or the person tampering with signaling equipment) must act knowing that the conduct could create a danger to others. Both direct intent and a lower form of intent — where the person foresees the possible danger and proceeds regardless — are sufficient; there is no requirement that the person specifically wanted the danger to occur. What matters is that the underlying risky conduct was carried out knowingly and willingly, not that the harmful consequence itself was desired. A purely negligent traffic mistake, without this awareness, does not meet this threshold for Article 179(2) or 179(3).

## Why Careless Driving on Ordinary Roads Falls Outside the Negligence Provision

A separate provision, Article 180, punishes negligently — rather than intentionally — creating a danger to life, health or property in the context of sea, air or rail transport, with a sentence of three months to three years. Ordinary road traffic is deliberately excluded from this negligence-based provision. Because Articles 179(2) and 179(3) can only be committed intentionally, this creates a structural gap: negligent dangerous driving on ordinary roads, where no actual injury or death results, generally falls outside the reach of both Article 179 and Article 180, leaving only administrative traffic penalties to apply. This is understood in Turkish legal commentary as a deliberate legislative choice rather than an oversight.

## What Happens if the Danger Turns Into an Actual Injury or Death

If the dangerous conduct escalates and someone is actually killed or injured, the relationship between Article 179/180 and the separate offenses of negligent or intentional homicide and bodily injury needs to be resolved. The generally accepted approach in Turkish criminal law is that the danger offense is absorbed into the resulting harm offense: once an actual injury or death occurs, the driver's liability is determined under the negligent or intentional homicide/injury provisions, depending on the degree of fault, and Article 179/180 is not applied on top of that. The danger offense functions as a preparatory stage that is superseded once real harm materializes, rather than being prosecuted as a separate, additional crime.

## Procedure: Prosecution, Jurisdiction and Related Issues

Article 179 offenses are prosecuted by the public prosecutor on the state's own initiative — a private complaint from a victim is not required, and the case cannot be resolved through mediation. Trials are heard by the competent Criminal Court of First Instance (Asliye Ceza Mahkemesi) in the place where the act occurred. Attempt is conceptually possible for Article 179(1), where interference with a signal is complete but the resulting danger has not yet materialized; it is more limited for Articles 179(2) and 179(3), since these describe conduct that is inherently bound up with the act of driving itself. General rules on complicity apply, so inciting or assisting someone to tamper with a transport signal, for example, can also give rise to liability.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is every traffic violation in Turkey a criminal offense under Article 179?**
No. A breach of ordinary traffic rules, such as speeding or an unsafe lane change, is not automatically a crime. Prosecutors and courts must additionally show that the specific conduct created a real, concrete danger to the life, health or property of identifiable people — not just a theoretical risk.

**Does a positive alcohol or drug test automatically prove the offense under Article 179(3)?**
Not by itself. While a high level of impairment is treated as strong evidence, the prevailing approach requires that the driver's actual conduct on the road created a genuine danger to others, beyond the mere fact of impairment.

**What happens if dangerous or drunk driving leads to an actual accident with injuries or a fatality?**
In that case, Article 179 generally gives way to the more specific negligent or intentional homicide and bodily injury provisions of the Turkish Penal Code, which are applied instead of — not in addition to — the danger offense.

**Can a case under Article 179 be settled privately with the other party, or dropped if no complaint is filed?**
No. This offense is prosecuted by the state on its own initiative regardless of any private complaint, and it falls outside the categories of offenses that can be resolved through mediation.
