Turkish criminal law separates threats and provocative speech directed at one identifiable victim from the same conduct aimed at the public at large. Articles 213, 214 and 215 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) form a cluster of offenses grouped under "crimes against public peace," and they apply when a statement is not addressed to a specific person but broadcast in a way that an indefinite number of people could hear or read it. For journalists, foreign residents, and international counsel following a Turkish criminal file, understanding what separates these public-order offenses from ordinary threat or defamation charges — and where the line runs against protected speech — is essential, since the same words can be charged very differently depending on their audience.

## What Ties These Offenses Together: Publicness and Collective Harm

The shared feature of Articles 213 through 215 is that none of them protect a named individual as their primary victim. Instead, what the law protects is society's general sense of security and its ability to go about daily life without living under a cloud of fear or being pushed toward internal conflict. Because the intended or actual audience is the public rather than one person, each offense requires that the statement be made openly — in a place, medium, or manner capable of reaching an indeterminate group of people. A remark made privately to a small, fixed circle of listeners will not satisfy this requirement, no matter how alarming its content. Turkish courts and legal commentary treat a public social media post as capable of meeting this openness requirement just as easily as a loudspeaker announcement or a printed leaflet would. Because these are speech-based offenses, every case under this cluster also raises the underlying question of where legitimate expression ends and a genuine threat to public order begins — a boundary that Turkish law tries to draw with objective, provable elements rather than by looking at how offensive or unpopular the speech is.

## Threatening the Public to Create Fear or Panic (Article 213)

Article 213(1) punishes a person who openly threatens harm to life, health, physical or sexual inviolability, or property, where the purpose is to create anxiety, fear, or panic among the public. The threat can point to any one of these protected interests — the statute treats them as alternatives, so a credible public threat against property is enough even without any threat to physical safety. Anyone can commit the offense, but the "victim" is not a specific individual; it is an indeterminate group of people. Whether the threatened harm could realistically be carried out is not the test — what matters is whether the statement, objectively, was capable of frightening the public. Because the law requires the specific intent of generating fear or panic among the public, ordinary careless or exaggerated speech made without that purpose does not fall under this article. The key line that separates Article 213 from an ordinary threat under Article 106 is the identity of the target: once the speaker directs the threat at a specific, identifiable person rather than the public, the case moves out of Article 213 and into the general threat provision. Article 213(2) treats use of a weapon while committing the offense as an aggravating circumstance, allowing the sentence to be increased by up to half depending on how the nature of the weapon affects the seriousness of the threat and its impact on the public.

## Inciting Others to Commit a Crime (Article 214)

Article 214(1) makes it a standalone offense to openly incite others to commit a crime, independent of whether anyone actually goes on to commit it. The incitement itself is treated as already complete once it is made public — the law does not require proof that any listener acted on the call. For the same reason, a vague, general statement that does not point toward any identifiable offense does not qualify as incitement under this article; the call must be concrete enough that a specific crime can be identified. Article 214(2) singles out a far more serious variant: inciting one part of the public to take up arms against another part with the aim of having them kill each other, which carries a dramatically heavier custodial sentence than ordinary incitement. This heightened version targets speech capable of sparking large-scale civil conflict, reflecting how much more severely Turkish law treats a call to communal violence than an isolated call to commit an individual crime. Article 214(3) then supplies an important interaction rule: if the incited crime is actually committed, the inciter is no longer prosecuted for the separate offense of incitement, but instead is held liable as an instigator of the crime that followed, under the Penal Code's general instigation rules. In other words, incitement functions as a fallback offense that only applies for as long as the underlying crime has not been carried out; once it is, the case is absorbed into ordinary instigation liability rather than punished twice.

## Praising a Crime or an Offender (Article 215)

Unlike Articles 213 and 214, which look forward to a threat or a call to future conduct, Article 215 looks backward: it punishes openly praising a crime that has already been committed, or praising the specific person who committed it. As with the other two offenses, the audience must be the public rather than one listener, and the praise must attach to a concrete, identifiable crime or offender — general or abstract statements of approval that cannot be tied to a specific case fall outside the article. The most debated feature of Article 215 is a qualifying phrase added by a 2013 legislative amendment (Law No. 6459), which requires that the praise actually give rise to a clear and imminent danger to public order. That amendment changed the character of the offense: praise alone, however distasteful, is no longer sufficient on its own — prosecutors and courts must also establish a causal link between the statement and a concrete, close-in-time risk to public order. Precisely how that clear-and-imminent-danger standard is proven in practice is where the tension between free expression and public-order protection is sharpest under this article; a merely offensive or widely condemned statement praising an offender does not, by itself, satisfy the additional element the 2013 amendment introduced.

## Procedure, Attempt, and Jurisdiction

All three offenses are conduct-based rather than result-based, and because they exist to protect public order rather than a private individual, none of them require a private complaint — prosecutors pursue them on their own initiative once they come to light. Attempt is conceptually possible for Articles 213 and 215 to the extent the underlying act can be broken into separable stages, but Article 214 is treated as complete the moment the incitement itself becomes public, leaving little room for an attempt analysis. General rules on participation apply, subject to the special rule in Article 214(3) described above, which redirects liability toward instigation once the incited crime is actually carried out. On the question of which court hears the case, Article 213, Article 214(1) and Article 215 all carry maximum sentences that keep them within the jurisdiction of the ordinary criminal courts of first instance, whereas the aggravated form in Article 214(2) — given its far higher sentencing range — falls instead within the jurisdiction of the heavy penal courts under the law governing court organization. Where a single statement arguably satisfies both Article 213 and Article 214 at once, the correct approach is to identify which characterization the statement's content predominantly matches, since Turkish law generally holds a defendant liable under a single offense rather than convicting for both in respect of the same act. Publication through press or broadcast media can also bring separate liability regimes under Turkey's media-specific legislation into play alongside these Penal Code provisions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a threatening comment made only to a small group of friends fall under Article 213?**
No. Article 213 requires that the threat be made openly, in a manner capable of reaching an indeterminate section of the public. A statement confined to a small, identifiable circle of listeners does not satisfy this element, even if its content would otherwise qualify as alarming.

**If I criticize or discuss a crime without endorsing it, could that be charged under Article 215?**
Ordinarily, no. Article 215 targets open praise of a specific, already-committed crime or its perpetrator, and since 2013 it also requires that the praise create a clear and imminent danger to public order. Reporting on, describing, or critically discussing a crime, without praising it, does not meet these elements.

**Can someone be convicted of incitement under Article 214 even if no one actually committed the crime they called for?**
Yes. Article 214 treats the incitement itself as a complete offense once it becomes public, regardless of whether any listener went on to commit the crime that was urged.

**What happens if the crime someone incited under Article 214 is actually carried out?**
Article 214(3) redirects the case: rather than being convicted of the standalone incitement offense, the person who incited the crime is instead held liable as an instigator of the crime that was actually committed, under the Penal Code's general instigation rules.
