Employers and HR teams researching workplace safety in Turkey frequently run into Article 77 of Labour Code No. 4857 — a provision that is still cited in judgments and legal commentary even though it no longer has any operative effect. Understanding why it was removed, what replaced it, and why courts still care about its old wording is not a historical curiosity: it determines which statute a court applies to an incident, and it explains the legal architecture behind two mechanisms every employer in Turkey should know — the administrative power to halt work on safety grounds, and an employee's right to walk away from a dangerous task. This article looks at the subject from that angle, distinct from the detailed compliance obligations of Law No. 6331 covered elsewhere on this site.

## From a General Clause to a Standalone Statute

In its original form, Article 77 of the Labour Code was a short, general clause: it required the employer to take whatever measures were necessary for occupational health and safety at the workplace and to keep the relevant equipment on hand, while requiring employees to comply with those measures. For decades this single article — together with a handful of neighbouring provisions and their implementing regulations — was the entire statutory basis for workplace safety duties in Turkey.

That changed on 20 June 2012, when Law No. 6331 on Occupational Health and Safety entered into force. Article 37 of Law No. 6331 expressly repealed Articles 77 through 89 of the Labour Code — the whole block of provisions dealing with occupational health and safety, employee rights connected to it, and the mechanism for stopping dangerous work. The legislature did not merely relabel the old rule; it replaced a single general clause with a full regulatory regime that scales obligations to the hazard class of the workplace and the number of people employed there, and that reaches into specific risk categories such as night work. Article 77 itself was left standing in the statute book only as a repealed provision — a marker of where the subject used to live, not a rule anyone can rely on today.

## Why a Repealed Article Still Matters: The Temporal Application Rule

Because Law 6331 replaced rather than merely amended the old framework, Turkish courts must first decide which law governs a given dispute before applying any substantive rule. The Court of Cassation's 9th Civil Chamber has confirmed the dividing line: for occupational health and safety disputes concerning events before 20 June 2012, the (now-repealed) provisions of the Labour Code and their implementing regulations apply; for anything after that date, Law No. 6331 applies. The 10th Civil Chamber has reached the same conclusion when assessing liability for a workplace accident, expressly noting that Article 4 of Law 6331 — headed "the employer's general obligation" — reorganised in far greater detail what old Article 77 had addressed only in outline.

In practice, this means the first question in any older dispute — a long-pending accident claim, a case referred back after appeal, or litigation concerning an incident that occurred years before the parties actually went to court — is the date on which the accident or the safety breach occurred, not the date the lawsuit was filed. Get that date wrong and the wrong statute, the wrong obligations, and potentially the wrong evidentiary standard get applied to the case.

## Work Stoppage: A Public-Law Safety Valve Layered on Top of Private Obligations

One mechanism that sits alongside the employer's substantive duties — and that receives less attention than risk assessments or training obligations — is the power to stop work altogether. Where an inspection reveals a matter in the workplace's buildings, equipment, working methods, or techniques that creates a life-threatening danger for employees, the relevant part of the workplace, or the whole of it, must be shut down until the danger is removed. This decision is taken by a board of labour inspectors, or in urgent cases by a single inspector, and it is carried into effect through the local administrative authority.

The employer may object to a stoppage decision before the labour court within six working days, but — and this is the point most often missed — the objection does not suspend enforcement of the stoppage while it is pending. Employees affected by a stoppage keep their full entitlement to wages: the employer must either continue paying them or assign them to other suitable work without any reduction in pay. This allocation of risk to the employer, rather than to the workforce, reflects the same underlying principle that runs through the whole subject — the cost of an unsafe workplace is a business risk, not something that can be shifted onto the people working in it.

## The Right to Refuse Dangerous Work as a Contractual Safety Valve

Where the work-stoppage mechanism operates at the level of the whole workplace, the right to refuse dangerous work operates at the level of the individual employee, and it functions as a narrow, temporary suspension of the employee's core obligation to perform work under the employment contract. An employee facing serious and imminent danger can apply to the workplace's OHS board, or to the employer directly where no board exists, to have the danger assessed and appropriate measures decided. Once that request is granted, the employee may withhold their labour until the necessary measures are taken, without any loss of wages or other contractual and statutory rights.

Where the danger cannot realistically be avoided, the employee does not even need to follow that request procedure: they may simply leave the dangerous area for the designated safe location. Because this right suspends only the employee's performance and not the underlying contract, an employer that fails to act despite a substantiated request leaves the employee free to terminate the employment relationship for just cause — with all of the severance consequences that follow from a termination attributable to the employer's own default. Courts have also made clear that this protection is not confined to physical safety complaints in the narrow sense; an employee's justified refusal to remain at a workplace where the employer is itself in serious default (for example, over unpaid wages) has been treated as conduct that cannot be penalised as unauthorised absence, underlining how broadly the underlying "employee is not at fault for staying away" logic is applied.

## Three Separate Tracks of Liability for the Same Breach

A single occupational-safety failure in Turkey is rarely dealt with under only one legal heading. Three tracks of liability run independently of one another, and a single accident or inspection finding can trigger all three at once:

- **Administrative liability**: breach of the statutory obligations under Law 6331 is sanctioned through administrative fines, and carrying out work in a part of the workplace that has been ordered stopped, without authorisation, is treated seriously enough to carry a criminal penalty of three to five years' imprisonment in its own right.
- **Civil liability**: the employer must compensate the pecuniary and non-pecuniary damage caused by a workplace accident or occupational disease resulting from the breach. This head of liability is broad and fact-specific enough that it is treated as a distinct subject in its own right.
- **Criminal liability**: where the breach involves gross negligence or intent, the general criminal-law provisions on causing injury or death through negligence can come into play alongside the administrative and civil consequences.

Because these tracks are independent, resolving one — paying an administrative fine, for instance — does not resolve or reduce exposure under the other two. Employers evaluating their exposure after an incident need to assess all three separately rather than assuming that one process disposes of the matter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Article 77 of the Labour Code still in force?**
No. It was repealed, along with Articles 78 through 89, by Article 37 of Law No. 6331 with effect from 20 June 2012. It remains relevant only as a historical and interpretive reference point, and as the marker courts use to decide which law applies to pre-2012 events.

**Which law applies to a workplace accident that happened before June 2012 but is only being litigated now?**
The date of the accident or breach controls, not the date the case is filed. Turkish courts apply the former Labour Code provisions and their implementing regulations to events before 20 June 2012, and Law No. 6331 to events after that date.

**Can an employer challenge a work-stoppage order and keep the workplace running while the challenge is pending?**
An objection can be filed with the labour court within six working days, but filing the objection does not suspend the stoppage. The affected part of the workplace must remain closed until the court rules or the danger is otherwise removed.

**If my company pays an administrative fine for an OHS breach, does that resolve the matter?**
No. Administrative, civil and criminal liability are independent of one another. Paying a fine does not prevent an injured employee from pursuing a compensation claim or preclude a separate criminal inquiry where the facts warrant one.
