Most privacy-related offenses in criminal law punish someone for learning something they should not have. Turkish law also punishes the opposite: preventing a communication from happening at all, without ever finding out what was said. Article 124 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") criminalizes obstructing or cutting off communication between people, between public institutions, or through a press or broadcasting outlet — regardless of whether the person responsible ever accesses the content of what was blocked. For foreign residents, business owners, or counsel dealing with a Turkish criminal file involving a severed phone line, a blocked letter, or a disrupted broadcast, understanding how this offense is structured is essential, because its logic differs sharply from surveillance or privacy-invasion offenses that most legal systems focus on instead.

## Three Different Interests Under One Article

Article 124 is not a single offense but three related ones sharing a common structure, each protecting a different interest. The first paragraph addresses communication between private individuals and protects personal freedom — a person's ability to reach, and be reached by, whoever they choose, through whatever channel they choose. The second paragraph addresses communication between public institutions, where the interest at stake shifts from individual liberty to the uninterrupted functioning of public administration. The third paragraph addresses obstruction of a press or broadcasting outlet's transmission, where the protected interest becomes the public's collective right to receive information. This is not a stylistic detail: the second and third paragraphs carry materially heavier penalties than the first, precisely because lawmakers treated disruption of institutional or public communication as more serious than disruption of a purely private exchange.

## What Counts as Obstruction

The conduct punished is broadly understood to cover both preventing a communication from ever being established and cutting off a communication that has already begun. Physically preventing a letter from being posted, disabling a device or a line, jamming a signal, or taking down an institution's communication infrastructure can all fall within this conduct, and the law does not tie itself to any particular technology — written, oral and electronic communication are all covered equally. What matters is that the interference targets the technical channel of communication itself. Simply making it unpleasant or difficult for someone to speak — shouting over a conversation, for instance — does not, on its own, satisfy the offense unless it actually severs the technical flow of the communication.

## Are the Three Paragraphs the Same Offense or Separate Crimes?

There is ongoing debate over whether the second and third paragraphs are aggravated versions of the first paragraph's offense or genuinely distinct offenses. The prevailing view treats them as separate offenses, because what changes between them is not merely the severity of the penalty but the subject matter and the class of victim. Under the first paragraph, the victim is the private individual whose communication was blocked, and the offense can be committed by anyone — no special status is required of the offender. Under the second paragraph, the interest harmed belongs to the public institutions involved and, by extension, the orderly functioning of public administration. Under the third paragraph, the affected parties include both the broadcasting outlet itself and the public whose access to information has been curtailed. This distinction has a concrete consequence: for offenses against private individuals, a court may impose a fine instead of imprisonment, whereas obstruction of institutional or press communication carries only imprisonment, with a substantially higher minimum sentence.

## The Unlawfulness Requirement

All three paragraphs require that the obstruction be unlawful, and this qualifier does real work in practice. Communication restrictions imposed under a valid legal framework in a detention facility, measures ordered through a properly issued court decision, disruptions caused by a genuine technical failure, or a service provider lawfully suspending a subscription under its own contract can all fall outside the offense even though the underlying conduct — stopping a communication — looks identical on its face. Consent operates in the same space: a person can consent to having their own communication interrupted, and that consent removes the unlawfulness of the act, but only with respect to that person's own communication freedom — it cannot extend to cutting off a third party's line or correspondence without that third party's own consent. In practice, a significant share of disputes under this article turn less on whether an interruption occurred than on whether it can be justified under one of these grounds.

## Distinguishing Obstruction from Violating the Confidentiality of Communication

The offense most often confused with obstruction of communication is violating the confidentiality of communication, a separate offense under Article 132 of the same code. The distinction is straightforward in principle: violating confidentiality requires the offender to access, learn, record or disclose the content of a communication, whereas obstruction never touches the content at all — it simply prevents the communication from taking place or continuing. Someone who burns another person's letter without reading it has obstructed communication; someone who opens and reads it has violated its confidentiality instead. Where a single person both learns the content and also prevents the message from reaching its recipient, the two interests protected are distinct enough that both offenses may be charged concurrently.

## Intent, Attempt and Participation

This is an intentional offense only; negligent conduct is not punishable under Article 124. General intent suffices — the offender need only be aware that they are obstructing a communication between people or institutions and want that result, without any particular motive being required. Whether the interference is driven by jealousy, commercial rivalry, or political motive makes no difference to whether the offense is made out, though a court may weigh motive when individualizing the sentence. Because the offense requires knowledge and intent, an interruption caused by an accident, a technical fault, or simple carelessness — without the intent to obstruct communication — does not constitute this crime, a distinction that has proven decisive in a number of reported decisions where physical damage to cables or wires was found to be incidental to theft rather than aimed at stopping any specific communication.

Because it protects against the risk created by cutting off communication rather than requiring any further material harm, the offense is classified as one of abstract danger — completion of the obstruction itself is enough, and the victim does not need to show any additional loss. Since the conduct can be broken into distinct stages, attempt is possible: someone caught in the act of severing a communication line before the interruption is actually completed can be held liable for attempt, although this is harder to establish where the act is instantaneous. All forms of participation are possible as well, and in cases involving obstruction of institutional communication systems, a person with technical access who assists in the disruption may be treated as a co-perpetrator or as an accessory depending on how significant their contribution was.

## Penalties and Procedure

Unlawfully obstructing communication between private individuals is punishable by six months to two years of imprisonment or a judicial fine, with the choice between the two left to the court. Obstructing communication between public institutions is punishable by one to five years of imprisonment, and unlawfully obstructing the broadcast of a press or media outlet carries the same one-to-five-year range. The law does not condition prosecution of this offense on a victim's complaint — proceedings are initiated and pursued by the public prosecutor on the state's own initiative, and a victim's withdrawal of a complaint does not by itself terminate the case. Given the sentencing range, first-instance cases are tried before the criminal court of general jurisdiction (asliye ceza mahkemesi), and mechanisms such as deferral of the announcement of the verdict or suspension of the sentence remain available where their statutory conditions are otherwise met, particularly for the first-paragraph offense.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the offender need to have read or listened to the communication that was blocked?**
No. Obstruction of communication is defined entirely by preventing or cutting off the communication itself; the offender's lack of access to its content is what separates this offense from violating the confidentiality of communication under Article 132.

**Can a service provider be liable for suspending a customer's phone or internet line?**
Not automatically. If the suspension is carried out under a valid contractual right, a lawful court order, or a genuine technical necessity, the interruption is not unlawful and the offense is not made out, even though the conduct otherwise resembles obstruction.

**Is obstruction of communication punished the same way regardless of who is affected?**
No. Obstruction between private individuals allows a court to choose between imprisonment and a judicial fine, while obstruction of communication between public institutions or of a press outlet's broadcast is punished only with imprisonment, at a materially higher minimum.

**Does a victim's decision to drop the complaint end the case?**
No. Prosecution proceeds ex officio, meaning the case continues to be pursued by the public prosecutor regardless of whether the victim later withdraws their complaint.
