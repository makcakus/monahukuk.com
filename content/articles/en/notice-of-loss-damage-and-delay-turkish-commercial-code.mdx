TTK Art. 1185 is arguably the single most litigated provision in Turkish maritime cargo law — the procedural gate every damage claim must pass through before the merits are even reached.

## The Core Rule: Notice at Delivery, or Within Three Days

Loss or damage must be reported to the carrier in writing at the latest at delivery. If the damage isn't externally apparent, written notice within three consecutive days of delivery suffices, and must at least generally describe what the loss or damage consists of. In practice, this is the first battleground of nearly every cargo case: a consignee who takes delivery without reservation and misses the three-day window starts from a severely weakened position.

## Inspection Can Substitute for Notice

If the cargo was inspected with both parties' participation by a court, authority, or officially appointed expert, no separate written notice is needed — directly rewarding use of the Art. 1184 inspection mechanism discussed previously.

## The Dual Presumption for Missing Notice — and Why It's Devastating

If neither notice nor inspection occurs, the law presumes (a) that the carrier delivered the cargo exactly as described in the bill of lading, and (b) that any loss or damage later discovered stemmed from a cause the carrier isn't responsible for. Both presumptions are technically rebuttable, but in practice this flips the entire burden structure: instead of the carrier proving it wasn't at fault, the claimant must now prove both that the damage occurred during sea carriage *and* that it's attributable to the carrier. Turkish courts apply this consistently and strictly.

## Delay Notice: Longer Window, Harsher Consequence

Delay claims get a much longer sixty-day notice window from delivery — reflecting how much longer economic delay damages take to quantify than physical damage. But the penalty for missing it is harsher: "no compensation is paid" for delay damages, full stop, with no rebuttable presumption to fall back on.

## Who Notice Must Reach

Notice to an actual carrier counts as notice to the contracting carrier and vice versa, and notice to the master or any responsible ship's officer counts as notice to either — sparing claimants from having to untangle a carrier's internal corporate structure.
