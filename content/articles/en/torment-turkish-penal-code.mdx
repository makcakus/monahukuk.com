Turkish criminal law addresses severe, degrading ill-treatment through two separate offenses depending on who commits it. Where a public official inflicts such treatment in connection with official duties, the applicable offense is torture under Article 94 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK). Where the same intensity of mistreatment occurs between private individuals — most commonly within a family, a household, or a custodial relationship — Turkish law does not leave the conduct unpunished or treat it merely as an aggravated form of battery. Instead, Article 96 TCK creates a distinct offense generally referred to as "torment" (eziyet), aimed at repeated or systematic conduct that subjects a person to sustained suffering, helplessness or humiliation. This article explains the structure of the offense, how it is distinguished from both torture and ordinary intentional injury, and what it means in practice for anyone involved in a Turkish criminal proceeding touching on domestic or custodial abuse.

## What Interest the Offense Protects

Article 96 sits within the part of the Turkish Penal Code dealing with offenses against persons, in the chapter grouping torture and torment together. The legislature's concern is conduct that reduces a victim from an autonomous subject to an object of another person's will — conduct whose defining harm is the erosion of human dignity, not simply physical damage. Because of this, the offense also protects a victim's physical and psychological integrity as a secondary interest: some torment consists of physical mistreatment, some consists purely of psychological abuse, and either form is capable of satisfying the offense on its own.

## Who Can Commit and Who Can Be a Victim

Article 96 does not restrict the offender to any particular status. Unlike torture under Article 94, which by definition can only be committed by a public official acting in connection with official duties, torment can be committed by anyone — a family member, a caregiver, an employer, or an unrelated third party. Even a public official can be held liable under Article 96 rather than Article 94 where the mistreatment occurs in a purely private capacity, without any use of the authority attached to the official's position.

The victim, too, can in principle be anyone, but the statute singles out certain circumstances for heightened treatment. Where the victim is a woman, the minimum sentence for the basic offense is raised, without changing the overall sentencing range. Separately, the statute treats it as an aggravated form of the offense — carrying a materially higher sentencing range — where the conduct is directed at a child, at a person who cannot defend themselves physically or mentally, at a pregnant woman, or at an ascendant, descendant, parent-in-law equivalent, spouse, or former spouse.

## The Conduct Requirement: Repetition and System, Not a Single Act

Article 96 does not list specific acts. It is deliberately open-ended, covering any conduct capable of causing a person to suffer — physical mistreatment, deprivation of food, water or sleep, sustained insults and humiliation, intimidation, or forcing someone to live in degrading conditions. What matters is not the form the conduct takes but its cumulative effect on the victim.

Turkish doctrine and judicial practice have converged on two criteria that separate torment from other offenses: continuity and system. Torment is not completed by a single, isolated act, however serious; it is constituted by a course of conduct repeated over a period of time, through which the victim is driven into a state of helplessness, fear and humiliation beyond ordinary physical pain. Legally, the offense is treated as a single unified crime even though it is made up of multiple individual acts spread across time.

## The Mental Element

Torment can only be committed intentionally; the Penal Code does not provide for a negligent form of the offense. The offender must be aware that the conduct will cause the victim suffering, helplessness or humiliation, and must want that result — or, on a more debated reading, at least accept it as a likely consequence of sustained conduct. No special motive is required for the offense to exist: motives such as revenge, jealousy, or a purported intent to "discipline" the victim do not determine whether the offense has occurred, though they may be relevant to how severely the court sentences within the applicable range.

## Distinguishing Torment From Intentional Injury and From Torture

The most frequently litigated question is whether a given set of facts amounts to intentional injury under Article 86 TCK or to torment under Article 96. The decisive factor is the intensity and time-distribution of the conduct, not its outcome in isolation. A single, sudden act — even one causing serious injury — is, as a rule, classified as intentional injury under Article 86. By contrast, a series of acts against the same victim, repeated over a period of time and forcing the victim to live under sustained fear and pressure, is classified as torment. Courts are therefore expected to assess the incidents together, in light of their connection to one another and their cumulative effect on the victim, rather than examining each incident in isolation.

The line between torment and torture, by contrast, is drawn by reference to the offender rather than the conduct itself. Where mistreatment of comparable severity is inflicted by a public official acting in connection with official duties, it falls under Article 94 as torture; where it is inflicted by anyone else, it falls under Article 96 as torment. Anyone who participates in torture committed by a public official is punished as if they held that same official status, even if they do not. The difference in sentencing ranges reflects this distinction: torture carries a substantially higher minimum and maximum sentence than the basic form of torment.

Because torment is legally treated as a single ongoing offense rather than a series of separate crimes, conduct that occurs as part of the same course of mistreatment — minor injury, threats or insults committed in the course of the torment — is generally absorbed into the torment charge rather than punished separately, provided its unlawfulness does not exceed what the torment charge already reflects. Independent and more serious wrongs, such as aggravated injury resulting in a specific serious consequence, or offenses against sexual integrity, are not absorbed in this way and give rise to separate liability alongside the torment conviction.

## Sentencing and Procedural Features

The basic form of the offense carries a sentencing range set out in Article 96(1), with a raised minimum where the victim is a woman. The aggravated forms listed in Article 96(2) — victims who are children, persons unable to defend themselves, pregnant women, or the specified family relationships — carry a distinctly higher range. This shift in the minimum sentence has practical consequences beyond the raw sentence length: at the basic-offense minimum, suspension of the sentence or a deferral of the announcement of the verdict may be available if the general conditions are met, while the higher minimum applicable to the aggravated forms generally puts those mechanisms out of reach.

Torment is not an offense subject to private complaint — prosecution proceeds regardless of whether the victim wishes to pursue it, and a victim's withdrawal does not terminate the case. It also falls outside the offenses eligible for mediation between offender and victim. Cases are heard before the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi), and the limitation period for prosecution follows the general rules tied to the maximum sentence attached to each form of the offense. In cases arising from domestic violence, criminal proceedings under Article 96 typically run alongside protective and preventive measures available under Turkey's domestic violence framework, Law No. 6284.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a single violent incident ever qualify as torment rather than intentional injury?**
As a rule, no. Torment requires a course of conduct repeated over time that drives the victim into sustained helplessness and humiliation. A single, sudden act is ordinarily assessed under the intentional injury provisions of Article 86, regardless of how severe its outcome is.

**Does a victim have to file or maintain a complaint for a torment case to proceed?**
No. Torment is prosecuted regardless of the victim's wishes, and it is not subject to withdrawal of complaint or to mediation between the parties. The public prosecutor pursues the case independently once the conduct comes to light.

**Can a public official be charged with torment instead of torture?**
Yes, where the mistreatment occurs outside the scope of the official's duties and does not involve any use of official authority. If the conduct is connected to the exercise of public functions, it is instead assessed as torture under Article 94, which carries a higher sentencing range.

**Are threats or minor injuries committed during a period of torment charged as separate offenses?**
Generally not, if their severity does not exceed what the torment charge already reflects — Turkish practice treats the underlying course of conduct as a single offense. More serious and independent wrongs, however, such as aggravated injury with a defined serious consequence or sexual offenses, are prosecuted separately in addition to the torment charge.
