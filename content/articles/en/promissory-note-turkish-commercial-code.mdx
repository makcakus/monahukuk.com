TTK Art. 776-779 regulate the promissory note (bono) — the second of Turkey's three negotiable instruments. A note follows nearly the same rules as a bill of exchange for endorsement, maturity, payment, recourse, and limitation, but structurally it has no drawee: instead of ordering a third party to pay (as a bill does), the maker promises to pay directly.

## The Mandatory Elements

Art. 776 requires seven elements largely mirroring the bill of exchange: the word "promissory note" in the text, an unconditional promise to pay a specific sum, the maturity date, the place of payment, the payee's name, the date and place of issuance, and the maker's signature. The single structural difference from a bill is element (b): a promise to pay, not an order directed at a third party — because the maker is both drawer and payer. As with bills, three gaps are curable by presumption (unstated maturity defaults to payable at sight; unstated payment place and place of issuance default to the place shown by the maker's name), but a missing note-designation word, unconditional-promise language, payee name, or maker's signature is fatal to the instrument's status as a note.

## Which Bill-of-Exchange Rules Apply

Art. 778 imports roughly forty articles of bill-of-exchange law wholesale — endorsement, maturity, payment, recourse, aval, alteration, limitation, and cancellation all apply to notes just as they do to bills, "unless inconsistent with the nature of the note." What is conspicuously absent from this cross-reference list: nothing on acceptance or the bill's maturity-type mechanics tied to a drawee, because a note has no acceptance stage at all.

## Direct Liability of the Maker

The single most important rule is Art. 779/1: a person who makes a promissory note is liable exactly as if they were the acceptor of a bill of exchange. Where a bill's drawer is only secondarily liable until the drawee accepts, a note's maker is primarily and immediately liable from the moment of issuance — no acceptance stage intervenes. This makes the maker the direct target of kambiyo-specific enforcement proceedings without any need for protest to trigger liability, and is a major reason promissory notes are so widely used commercially. The one procedural nuance concerns notes payable a fixed period after sight: these must be presented to the maker within the one-year window under Art. 693 (applied via Art. 779/2), with the maker's dated endorsement — or a protest if they refuse to endorse — fixing the start of the maturity period.

## Practical Takeaway

A foreign creditor holding a Turkish promissory note is dealing with a primary obligor from day one, making the note a faster and more predictable collection tool than a bill of exchange — provided the seven mandatory elements are all present.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which elements must a promissory note contain?

TTK Article 776 lists seven elements: the word <b>'promissory note' or 'order instrument'</b> in the text; an <b>unconditional promise to pay a definite sum</b>; the <b>maturity date</b>; the <b>place of payment</b>; the <b>name of the payee, or of the person to whose order it is payable</b>; the <b>date and place of issue</b>; and the <b>issuer's signature</b>. If the instrument is written in a language other than Turkish, the word used in that language for 'promissory note' must appear.

### Is a promissory note without a stated maturity date invalid?

No. Under TTK Article 777/2 <b>a promissory note that does not state its maturity date is deemed payable at sight</b>. Similarly, under Article 777/3, if the place of payment is not stated, the place of issue is taken to be the place of payment and also the issuer's place of residence; under Article 777/4, if the place of issue is not stated, the place written next to the issuer's name is taken to be the place of issue. Omissions other than these deprive the instrument of its character as a promissory note.

### To what extent is the issuer of a promissory note liable?

Under TTK Article 779/1 the issuer of a promissory note is liable <b>in the same way as the acceptor of a bill of exchange</b>. The issuer is thus the principal debtor on the instrument: the holder may proceed directly against them without having to satisfy the conditions for recourse, and there is no need to protest against them. Actions against the issuer are time-barred <b>three years</b> after the maturity date (Article 749, applied by the reference in Article 778).
