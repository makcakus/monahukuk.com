Turkish criminal law does not confine itself to punishing violence directed at a person's body or life. It also punishes the far quieter act of leaving someone unprotected when that person cannot look after themselves. The Turkish Penal Code (TCK) addresses this through two related but distinct offenses grouped together under the chapter on breaches of duties of protection, supervision, assistance or notification: abandonment under Article 97, and failure to render assistance or notify the authorities under Article 98. Foreign nationals and companies dealing with Turkish criminal proceedings — whether as caregivers, employers of domestic or medical staff, or parties to a road traffic incident — frequently encounter these provisions without realizing that Turkish law separates them along a specific line: whether the person who failed to act owed the victim a pre-existing duty of care, or was simply an ordinary bystander who happened upon someone in danger.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Both offenses protect the same underlying interest: the life, physical integrity and psychological well-being of a person who is, at the relevant moment, incapable of protecting themselves. Neither offense requires proof that the victim actually suffered harm — both are constructed as endangerment offenses, meaning the law intervenes once a person has been exposed to danger, independent of whether that danger later materializes into an injury or death. Article 98 in particular also reflects a broader social policy: Turkish law treats indifference toward a person in evident danger, when helping would have cost little, as more than a moral failing — it treats it as unlawful conduct with criminal consequences.

## Who Can Commit Each Offense

Abandonment under Article 97 is what is known in Turkish doctrine as a "special" offense — one that can only be committed by a defined category of people. The offender must be someone who owed the victim a duty of protection and supervision at the time of the act. That duty can arise from law, from a contract, or from a role a person has factually taken on — a parent, a legal guardian, a relative who has assumed a dependent's care, or a professional caregiver or medical attendant are the typical examples. Someone with no such relationship to the victim cannot be convicted of abandonment as such; at most, their conduct may fall under Article 98 if its own conditions are met.

Article 98, by contrast, can be committed by anyone. It is a textbook example of what Turkish criminal theory calls a "genuine omission offense": the law imposes a direct duty to act on whoever encounters a person in a specific kind of danger, regardless of any prior relationship between them. Simply being present when the danger is discovered is enough to trigger the duty.

## Who the Law Protects

The two offenses also differ in how broadly they define the vulnerable person. Article 97 protects someone who, because of age or illness, is incapable of managing on their own and is for that reason under the offender's protection and supervision. The provision is deliberately narrow: a person left helpless for a reason other than age or illness does not qualify as a victim for purposes of this article.

Article 98 casts a wider net. It covers a person who cannot manage on their own because of age, illness, injury, "or any other reason" — a phrase that Turkish courts and commentators read expansively, extending to situations such as being trapped after an accident, at risk of drowning, or exposed to extreme heat or cold. In both offenses, the deciding question is the same: whether the victim, given the circumstances they actually faced at that moment, lacked the capacity to remove themselves from danger.

## What Conduct Triggers Liability

Abandonment consists of leaving the dependent person to their own devices. This can happen actively — walking away and leaving the person where they are — or through conduct that amounts to the same result, such as depositing the person somewhere no one can look after them, or simply cutting off care and supervision altogether. What matters is that the victim ends up genuinely unprotected and exposed to risk; the offender does not need to physically walk away from the scene for the offense to be complete.

Article 97(2) provides that where the abandonment causes the victim to fall ill, become injured, or die, the offender is punished according to the general rules on offenses aggravated by their consequences, rather than under a separately stated penalty. Because Turkish law makes those aggravated-consequence rules conditional on at least negligence regarding the outcome, an offender cannot be held liable for the aggravated result unless a causal link between the abandonment and that result is established, and the offender was at least negligent as to it.

Article 98 gives the offender a choice between two ways of discharging the duty: rendering assistance to the extent circumstances allow, or promptly notifying the competent authorities. Satisfying either one is enough — someone unable to intervene personally fulfills the duty by reporting the situation to the authorities. The qualifier "to the extent circumstances allow" sets the outer limit of what can be expected: a person is never required to place their own life or physical safety in serious danger to help someone else, and the duty is bounded by what is reasonable given the actual circumstances. Because notification must be "prompt," a report made only after an unreasonable delay does not satisfy the obligation.

## The Mental Element Required

Both offenses require intent; neither can be committed negligently as a standalone charge. For abandonment, the offender must know that the victim is incapable of self-care and that a duty of protection runs from the offender to that victim, and must nonetheless choose to leave the victim unprotected. For Article 98, the offender must be aware of the danger the other person faces and must deliberately choose not to help or report it — a lower threshold of intent, where the offender merely accepts the risk of the consequence without specifically aiming for it, suffices as well. A person who genuinely fails to notice the victim's condition lacks the necessary intent, and no offense arises. As for the aggravated consequences under Article 97(2), negligence as to the outcome is enough; if the offender actually intended the aggravated result, the case moves into the territory of intentional injury or intentional homicide instead.

## Drawing the Line Between the Two Offenses

The dividing line between the two offenses is the existence of a duty of protection and supervision — what Turkish doctrine calls a "guarantor" relationship. Someone bound to the victim by such a relationship who leaves the victim unprotected commits abandonment; someone with no such bond who remains indifferent to a person in danger commits, instead, failure to assist or notify. In practice, the two most common scenarios are a driver leaving an injured party at the scene of a traffic accident, and an elderly, ill or young dependent left without care. A person injured in a road accident generally does not meet Article 97's age-or-illness criterion, so someone who leaves the scene typically falls under Article 98; a relative who has taken on responsibility for that person's care, by contrast, is assessed under Article 97.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence

Abandonment is completed the moment the victim becomes unprotected. Where the conduct unfolds as an active, divisible sequence of acts, an attempt is, as a rule, conceivable. Article 98, being a genuine omission offense, is different: it is completed once the moment for compliance has passed, which leaves no room for an attempt.

Because abandonment is a special offense limited to those with a duty of protection, a person outside that relationship cannot be a principal or co-principal offender, though they can still be held liable as an instigator or accessory. Under Article 98, since the duty runs to every individual personally, each bystander at a scene is independently responsible for their own failure to act, regardless of what anyone else present did or did not do.

Where a single act leaves multiple people abandoned, the offender is treated as having committed one continuing offense with an enhanced penalty under the general rule on continuing offenses, since abandonment is not among the offenses excluded from that rule. Where the offender's intent was actually directed at the victim's death or injury, the case is instead prosecuted as intentional homicide or intentional injury committed by omission, and no separate penalty is imposed for abandonment on top of that. Similarly, where the same conduct satisfies the elements of abandonment, Article 98 — being the more general, residual provision — is not applied in addition.

## Penalties and Procedural Features

Abandonment in its basic form carries a prison sentence of three months to two years; where an aggravated consequence occurs, the general rules on offenses aggravated by their result apply on top of that base sentence. Failure to assist or notify carries a sentence of up to one year in prison or a judicial fine, rising to one to three years in prison where the person dies as a result of the failure.

Neither offense is classified among those requiring a formal complaint by the victim, so prosecution proceeds on the authorities' own initiative and withdrawal of a complaint does not end the case. Because they are not complaint-based offenses and are not separately listed among those eligible for mediation, mediation procedures generally do not apply. Given the applicable sentence ranges, jurisdiction lies with the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi). Depending on the circumstances, mechanisms such as deferral of the announcement of the verdict, suspension of the sentence, or conversion to an alternative sanction may be available. The statute of limitations follows the shortest general limitation period given the sentence ranges involved, though where an aggravated consequence under Article 97(2) materializes, the applicable period is determined by reference to the provision actually applied to that consequence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a bystander with no relationship to the victim be charged with abandonment under Article 97?**
No. Abandonment can only be committed by someone who owed the victim a duty of protection and supervision — a parent, guardian, or person who has taken on the victim's care, for example. A stranger who fails to help someone in danger may instead be liable under Article 98, provided that provision's own conditions are met.

**Does helping in some way but not fully satisfy the duty under Article 98?**
The law requires either rendering assistance to the extent the circumstances allow, or promptly notifying the competent authorities — meeting either one discharges the duty. A person unable to intervene physically satisfies the obligation by making a timely report; a report made only after significant, unjustified delay does not.

**Is a driver who leaves an accident scene automatically guilty of abandonment?**
Not necessarily. Article 97 applies only where the injured person qualifies as a victim under its age-or-illness criterion and the driver owed that person a pre-existing duty of protection. Absent that relationship, the driver's departure is typically assessed under Article 98 instead.

**Can someone be convicted of attempting to fail to assist under Article 98?**
No. Because Article 98 is a genuine omission offense, it is treated as complete once the moment for compliance has passed, leaving no legal room for an attempt.
