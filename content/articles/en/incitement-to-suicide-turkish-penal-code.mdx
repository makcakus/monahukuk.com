Turkish criminal law does not punish a person for taking, or attempting to take, their own life. What it does punish is third-party interference with another person's decision to die — pushing someone toward suicide, reinforcing a decision already forming, or making the act easier to carry out. This offense is set out in Article 84 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), within the chapter on offenses against life. Because the conduct sits at the intersection of individual autonomy, causation and criminal responsibility, it raises evidentiary and doctrinal questions that are less familiar to lawyers trained outside the Turkish system, particularly where the victim's own final act complicates the analysis of who is legally responsible for the death. This article explains how the offense is structured, who can be held liable, and when the same facts are instead prosecuted as intentional homicide.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Article 84 protects human life as a value the legal order safeguards independently of what the individual concerned may want at a given moment. A person's own wish to die does not make a third party's contribution to that outcome lawful. The rationale is that a decision of this gravity must be left to form — or not form — free of external pressure, persuasion or facilitation; the law treats the space in which that decision is made as something worth protecting from outside interference, even where the person who eventually dies acts through their own hand. This is why the perpetrator's criminal responsibility is not erased by the fact that it was the victim, and not the perpetrator, who carried out the final act.

## Who Can Commit the Offense, and Against Whom

The offense has no special requirements on the side of the perpetrator: anyone can commit it, regardless of relationship to the victim or any particular status. The victim, correspondingly, needs no special characteristics under the offense's basic form in the first paragraph — any person whose suicide is instigated, encouraged, reinforced or assisted can found a charge. The picture changes sharply, however, once the victim lacks the capacity to understand the meaning and consequences of the act — for example because of age or a condition that removes that capacity — or where physical force or threats are used to compel the person toward suicide. In those situations, discussed further below, the law no longer treats the matter as incitement at all.

## The Conduct: Four Alternative Forms of Involvement

Article 84 is what Turkish doctrine calls an offense of alternative conduct: the law lists several distinct ways of committing it, and proof of any one of them is sufficient. These are instigating another person to commit suicide (planting a suicidal resolve that did not previously exist), encouraging the act, strengthening a decision the victim had already reached on their own, and assisting the suicide in any manner. Engaging in more than one of these forms against the same victim is still charged as a single offense, not as multiple counts.

Under the offense's basic form, the suicide itself does not need to occur — the instigating, encouraging, reinforcing or assisting conduct is enough on its own to complete the crime. Where the suicide is actually carried out, this operates as an aggravating consequence that raises the applicable sentence. In that scenario, establishing a causal link between the perpetrator's conduct and the resulting death becomes central: the prosecution must show that the victim's will was in fact shaped by the perpetrator's contribution, and that the death followed from it.

## Intent Requirement

The offense can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligence-based variant. The perpetrator must be aware that their conduct amounts to instigating, encouraging, reinforcing or assisting another person's suicide, and must want that outcome (or, on a reckless-intent analysis, foresee the possibility that the victim may take their own life as a result and proceed regardless). Careless remarks or influence exerted without any awareness of its effect fall outside the offense, since intent cannot be presumed from consequence alone. This relatively narrow mental-element requirement is what keeps the offense from spilling over into the exercise of ordinary free speech.

## Public Incitement to Suicide as a Separate Offense

A third, freestanding form of liability arises where a person publicly incites suicide, not directed at any one individual but broadcast toward an indeterminate audience. What is punished here is the act of public incitement itself, regardless of whether any particular listener acts on it or whether any suicide actually results — the law treats the abstract danger created by normalizing or promoting suicide in a public setting as sufficient in itself. Because no specific victim or outcome needs to be proven, this variant is analytically distinct from the instigation-and-assistance offense in the first paragraph, even though both are housed in the same article.

## Indirect Perpetration: When the Charge Becomes Intentional Homicide

The most consequential feature of Article 84 is its fourth paragraph, which reclassifies certain conduct entirely. Where a person drives someone lacking the capacity to understand the meaning and consequences of their acts toward suicide, or does the same to someone whose capacity has been removed, or uses force or threats to compel a person into suicide, that person is not held liable for incitement to suicide at all — they are held liable for intentional homicide. The doctrinal basis is indirect perpetration: in these scenarios the victim's will either never genuinely formed or was overridden by coercion, so the victim is treated, in legal terms, as an instrument through which the perpetrator achieved the death, rather than as an autonomous actor whose own decision breaks the chain of responsibility. The practical effect is substantial — sentencing exposure, the competent trial court, and the applicable rules on attempt all shift to those governing intentional homicide rather than incitement to suicide.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence

Because the basic form of the offense does not require the suicide to occur, an incomplete attempt at the underlying incitement conduct is analyzed differently than in outcome-based crimes — completion of the instigating, encouraging or assisting act is generally treated as the completed offense in itself, leaving limited room for a true attempt analysis except where the conduct can meaningfully be broken into stages. Where the fourth paragraph applies and the charge becomes intentional homicide, the ordinary attempt rules for that offense govern instead. As for participation, ordinary rules on complicity apply, but courts must take care not to double-count: because instigation and assistance are themselves defined as principal conduct under Article 84, the same behavior cannot also be separately punished under the general provisions on aiding or instigating another offense. Multiple qualifying acts directed at a single victim merge into one offense; acts directed at different victims are assessed separately under the general rules on concurrence of offenses.

## Sentencing and Procedural Characteristics

The basic form of the offense carries a prison sentence of two to five years. Where the suicide is actually carried out, the sentence rises to a range of four to ten years. Public incitement to suicide, as a separate offense, carries a sentence of three to eight years. Where the fourth paragraph applies, sentencing instead follows the rules for intentional homicide, which carries materially higher penalties.

As an offense against life, incitement to suicide is not subject to a complaint requirement — prosecution proceeds automatically once the conduct comes to the authorities' attention, and the offense falls outside the scope of mediation between the parties. Jurisdiction depends on which form of the offense is charged: the basic form, its aggravated (completed-suicide) variant, and the public-incitement variant are tried before the ordinary criminal court (asliye ceza mahkemesi), while cases that fall under the fourth paragraph and are prosecuted as intentional homicide go to the heavy penal court (ağır ceza mahkemesi). The limitation period for bringing a prosecution is likewise calculated by reference to the maximum sentence available for whichever variant applies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is attempting suicide itself a crime in Turkey?**
No. Turkish law does not criminalize suicide or attempted suicide by the person who takes that action. Article 84 punishes third-party conduct — instigating, encouraging, reinforcing or assisting another person's suicide — not the act of the person who dies or survives.

**Does the suicide have to actually happen for someone to be prosecuted?**
No, not under the basic form of the offense. Instigating, encouraging, reinforcing or assisting is enough to complete the crime on its own. If the suicide does occur, the sentence increases, but its occurrence is not a precondition for liability in the first place.

**What is the difference between incitement to suicide and intentional homicide in this context?**
The dividing line is who carries out the fatal act and whether the victim's will was genuine. If the victim, acting with a free and informed will, carries out the fatal act themselves after being instigated, encouraged or assisted, the offense is incitement to suicide. If the perpetrator instead carries out the fatal act directly, or the victim's will was never capable of forming freely (due to incapacity) or was overridden by force or threats, Turkish courts treat this as intentional homicide committed through indirect perpetration.

**Can a family member or someone in a position of authority over the victim be prosecuted under this article?**
Yes. The offense has no special requirement as to who the perpetrator is — any person can commit it, including a family member, partner or anyone else with influence over the victim. Where that relationship involves coercion, threats or exploitation of a victim's diminished capacity, the case is more likely to fall under the fourth paragraph and be prosecuted as intentional homicide rather than incitement to suicide.
