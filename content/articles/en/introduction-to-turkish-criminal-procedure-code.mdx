Anyone facing a criminal matter in Turkey — whether as the person under investigation, a family member trying to understand what is happening, or a lawyer coordinating with local counsel — needs to grasp one foundational distinction before anything else makes sense: substantive criminal law defines which acts are crimes and what punishment attaches to them, while a separate body of procedural law, the Criminal Procedure Code (Law No. 5271, commonly referred to as the CMK), governs how the state investigates, prosecutes and adjudicates those crimes, and what rights the people caught up in that process are entitled to. Articles 1 and 2 of the CMK open the statute by setting out exactly this: the scope of the process and the core vocabulary — suspect, defendant, defense counsel, legal representative, investigation, prosecution — that the rest of the code builds on. Understanding these opening provisions is the necessary first step to understanding any Turkish criminal case.

## What the Criminal Procedure Code Actually Governs

The CMK is Turkey's master procedural statute for criminal matters. It does not decide guilt or innocence on its own terms and does not define offenses — that is the job of the Turkish Penal Code and other substantive criminal statutes. Instead, it regulates the mechanics of getting from a suspicion that a crime occurred to a final, binding judgment: who has authority to investigate, what steps a prosecutor and the police may take, how evidence must be gathered and tested, what a court proceeding looks like, and — critically — what rights and obligations belong to everyone involved along the way, including the person accused, their lawyer, victims, and the authorities themselves. Because Turkish criminal cases can move quickly and the terminology carries real legal consequences, this procedural framework matters as much to the outcome of a case as the underlying facts.

## Balancing Truth-Finding Against Fair Trial Guarantees

The stated purpose of Turkish criminal procedure is to establish what actually happened, beyond reasonable doubt, using lawful methods. That second qualifier is doing real work: Turkish law does not treat "finding the truth" as a goal that justifies any means. Evidence obtained through unlawful methods cannot be used against a defendant regardless of how reliable or convincing it might otherwise appear, and the process as a whole is meant to respect human dignity and the right to a defense throughout. This is why the rights of suspects and defendants — including access to a lawyer, the right to remain silent, and the presumption of innocence — are treated as core structural features of the system rather than optional courtesies. Turkish appellate practice has repeatedly reinforced that fact-finding must be evaluated against standards of logic and reason, and that a conviction cannot rest on a chain of inference that does not hold up to that scrutiny.

An important consequence of this approach is that investigating authorities are expected to gather evidence in both directions — for and against the person under suspicion — rather than building a one-sided case file. This obligation to investigate even-handedly runs through much of the CMK and is one of the clearest markers that Turkish criminal procedure treats the investigation stage as more than a preliminary formality: decisions made and evidence collected during the investigation shape the entire trajectory of the case, including whatever happens in court later. Turkish courts have also made clear, drawing on the European Court of Human Rights' reasoning in the well-known *Salduz v. Turkey* case, that fair trial protections are not confined to the courtroom phase — they attach from the moment an investigation begins.

## Where Criminal Procedure Sits in the Legal Hierarchy

Turkish criminal procedure is a branch of public law: it governs how the state exercises its power to prosecute and punish, and that relationship between the individual and the state is structured hierarchically. At the top sits the Turkish Constitution, with its guarantees of a fair trial, the presumption of innocence, and personal liberty and security. Beneath that, by virtue of Article 90 of the Constitution, sits the European Convention on Human Rights and the case law of the European Court of Human Rights, both of which form part of Turkish domestic law. At the base of this hierarchy is the CMK itself, together with related special statutes. Because this is a matter of public order rather than private agreement between the parties, many of its rules cannot be waived or altered by consent — for example, rules on which court has jurisdiction are policed automatically throughout a case, and the parties cannot agree among themselves to vest authority in a court that does not legally have it. Confidentiality during the investigation phase serves a similar public function: it protects not only the integrity of evidence but also the reputational interests of a person who has not yet been found guilty of anything.

## The Two Phases of a Criminal Case: Investigation and Prosecution

Article 2 of the CMK splits every criminal case into two consecutive phases, and this division is not a mere formality — it changes the vocabulary used, the applicable rules, and even which legal principles apply at all. The investigation phase begins the moment the competent authorities learn of a suspected crime and runs until the court formally accepts the indictment. During this phase, the case is driven by the public prosecutor, the person involved is referred to as a "suspect," and the process is, as a rule, confidential. The prosecution phase begins once the indictment is accepted and continues until the judgment becomes final; here, the person is referred to as a "defendant," and the proceeding takes place in open court, subject to principles of publicity and oral argument. The line between the two phases has practical bite: the period between when an indictment is submitted to the court and when the court actually accepts it is still treated, in both scholarly commentary and Court of Cassation practice, as part of the investigation phase, not the prosecution phase.

This distinction also determines which protections are available at which point. The principle that doubt benefits the accused, for instance, presupposes that a judgment is being rendered — it can only apply during the prosecution phase, not the investigation phase, because no finding of guilt or innocence is made while a prosecutor is still assessing whether there is sufficient suspicion to bring a case at all.

## Why the Suspect/Defendant Distinction Matters in Practice

The definitions in Article 2 are not abstract terminology — they are the key to reading the rest of the statute correctly. The clearest example is the suspect/defendant distinction itself: the same individual is called a "suspect" during the investigation and a "defendant" during prosecution, and while the change in label does not itself expand or reduce the underlying procedural guarantees available to that person, it does determine which specific provisions and terminology apply to them at a given moment — for example, questioning a suspect before an indictment is treated differently in the code from the interrogation of a suspect or defendant before a judge or court. Turkish appellate courts have stressed that mislabeling someone in a document does not by itself invalidate proceedings; what matters is correctly identifying which procedural safeguards that specific person is entitled to at that specific stage. This has real consequences even in edge cases — for instance, the fact that a suspect who has absconded could not be formally questioned does not, by itself, prevent the prosecutor from filing an indictment, since the law allows certain proceedings to continue against an absconded defendant under defined conditions.

A related and frequently confused pair of terms is "defense counsel" (müdafi) and "legal representative" (vekil): the former is the lawyer acting for the suspect or defendant, while the latter represents a victim, an injured party, or someone with a related civil claim. Confusing the two can lead to real errors about who is entitled to exercise which procedural right, which is why getting this distinction right is foundational to understanding how a defense is actually conducted in a Turkish criminal case.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code decide whether an act is a crime?**
No. The CMK governs process — investigation, evidence-gathering, prosecution and trial procedure. Whether specific conduct is a crime, and what punishment applies, is determined by the Turkish Penal Code and other substantive criminal statutes.

**What is the practical difference between being called a "suspect" and a "defendant"?**
A person is a suspect during the investigation phase (before an indictment is accepted) and becomes a defendant once the prosecution phase begins. The underlying procedural protections available to that person are not eliminated by this change in label, but the specific rules and terminology that apply to them do shift between the two phases.

**Can evidence obtained through unlawful methods still be used in a Turkish criminal trial?**
No. Turkish criminal procedure treats lawful methods of evidence-gathering as a condition for using that evidence at all, regardless of how reliable it might appear. This principle is closely tied to the protection of the rights of suspects and defendants throughout the process.

**Do fair trial protections apply only once a case reaches trial?**
No. Turkish courts, consistent with the European Court of Human Rights' reasoning in *Salduz v. Turkey*, have confirmed that fair trial guarantees attach from the investigation phase onward, since evidence and decisions made at that stage shape the outcome of any later prosecution.
