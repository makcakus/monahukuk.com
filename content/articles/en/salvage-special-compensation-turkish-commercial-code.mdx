Art. 1304 introduced the "no cure, no pay" principle with a telling qualifier — "unless the chapter states otherwise" — leaving open the possibility of an exception later in the salvage chapter. Art. 1312 is that exception: a special compensation mechanism, rooted in Article 14 of the 1989 International Convention on Salvage, that applies specifically where a salvage operation targets environmental danger.

## The Basic Mechanism

Under Art. 1312(1), a salvor who worked on a vessel or cargo threatening environmental damage, and who would not otherwise earn an ordinary Art. 1305 award at least equal to the special compensation calculated under this article, may claim its expenses as special compensation from the owner — even if the operation produced no useful result at all, breaking cleanly from "no cure, no pay." Courts need not first push the ordinary award up to the salved property's value ceiling before turning to special compensation; the two mechanisms are assessed independently.

## Uplift, Floor, and Limits

Bare expenses — reasonable outlays plus a reasonable amount for equipment and personnel actually and reasonably used, assessed under the same promptness, readiness, and equipment-value criteria found in Art. 1305(1)(h)-(j) — are only the floor. Under Art. 1312(2), if the operation actually prevented or minimized environmental damage, expenses can be increased by up to 30%; where equity requires it, considering the Art. 1305(1) criteria, a court or tribunal may increase compensation further, but never beyond 100% of expenses, i.e., double. Art. 1312(4) then caps the whole mechanism: special compensation is paid only to the extent it exceeds the ordinary Art. 1305 award, not stacked on top of it. If the ordinary award is 100 and special compensation calculates to 150, the salvor collects the ordinary 100 plus only the 50 excess. This mirrors the value-ceiling logic of Art. 1304(3): both rules keep the salvage-reward system from becoming a source of double recovery.

## Forfeiture, Recourse, and General Average

Art. 1312(5) mirrors Art. 1311's forfeiture logic in a narrower form: a salvor may lose special compensation, wholly or partly, if its own negligence caused it to fail to prevent or limit environmental damage — narrower than Art. 1311 because it targets only the environmental duty of care, not the fuller range of fault or dishonesty. Art. 1312(6) preserves the owner's recourse against third parties actually responsible for the danger, and Art. 1312(7) excludes these payments from general average contribution: special compensation rewards the salvor's individual effort, not a cost shared among the ship, cargo, and freight interests the way a general average sacrifice would be.

## Court of Cassation Track Record

No Court of Cassation decision has been found applying Art. 1312's calculation mechanism — the 30%/100% uplift or the expense definition — to the merits of a case. Two indirect data points exist. First, an 11th Civil Chamber ruling in the Tibil vessel dispute (E.2014/11224, K.2014/13369, T.08.09.2014) confirmed that a special compensation claim qualifies as a maritime lien under Art. 1352(c), though the underlying case was ultimately decided on an ordinary Art. 1305 award, not special compensation. Second, an Istanbul 17th Commercial Court case (E.2015/27, K.2023/280, T.06.07.2023) expressly framed its subject matter as an Art. 1310/1312 claim, but was rejected at an earlier threshold — the claimants' own fault had caused the explosion they then responded to, so their conduct did not qualify as salvage activity under Art. 1298, and Art. 1312's mechanics were never reached.
