The opening articles of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK), Art. 1 through 5/A, set the interpretive framework for the entire Code. They establish that the TTK is inseparable from the Turkish Civil Code, define what counts as a "commercial provision," rank the sources a court must apply when no commercial provision exists, define "commercial transaction" and "commercial dispute," and fix which court hears commercial cases.

## What Counts as a Commercial Provision, and the Hierarchy of Sources

Under TTK Art. 1, a commercial provision is not limited to rules found in the TTK itself — any special rule in any statute that governs a commercial enterprise counts. Where no commercial provision applies, courts turn first to commercial custom, and only then to the general provisions of the Turkish Code of Obligations and Civil Code. For a custom to bind a court, it must be shown to be an established practice in the relevant trade or region — a mere habit is not enough, and courts typically verify this through the relevant chamber of commerce.

## Commercial Transactions and Commercial Disputes

Art. 3 defines a commercial transaction broadly: any matter regulated by the TTK, or any act connected to a commercial enterprise. Art. 4 then narrows this into "commercial dispute," which follows a two-track classification under Turkish case law. In "relative" commercial disputes, what matters is that the matter concerns the commercial enterprises of both parties. In "absolute" commercial disputes, the law itself designates certain subject matters (negotiable instruments, intellectual property, banking transactions) as commercial regardless of whether the parties are merchants at all.

## Jurisdiction and Mandatory Mediation

Under Art. 5, commercial courts of first instance (asliye ticaret mahkemesi) have jurisdiction over commercial cases regardless of amount. Since a 2019 amendment, Art. 5/A makes prior application to a mediator a procedural precondition for commercial claims involving a sum of money — debt collection, compensation, or restitution. A case filed without first attempting mediation is dismissed on procedural grounds without even being served on the defendant.

Foreign companies contracting with a Turkish merchant should note that mandatory mediation is not optional paperwork: skipping it can be fatal to a claim regardless of its merits, and Turkish courts apply this strictly — including in disputes over negotiable instruments, which the Court of Cassation has repeatedly confirmed fall under this mediation requirement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is mediation compulsory in a commercial action?

Where the subject matter is a sum of money, yes. Under Article 5/A TTK, added in 2019 by Law No. 7155, <b>in commercial actions specified in Article 4 and in other laws whose subject matter is a sum of money — claims for debt, damages, annulment of objection, negative declaratory relief and restitution — having applied to a mediator before bringing the action is a condition of action</b>. An action brought without meeting that condition is dismissed on procedural grounds without being served on the other party. The mediator must conclude the application within <b>six weeks</b>; in compelling cases the period may be extended by at most two weeks.

### Which actions count as commercial actions?

Under Article 4/1 TTK commercial actions fall into two groups. <b>Relative commercial actions</b>: civil actions and non-contentious matters arising from issues relating to the commercial enterprises of both parties. <b>Absolute commercial actions</b>: actions arising, irrespective of whether the parties are traders, from matters regulated in the TTK and from the laws listed in the article (such as pledge-backed lending, intellectual property, exchanges and commercial goods). Under Article 5/1 these are heard by the <b>commercial court of first instance</b>.

### Is commercial usage evidence before the court?

Not directly. Under Article 2/1 TTK, <b>unless the law provides otherwise, a usage cannot form the basis of the court's judgment unless it has been established to be accepted as a commercial custom</b>. For a practice to be taken into account it is therefore not enough that it be widespread; it must have acquired the quality of <b>commercial custom</b>. Moreover, under Article 2/3, commercial custom applies to persons who are not traders only where they knew or ought to have known of it.
