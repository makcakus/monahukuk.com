Genocide opens the "International Crimes" book of Turkey's Penal Code, Law No. 5237 (TCK), and it is treated by the legislature as categorically different from ordinary violent crime. Article 76 of the TCK, read together with the general provisions on limitation periods, places genocide outside the reach of any statute of limitations — a prosecution may be brought however many decades have passed since the acts occurred. This article explains how Turkish law defines the offense, what distinguishes it from the neighboring crime of persecution against humanity, and why the legislature chose to remove the time bar altogether. It draws on the statutory text of Article 76 and the small but consistent body of Court of Cassation (Yargıtay) case law that has touched the offense, mainly in the context of extradition and procedural safeguards rather than domestic convictions.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Most crimes in the TCK protect an individual's life, body, or property. Genocide is structured differently: the law protects the continued existence of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as a collective. The acts that make up the offense — killing, causing serious harm, and the other conduct listed in Article 76 — are necessarily carried out against individual members of the group, but the object of protection is the group's survival as such, not merely the sum of the individual victims. This collective dimension is what separates genocide analytically from an ordinary case of intentional homicide or aggravated bodily harm, even where the underlying physical conduct looks identical.

## The Conduct That Constitutes Genocide

Article 76 is not limited to killing. It lists several alternative forms of conduct, any one of which is sufficient to complete the offense once the other elements are present: intentional killing of group members; inflicting serious harm on their physical or mental integrity; deliberately subjecting the group to living conditions calculated to bring about its destruction, in whole or in part; imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; and forcibly transferring children belonging to the group to another group. Because the statute frames these as alternatives rather than cumulative requirements, a single qualifying act — for example, forced child transfer alone, without any killing — can in principle satisfy the conduct element.

As for who can commit the offense, Article 76 imposes no special status requirement on the perpetrator. Genocide is not limited to state officials or persons in positions of authority, even though in practice it is almost invariably carried out by those who control organized force. Organizing or leading a group specifically to commit these crimes is treated separately: TCK Article 78 criminalizes that conduct as an independent offense in its own right.

The victims, by contrast, are defined narrowly. The group targeted must be national, ethnic, racial, or religious. Political, social, economic, or other categories of groups fall outside this definition entirely — a mass atrocity directed at a political faction or social class, however severe, does not meet the victim-group requirement of Article 76 unless the group also happens to coincide with one of the four protected categories.

## The Plan Requirement

Article 76 further requires that the qualifying conduct be carried out "through the execution of a plan." This is a structural requirement, not a mere aggravating detail: an isolated, unplanned killing or act of violence against a member of a protected group — no matter how brutal — does not amount to genocide under Turkish law if it lacks this organized, systematic character. The plan element is what elevates the underlying acts from ordinary individual crimes to an offense understood as an assault on a group's existence, and it operates alongside, not instead of, the special intent discussed below.

## Special Intent: The Element That Defines the Offense

The feature that most sharply distinguishes genocide from every other crime in the TCK is its mental element. Ordinary intent to commit the listed act is not enough. Article 76 requires that the perpetrator act with the specific purpose of destroying the targeted group, in whole or in part — what Turkish doctrine, following the international literature, calls dolus specialis, or special/particular intent. Without this destructive purpose, the very same physical conduct simply constitutes the underlying ordinary offense — intentional homicide, aggravated bodily harm, and so on — rather than genocide.

Two consequences follow from this design. First, the offense does not require that the group actually be destroyed, or even that the campaign come close to succeeding: it is complete once a qualifying act is committed with the necessary destructive purpose, regardless of the ultimate outcome. Second, this makes genocide a purpose-based (or intent-based) offense rather than a result-based one — its legal center of gravity sits in what the perpetrator meant to achieve, not in how much damage was actually inflicted.

## Distinguishing Genocide from Persecution Against Humanity

In practice, genocide (Article 76) is frequently confused with the neighboring offense of persecution against humanity (Article 77), and the two share real structural similarities: both are typically carried out pursuant to an organized plan and in systematic fashion. Two distinctions nevertheless separate them. First, genocide requires the special intent to destroy a specific protected group; persecution against humanity requires no such destructive purpose. Second, the two offenses protect different classes of victims — genocide is confined to national, ethnic, racial, or religious groups, whereas persecution against humanity reaches acts committed against "a segment of society" for political, philosophical, racial, or religious motives, a broader and less rigidly defined category. Because of the added special-intent requirement, genocide is sometimes described in Turkish scholarship as a qualified, more demanding variant sitting alongside persecution against humanity, rather than as a wholly separate category of harm.

## Attempt, Participation, and Concurrence

Ordinary rules on inchoate and multi-actor liability apply to genocide with certain adaptations specific to the offense:

- **Attempt.** Where execution of one of the qualifying acts has begun but the offense is not completed for reasons beyond the perpetrator's control, the general rules on attempt apply.
- **Participation.** Instigation and complicity follow the general provisions of the TCK. Establishing, leading, or joining an organization for the purpose of committing these crimes, however, is punished separately and independently under Article 78.
- **Concurrence of offenses.** Article 76(2) provides that, for the intentional killings and intentional injuries committed within the scope of a genocide campaign, the rules on genuine concurrence apply according to the number of victims identified — meaning a separate penalty is fixed for each individual victim rather than a single composite sentence for the campaign as a whole.

## Sanctions and Distinctive Procedural Features

A person convicted of genocide faces aggravated life imprisonment under Article 76(2). Legal entities can also be made subject to security measures in connection with the offense, under Article 76(3).

The most distinctive procedural feature of the offense is found in Article 76(4): genocide is never subject to a statute of limitations. However much time has elapsed since the acts occurred, a prosecution remains legally available. Genocide additionally falls within the universal jurisdiction regime set out in TCK Article 13, meaning Turkish criminal law can apply even where the offense was committed abroad by a foreign national, with no ordinary territorial or nationality link to Turkey required. In extradition proceedings, genocide is also expressly excluded from being treated as a "political offense" — a classification that exists precisely so that individuals responsible for genocide cannot escape accountability by fleeing across a border and invoking the political-offense exception that shields many other categories of alleged wrongdoing from extradition. Jurisdiction to try the offense at first instance lies with the heavy penal courts (ağır ceza mahkemesi).

## The State of Turkish Case Law on Genocide

Reported Court of Cassation case law resulting in an actual conviction for genocide is essentially nonexistent in Turkey. This is largely a function of how narrow the offense's constitutive elements are — the special destructive intent and the organized-plan requirement set a high evidentiary bar — combined with the fact that domestic prosecutions charging this specific offense are rare. Where the Court of Cassation has engaged with genocide at all, it has generally done so at the margins: confirming that genocide cannot be treated as a political offense for extradition purposes, situating it within the enhanced seizure regime applicable to a defined list of serious offenses under the Code of Criminal Procedure, and — separately — addressing where the boundary lies between public discussion of the term "genocide" and the criminal law of expression. The extradition-related rulings are the most consistent and frequently cited line of authority: they confirm, across multiple nationalities and underlying facts, that genocide and persecution against humanity are carved out of the political-offense exception under Article 11 of Law No. 6706, which governs international judicial cooperation in criminal matters. Separately, the Court has confirmed that genocide sits within the catalogue of serious offenses subject to the heightened, unanimous-panel seizure procedure under Article 128 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CMK).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a genocide prosecution in Turkey ever become time-barred?**
No. Article 76(4) of the TCK expressly excludes genocide from any statute of limitations, so the passage of time, however long, does not prevent prosecution.

**Does genocide require that the targeted group actually be destroyed?**
No. The offense is defined by the perpetrator's special intent to destroy the group, in whole or in part. Committing one of the listed qualifying acts with that destructive purpose is sufficient; the group's actual, physical destruction is not a required outcome.

**How is genocide different from persecution against humanity under Turkish law?**
The core distinction is intent and victim class. Genocide requires a specific intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Persecution against humanity, covering acts against a broader "segment of society" motivated by political, philosophical, racial, or religious grounds, does not require that destructive purpose.

**Can someone accused of genocide avoid extradition to Turkey by claiming it is a political offense?**
No. Turkish extradition law, and the Court of Cassation decisions applying it, expressly exclude genocide and persecution against humanity from the political-offense exception, precisely to prevent this kind of escape from accountability.
