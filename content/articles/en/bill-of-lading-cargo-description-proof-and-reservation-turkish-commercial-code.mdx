Few provisions in maritime law generate more litigation than the "clean bill of lading" rule. TTK Art. 1239 governs how binding the bill's statements about cargo type, marks, quantity, and apparent condition really are.

## The Duty to Qualify

Under Art. 1239(1), a carrier must add a reservation to the bill if it knows, has reasonable grounds to doubt, or simply lacks a reasonable means of checking that the stated cargo matches what was actually received or loaded. That third trigger — no reasonable means to check — is what makes container shipping workable: a carrier receiving a sealed, shipper-packed container cannot open it, so the law lets the carrier qualify the description with wording like "shipper's load, stow, count and seal" rather than forcing it to underwrite a description it cannot verify.

## Silence on Apparent Condition Cuts Against the Carrier

Art. 1239(2) flips the incentive the other way: if the carrier says nothing about the cargo's externally visible condition, the bill is deemed to state the cargo was received in apparent good condition. A carrier that notices visible damage and simply omits mention of it does not get the benefit of the doubt — silence is read as the most carrier-unfavorable statement available.

## Conclusive Against Good-Faith Holders

Art. 1239(3) is the doctrinal core: absent a valid reservation, the bill creates a presumption that the carrier received (or loaded) the cargo exactly as described. Between the carrier and the shipper, this presumption is ordinarily rebuttable. But against a good-faith third party who relied on the description — including the consignee — it cannot be rebutted at all. This asymmetry is what makes documentary trade function: a bank paying against a letter of credit never sees the cargo, only the bill; if carriers could later claim they actually shipped less, no bank or buyer could safely rely on a clean bill. The price of that reliability is the carrier's duty to qualify whenever it has doubts.

## Practical Note

Turkish courts have applied this doctrine consistently in container-shortage and container-damage disputes, confirming that a carrier who issues a "clean on board" bill without reservation bears the loss even where independent surveyors later confirm a shortfall — and equally that a carrier is not liable for damage from a defect inside a sealed container it had no ability to inspect.
