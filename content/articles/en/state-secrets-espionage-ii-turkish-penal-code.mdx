Turkish criminal law does not treat state-security offenses as a single, uniform category of "espionage." Alongside the classic espionage offenses defined in Articles 326 through 332 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), a second and structurally distinct group of offenses appears in Articles 333 through 339. These provisions target conduct that falls short of full-fledged espionage but still exposes the state's external security, diplomatic standing, or military interests to danger — a public official misusing confidential information for a foreign state's benefit, a person procuring or disclosing information the law has placed off-limits, careless handling that lets a secret slip into foreign hands, or the mere unauthorized possession of a sensitive document. For foreign nationals, businesses, and counsel encountering a Turkish criminal investigation in this area, understanding how these six articles relate to one another — and where the line between a minor and a serious charge is drawn — is essential to assessing exposure and building a defense.

## The Legal Interest Protected and Where This Group Fits

The chapter of the TCK dealing with offenses against state secrets and espionage is structured in two layers. Articles 326-332 punish the paradigm case of espionage — acting on behalf of, or in coordination with, a foreign state to obtain or transmit protected information. Articles 333-339 punish a broader and more varied set of behaviors that share a common feature: none of them require the fully developed intent structure of classic espionage, yet each still threatens the same interest, namely the internal and external security of the state and the integrity of public service. This second tier criminalizes intermediate stages and lesser degrees of culpability — misuse of confidential information short of transmission to a foreign power, procurement or disclosure of legally restricted information, careless conduct that allows a secret to reach a foreign state, and bare unauthorized possession of a sensitive document — so that conduct just below the threshold of espionage does not escape criminal liability altogether.

## Article 333: Disloyalty in State Service

Article 333 addresses the public official (or any person who has learned a secret through the performance of official duties) who uses confidential information relating to state security — such as an unpublished scientific discovery or industrial innovation — for the benefit of a foreign state or its agents, or who enables another person to do so. The offense differs from espionage in a subtle but important way: it does not require that the information actually be transmitted to a foreign state, only that it be used or exploited for that state's benefit. The center of gravity of the offense is therefore the breach of the duty of loyalty inherent in public service, rather than the act of communication itself. The offense requires direct intent — the person must know both the confidential nature of the information and that its use serves a foreign state's interest. Aggravated penalties apply where the act benefits a state at war with Turkey or endangers Turkey's war preparations, military operations, or effectiveness, and a separate, lesser penalty attaches to anyone who learns in advance that one of these offenses is about to be committed and fails to report it to the authorities in time.

## Articles 334 and 335: Procuring Prohibited Information — Intent as the Dividing Line

Articles 334 and 335 form the doctrinal core of this group of offenses, because they demonstrate how Turkish law uses a single mental-state distinction to separate a lesser offense from a substantially more serious one. Both articles punish procuring information that a statute has expressly declared off-limits for disclosure. Article 334 covers the basic case: the person obtains the restricted information knowing it is confidential, but without any specific intent to use it for a foreign state's benefit. Article 335 covers exactly the same physical conduct — procuring the same category of restricted information — but treats it as a materially more serious offense whenever it is committed with an espionage purpose, meaning the person acts with the specific intent of benefiting a foreign state or its agents. The only element separating the two offenses is this special intent. In practice, proving that intent is rarely a matter of direct admission; prosecutors typically rely on circumstantial evidence — who the person was in contact with, whether there was an attempt to pass the information onward, and the content of relevant communications — to establish the espionage purpose.

## Articles 336 and 337: Disclosure of Prohibited Information

Turkish law treats procuring information and disclosing it as two separate acts, and deliberately so: obtaining a piece of restricted information is one thing, but passing it on to a third party is what turns a latent risk into a realized one. Article 336 punishes the disclosure of prohibited information as the baseline offense. Article 337 mirrors the structure of Articles 334-335 by treating the same disclosure as a more serious offense when it is carried out with a political or military espionage purpose. The recipient of the disclosure can be a foreign state, its agent, or any person capable of passing the information on to a foreign state; it makes no difference whether the person disclosing the information does so in an official capacity or as a private individual, although a public official's involvement can raise separate questions about whether Article 333 also applies to the same conduct, and how the two offenses should be reconciled in sentencing.

## Article 338: Negligent Disclosure — Where Intent Gives Way to Carelessness

Article 338 is the most distinctive provision in this group, because the offenses surrounding it are, without exception, intentional crimes, while Article 338 punishes a negligent variant. Here, the person has no intention of getting information into foreign hands; rather, a document or piece of information that should have remained confidential ends up in the possession of a foreign state because the person failed to exercise the degree of care their position required. The outcome — the information reaching a foreign state — functions as an objective element of the offense, and a causal link must be established between the careless conduct and that outcome. Because it applies the general criminal-law distinction between intent and negligence to the specific context of national security, this article generates some of the most difficult evidentiary and doctrinal questions in the chapter, and it carries a materially lighter penalty than the intentional offenses around it.

## Article 339: Unauthorized Possession of State-Security Documents

Article 339 goes a step further than procurement or disclosure: it punishes the unauthorized possession itself of documents that must remain confidential because of the state's security or its internal or external political interests — without requiring proof that the person did anything with the document beyond holding it. This is a classic abstract-danger offense: the legislature has decided that unauthorized possession of such material is, by itself, a sufficient security risk, regardless of whether it is ever put to improper use. The conduct element is simply possessing the document. The person's underlying purpose for holding it — deliberate preparation for espionage, careless retention by an overly curious official, or accidental acquisition — does not affect whether the offense has been committed, but it can significantly affect how a court assesses intent and determines the sentence in a given case.

## Fault, Attempt, and Participation Across the Group

Articles 333 through 337 and Article 339 require direct intent; Article 338 is the sole exception, built around negligence. As for attempt, the acts of procuring and disclosing information under Articles 334-337 are capable of being broken down into distinct stages of execution, so attempt liability can apply to them; possession under Article 339, by contrast, is a crime that is complete the moment unauthorized possession begins, which sharply limits the scope for an attempt charge. General rules on participation and complicity apply throughout, though Article 333's reliance on the public official's duty of loyalty can raise complex questions when an outside foreign agent instigates the official's conduct, potentially bringing the espionage provisions (Articles 328 et seq.) into play alongside Article 333. Where the same piece of information is both procured and later disclosed by the same person, whether this amounts to a single offense or to multiple offenses charged cumulatively depends on the specific chain of conduct and outcome in the case at hand.

## Sentencing and Procedural Features

The legislature has built a graduated sentencing structure into this group of offenses that tracks the intensity of the underlying intent. The offenses committed with an espionage purpose — procuring information under Article 335 and disclosing it under Article 337 — carry heavier penalties than their baseline counterparts covering the identical physical conduct without that special intent (Articles 334 and 336, respectively). The negligent offense under Article 338 sits at the opposite end of the scale, carrying the lightest penalty in the chapter. Because these offenses are directed against state security, prosecution does not depend on a private complaint; the public prosecutor investigates and pursues these cases on its own initiative. Given the sensitive subject matter, proceedings frequently involve confidentiality orders and expert examination of the material at issue. A threshold question in almost every case is whether the information or document in question actually qualifies as a "state secret" or "information whose disclosure is prohibited" as a matter of law — a determination that courts typically make only after consulting the relevant public institution.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the practical difference between Article 334 and Article 335 if the underlying conduct — procuring restricted information — is the same?**
The physical act is identical in both provisions. The dividing line is the person's purpose: Article 334 applies when the information is procured without any specific intent to benefit a foreign state, while Article 335 applies to the same conduct when it is carried out with an espionage purpose, and it carries a substantially heavier penalty as a result.

**Can someone be prosecuted under Article 338 without ever intending to help a foreign state?**
Yes. Article 338 is a negligence offense. It applies precisely to situations where a person's carelessness — rather than any intention to assist a foreign power — allows confidential material to end up in foreign hands. The absence of intent does not eliminate liability; it changes the applicable article and results in a lighter sentence.

**Is simply holding a classified document a crime even if it is never shown to anyone?**
Under Article 339, yes. The offense is complete upon unauthorized possession of a document that must remain confidential for reasons of state security or political interest. No further act of disclosure or use is required for the offense to be established, although the person's actual purpose in holding the document remains relevant to sentencing.

**Do these cases require the state to prove the information was formally classified?**
Establishing that the information or document genuinely qualifies as a state secret, or falls within a category of legally prohibited disclosure, is typically a preliminary issue that courts resolve with input from the relevant public institution before assessing the elements of the specific offense charged.
