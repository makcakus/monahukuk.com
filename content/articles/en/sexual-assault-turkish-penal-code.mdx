Sexual assault is one of the offenses foreign nationals in Turkey are most likely to encounter directly — either as a defendant facing a criminal investigation after an incident on holiday or at work, or as a complainant seeking to understand how a report will be processed. The offense is set out in Article 102 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), which was substantially rewritten in 2014 by Law No. 6545. That reform did two important things: it introduced a formal, graded penalty structure running from a lower-level form of physical contact up to acts involving penetration, and it gave express statutory recognition to a category the Turkish courts had long wrestled with informally — brief, non-continuous acts of a sexual nature, now treated as a distinct and more lightly punished degree of the offense. Because the classification of an act — and not just its occurrence — determines both the sentencing range and whether the case can proceed at all without a complaint, this is an area where precise legal characterization matters as much as the underlying facts.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Article 102 sits among the offenses against individuals — not among offenses against public morality — and this placement is deliberate. What the provision protects is the individual's bodily inviolability and, more broadly, their sexual autonomy: the right to decide, through their own free will, matters relating to their own sexuality. Because the protected interest is personal rather than a matter of collective morality, whether the offense has occurred is assessed solely by reference to whether the complainant's will was violated — not by reference to any moral judgment about the complainant's conduct, character, or reputation.

## Who Can Commit the Offense and Who Can Be a Victim

Article 102 is a general offense: it can be committed by anyone, and the law does not require the perpetrator to hold any particular status. The sex of either the perpetrator or the victim is, as a rule, legally irrelevant to whether the offense exists. Certain personal relationships between perpetrator and victim do matter, however, at the sentencing stage — a perpetrator who abuses the influence conferred by a public office, a guardianship, or a position of service, or who holds a defined family relationship to the victim (stepfather, stepmother, stepsibling, adoptive parent, or adopted child), faces an aggravated penalty rather than a change in the basic classification of the offense.

On the victim side, Article 102 applies only to victims who have reached eighteen years of age. Sexual acts directed at a child fall instead under the separate offenses of sexual abuse of a minor or sexual intercourse with a minor, which carry their own — generally harsher — regime. A marital relationship between the parties does not remove the offense; the law's only concession to marriage is procedural, addressed below. A victim who cannot defend themselves because of a physical or mental condition, and a victim who is a blood or in-law relative of the perpetrator up to the third degree, both trigger the aggravated sentencing bracket rather than a separate offense.

## The Conduct Required and the Sarkıntılık (Molestation) Distinction

The physical conduct that Article 102 punishes is defined as a violation of a person's bodily inviolability through conduct of a sexual nature. Two elements must be present together: the conduct must be sexual in character, and it must be directed at the victim's body. Sexual remarks or behavior that never involve physical contact fall outside Article 102 altogether — they may instead constitute the separate offense of sexual harassment (TCK Art. 105), discussed below. The prevailing view in Turkish legal scholarship treats this as a "pure conduct" offense: it is complete once the physical act occurs, and no further harm or consequence needs to be shown. The absence of the victim's consent, although not spelled out in the text of the article, is treated as an essential — if unwritten — element of the offense; the dominant position is that where the victim was unable to meaningfully express their will, a separate showing of force, threat, or deception is not additionally required.

The single most consequential distinction within Article 102 is the line between the basic form of the offense and what Turkish law calls sarkıntılık — commonly rendered as molestation or groping. The statute itself sets two different penalty bands within its first paragraph: a heavier sentence for the ordinary form of the offense, and a markedly lighter sentence where the conduct remains at the level of sarkıntılık. Turkish courts and legal writing define sarkıntılık as sudden, isolated, and non-continuous acts of a sexual nature. Conduct that is sustained, repeated, or carried on with persistence against the victim's body, by contrast, is treated as the ordinary (more serious) form. There is no fixed rule for telling the two apart — courts weigh the duration of the act, its intensity, and whether it was interrupted, on the specific facts of each case.

## Aggravated Forms of the Offense

Article 102 sets out several tiers of aggravation. The most serious is treated as an independent, aggravated form of the offense rather than a sentencing adjustment to the basic act: where the conduct involves penetration of the body by an organ or any other object, the law prescribes a substantially higher minimum sentence. Notably, where this specific form of the offense is committed against a spouse, the case may only be investigated and prosecuted upon the victim's complaint — this is the one point at which the marital relationship affects the procedural regime, though it does not affect whether the underlying conduct is unlawful.

A separate set of aggravating circumstances increases the sentence otherwise applicable (to either the basic or the penetrative form) by half. These apply where the offense is committed: against a victim who cannot defend themselves due to a physical or mental condition; by abusing the influence of a public office, guardianship, or service relationship; against a relative by blood or marriage up to the third degree, or by a stepparent, stepsibling, adoptive parent, or adopted child; with a weapon or by more than one person acting together; or by taking advantage of an environment where people are compelled to live communally.

Two further rules address especially severe outcomes. Where the force or violence used to commit the assault itself causes the aggravated consequences associated with intentional injury, the separate provisions on intentional injury apply in addition to the sexual assault charge. And where the offense results in the victim entering a vegetative state or in the victim's death, the law imposes aggravated life imprisonment — the most severe sentence available under Turkish law.

## Attempt, Complicity, and Concurrence

Because the conduct covered by Article 102 can often be broken down into discrete stages, an attempted offense is legally possible: where the perpetrator has begun the act but fails to complete it for reasons beyond their control, the case is treated as an attempt and sentenced on a reduced scale. Attempt is also recognized in the penetrative form of the offense, which raises a further classification question — distinguishing a completed basic-form assault from an attempted penetrative assault can materially change the applicable sentence.

All ordinary forms of complicity apply to this offense, and the fact that the act was carried out by more than one person acting together is treated separately as an aggravating circumstance in its own right, rather than merely as a form of participation. Where the perpetrator commits the same type of act against the same victim on separate occasions pursuant to a single continuing decision, the general rule on continuing (repeated) offenses may come into play, since sexual assault is not among the offenses excluded from that rule; separately, where the force used causes the aggravated consequences of intentional injury, both offenses are charged concurrently rather than one being absorbed into the other.

## Penalties and Procedural Rules

The basic form of the offense carries a substantial prison sentence, with a markedly reduced range where the conduct is classified as sarkıntılık. The penetrative form carries a materially higher minimum sentence. Where the offense causes the victim's death or a vegetative state, the law imposes aggravated life imprisonment.

The complaint regime under Article 102 is split. Both the basic form and the sarkıntılık form described in the first paragraph may only be prosecuted on the victim's complaint. The penetrative form described in the second paragraph, by contrast, is prosecuted by the authorities on their own initiative — the sole exception being where it is committed against a spouse, in which case a complaint is again required. Where a complaint is required, it must be filed within six months of the date the victim learned of the act and the identity of the perpetrator, in line with the general statute-of-limitations rule for complaint-based offenses under Article 73 of the Penal Code. Mediation is not available for offenses against sexual inviolability, even where the offense is one that requires a complaint. As to which court has jurisdiction, Turkish procedural rules generally look to the statutory maximum sentence, disregarding any aggravating or mitigating adjustments: cases falling under the first paragraph are heard by the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi), while cases involving the penetrative form or the death/vegetative-state consequence are heard by the heavy penal court (ağır ceza mahkemesi). Applicable limitation periods for prosecution are likewise calculated under the general rules, by reference to how the specific conduct is classified and the maximum sentence attached to it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the practical difference between sarkıntılık (molestation) and the basic form of sexual assault?**
Both require physical contact of a sexual nature and both are prosecuted only on complaint, but they carry very different sentencing ranges. Courts distinguish them by looking at whether the conduct was a brief, isolated, interrupted act (sarkıntılık) or a sustained, repeated, or persistent one (the basic form). This is assessed case by case; there is no bright-line test.

**Can sexual assault charges proceed if the victim later withdraws the complaint?**
It depends on which paragraph of Article 102 applies. Because the basic form and the sarkıntılık form require a complaint, a genuine and timely withdrawal can end the case. The penetrative form, however, is prosecuted independently of any complaint (except where the victim is the perpetrator's spouse), so withdrawal does not by itself terminate proceedings for that form.

**How is sexual assault different from sexual harassment under Turkish law?**
The dividing line is physical contact. Sexual assault under Article 102 requires conduct that touches or otherwise physically violates the victim's body. Sexual remarks, gestures, or other sexually charged conduct that involve no bodily contact are instead considered under the separate offense of sexual harassment (Article 105), which carries a different — generally lower — penalty range.

**Does a family or guardianship relationship between the parties change the charge?**
It does not create a separate offense, but it does increase the sentence. Where the perpetrator is a stepparent, stepsibling, adoptive parent or adopted child of the victim, a relative by blood or marriage up to the third degree, or someone who abused the authority of a public office, guardianship, or service relationship, the applicable sentence — whether for the basic or the penetrative form — is increased by half.
