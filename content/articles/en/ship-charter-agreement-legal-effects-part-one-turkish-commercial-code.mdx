TTK Art. 1122-1126 spell out what a bareboat (demise) charter concretely obligates each side to do — a direct consequence of the charterer taking over operational control.

## Third-Party Claims Follow the Charterer

Under Art. 1122, the charterer must indemnify the lessor for any claim third parties bring against the lessor arising from operation of the ship. Because the charterer runs the ship commercially, this internal reimbursement duty tracks the general principle that whoever holds authority and benefit should also bear the resulting risk — Turkish courts have applied this squarely, holding a bareboat charterer liable (not the registered owner) for operating costs and even environmental damage from a sinking, because the charterer was the one running the ship for its own commercial account.

## Delivery, Repairs, and Ongoing Costs

Art. 1123 requires the lessor to deliver the ship seaworthy and fit for the contract's purpose at the agreed time and place. Art. 1124 then splits costs based on cause: repairs stemming from a defect existing at delivery fall on the lessor, while ordinary maintenance, minor repairs, and running costs fall on the charterer; if the ship is immobilized more than 24 hours by its own defect, no charter fee is owed for the excess period.

## Using the Ship and Insuring It

Art. 1125 lets the charterer use the ship freely within the contract's purpose, including equipment left aboard by the lessor, provided equivalent quantity and quality is returned at the end. Art. 1126 requires the charterer to insure marine and liability risks until redelivery and to name the lessor in the policy on a "for whomever it concerns" basis — ensuring insurance proceeds follow the real interest-holder at the time of loss, not whoever happened to hold title when the policy was written.

## Practical Takeaway

For charterers, the cost-splitting rule in Art. 1124 means budgeting for ordinary wear and running costs as a matter of course, while the delivery warranty in Art. 1123 gives real leverage if a defect surfaces that predates handover — Turkish courts have confirmed that a lessor who delivered a seaworthy ship is not liable for costs arising from the charterer's own operation of it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who bears liability arising from operation under a ship charter?

The charterer. Under TTK Article 1122/1, <b>the charterer is obliged to meet all claims that third parties may bring against the lessor arising from the operation of the vessel</b>. In a ship charter (bareboat charter), the vessel is delivered to the charterer unmanned, and the charterer operates it by employing the crew themselves; for this reason, under Article 1061/2, the charterer is treated as the <b>shipowner (donatan)</b> in relations with third parties.

### Who bears the cost of repairs to the vessel?

Under TTK Article 1124/1, <b>the costs of repairs arising from defects in the vessel and of parts replaced for this reason are borne by the lessor</b>. Conversely, expenses arising from the ordinary use, operation and maintenance of the vessel fall on the charterer. The distinction rests on the difference between a defect existing at the time of delivery and wear and tear arising later from use; the risk of a defect present at delivery lies with the lessor.

### Is it mandatory to take out insurance under a ship charter?

Yes. Under TTK Article 1126/1, <b>the charterer is obliged to take out insurance against the marine and liability risks that may arise until the vessel is returned, and to notify the lessor in advance that the insurance contract has been concluded</b>. The notification obligation exists so that the lessor can verify they are not left without cover. Failure to take out insurance gives the lessor the ability to exercise their rights under the contract.
