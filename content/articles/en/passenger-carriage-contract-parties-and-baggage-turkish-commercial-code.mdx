Three definitional provisions lay the groundwork for everything that follows in the passenger carriage chapter: who counts as carrier, who counts as passenger, and what counts as baggage.

## Carrier and Actual Carrier

Art. 1248 mirrors the cargo-carriage framework almost exactly: the "carrier" is whoever makes the carriage contract, or has it made on their account, regardless of who physically performs it. The "actual carrier" is a different person — the owner, charterer, or operator of a vessel — who physically carries out all or part of the carriage. This means a contracting carrier remains fully liable even after delegating performance to another operator; the delegation does not shrink the passenger's rights, it simply adds a second, parallel liability layer.

## Who Counts as a Passenger

Art. 1249(1) recognizes two categories: passengers under a direct passenger carriage contract, and — more interestingly — anyone the carrier allows aboard to look after a vehicle or livestock being carried under a separate cargo contract, such as a truck driver accompanying his own vehicle on a Ro-Ro ferry. Without this extension, such a person would fall through a gap: not cargo, but also not party to any passenger contract. Art. 1249(1) closes that gap by treating them as a passenger, with all the protective rules that follow. Separately, Art. 1249(2) makes a named ticket non-transferable — the holder cannot let someone else travel in their place.

## Baggage and the Cabin Baggage Distinction

Art. 1250(1) excludes livestock and cargo carried under a separate freight contract from "baggage" — baggage is only what travels as part of the passenger's own carriage contract. Within that category, Art. 1250(2) carves out **cabin baggage**: anything kept in the passenger's cabin or otherwise under their own possession, custody, or supervision, including items left in a passenger's own vehicle. This distinction matters because control determines liability standard — items the carrier never physically holds are treated differently from items formally handed over. Finally, Art. 1250(3) makes carriage of ordinary baggage presumptively included in the fare; extra charges require an explicit agreement.

## Why This Matters

For operators structuring ferry or cruise services, correctly classifying who is a "passenger" (including vehicle attendants) and distinguishing cabin baggage from checked baggage directly determines which liability standard applies later in the chapter.
