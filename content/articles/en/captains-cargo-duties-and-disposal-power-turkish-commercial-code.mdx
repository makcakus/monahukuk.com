TTK Art. 1111-1116 turn from the captain's relationship with the shipowner to the captain's duties toward cargo interests — a distinct axis of obligation running alongside statutory agency authority.

## No Loading for Personal Account

Art. 1111 bars the captain from loading cargo for personal account without the owner's consent — a clear conflict-of-interest rule, since cargo capacity belongs to the owner. Breach carries a deliberately punitive remedy: the captain owes the owner the *highest* freight obtainable for similar cargo at that time and place, not just what was actually earned, plus any further damages the owner can show.

## Protecting Cargo Interests En Route

Art. 1112 requires the captain to take all reasonable care to protect cargo during the voyage, seeking instructions from cargo interests where possible and acting on own judgment (with prompt notice) where not. In genuinely severe situations, the captain may unload, sell, or pledge cargo to prevent major loss, and may even pursue cargo-damage claims on behalf of interested parties who cannot act in time themselves. Art. 1113 lets the captain deviate from the planned route, pause the voyage, or return to the departure port if an unforeseen event blocks the planned course.

## Disposal Power Is Always Necessity-Gated

Beyond the cargo-preservation scenario of Art. 1112, Art. 1114 allows the captain to sell, pledge, or use cargo only where necessary to continue the voyage. Art. 1115 requires choosing, among available options for a general-average cash need, the one causing least harm to the parties involved. Art. 1116 (outside general average) permits disposal only if the cash need cannot otherwise be met, or if the alternative would cause the owner unbearable loss. Across all four provisions, the captain's power over someone else's property exists only to the extent genuine necessity demands — never as a matter of free discretion.

## Practical Takeaway

For cargo interests and insurers, these disposal powers are largely a relic of an era before real-time ship-to-shore communication; modern container shipping rarely triggers them, so disputes in this area are uncommon, but the necessity standard remains the controlling test whenever they are invoked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### May the master carry cargo on their own account?

No. Under Article 1111/1 TTK <b>the master may not load goods on the ship on their own account without the shipowner's consent</b>. The prohibition prevents the master from entering into a conflict of interest with the shipowner and from using the ship's capacity for their own trade. If goods are loaded without consent, the shipowner may demand that the benefit obtained be surrendered to it and may claim damages.

### May the master deviate from the route?

In case of necessity, yes. Under Article 1113/1 TTK, <b>where an unforeseen event prevents the voyage from being continued on the route being followed</b>, the master may, according to the requirements of the situation and any instructions that must be followed, <b>continue on another route, interrupt the voyage for a short or long period, or return to the port of departure</b>. A deviation without just cause, by contrast, aggravates the carrier's liability.

### May the master sell or pledge the cargo?

Only as a last resort. Under Article 1114/1 TTK, outside the cases in Article 1112, the master may dispose of the goods by sale, pledge or use <b>only where this is necessary for the continuation of the voyage</b>. Under Article 1115, if the need for funds arises from <b>general average</b>, the measure chosen must be the one <b>least harmful to the interests concerned</b>. Where there is no general average, Article 1116 lays down an even narrower framework, and under Article 1117 the shipowner compensates the loss of the cargo interests affected.
