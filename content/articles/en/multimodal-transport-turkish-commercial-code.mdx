TTK Art. 902-905 answer a question that comes up constantly in modern logistics: when a single contract covers goods moving by sea, then road, then possibly rail, which legal regime governs a loss — and does it matter where in the journey the loss happened?

## When the General Carriage Rules Apply

Art. 902 sets four cumulative conditions before Book Four's general carriage rules govern a multimodal contract: a single unified carriage contract, actual use of different vehicle types, a scenario where separate contracts for each leg would have been subject to at least two different legal regimes, and no overriding international convention. That last condition matters in practice — the CMR Convention's Article 2 applies CMR rules to the *entire* journey (not just the road leg) whenever a loaded road vehicle is carried by sea, rail, or air without unloading, displacing TTK's multimodal rules entirely for many Ro-Ro shipments.

## The Network System

Art. 903 is the operative mechanism: if the leg where the loss occurred is identifiable, the rules that would have governed a standalone contract for that leg apply instead of Book Four's general multimodal rules — the burden of proving where the loss occurred falls on whoever wants to invoke this. The Turkish Supreme Court's Grand General Assembly has applied this directly, sending a gas-turbine damage claim into CMR rules once the road leg where the accident occurred was pinpointed. Where the loss location cannot be established, the uniform Book Four regime governs the whole shipment by default.

## Notice, Limitation, and Household Goods

Art. 904 keeps notice and limitation stable regardless of whether the loss location is known — the general Art. 889 notice rule always applies, and limitation runs from actual delivery, with claims never expiring earlier than the Art. 855 minimum periods even where a stricter international regime would otherwise apply. Art. 905 resolves overlap with household-goods transport by giving the Art. 894-901 consumer-protective regime priority whenever the shipment qualifies as both multimodal and household goods — the network system only displaces it where a binding international convention applies.

## Practical Takeaway

A foreign party shipping goods into Turkey via combined sea-and-road or Ro-Ro transport should identify upfront whether CMR applies to the whole route — that single determination often decides which liability cap, exemption grounds, and limitation period actually govern the claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Under which provisions is loss compensated in multimodal carriage?

Under TTK Article 903/1, where it is <b>known in which part of the carriage</b> the event causing the loss, damage or delay in delivery occurred, the carrier's liability is determined not by the general provisions of the TTK but by <b>the provisions that would have applied had a separate contract of carriage been made for that part of the carriage</b>. Where the place of the loss is unknown, the general provisions of the TTK on the carriage of goods apply. In the literature this is called the 'network system'.

### Does not knowing where the loss occurred affect the notification period?

No. Under TTK Article 904/1, as regards notification of the loss, Article 889 applies <b>irrespective of whether the place of the loss is known or later becomes known</b>. Under Article 889, if apparent loss or damage is not notified <b>by the time of delivery at the latest</b>, and non-apparent loss <b>within seven days after delivery</b>, the goods are presumed to have been delivered in conformity with the contract; if the exceeding of the transit period is not notified <b>within twenty-one days of delivery</b>, rights arising from the delay are extinguished.

### Is a household removal subject to the multimodal provisions?

In part. Under TTK Article 905/1, where the subject matter of a contract of carriage by different modes is <b>removal goods</b>, the contract is governed by <b>the provisions of the Third Part of Book Six</b> (Articles 895-901 on the carriage of removal goods). Those provisions impose additional obligations on the carrier, such as packing, loading and assembly, and constitute a special regime protecting a consignor who is a consumer.
