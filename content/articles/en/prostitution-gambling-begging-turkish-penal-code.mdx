Turkish criminal law groups three seemingly unrelated forms of conduct — facilitating prostitution, organizing gambling, and exploiting vulnerable people through begging — under a single chapter of the Penal Code devoted to offenses against public morality. Articles 227 through 229 share a common structural feature that surprises many foreign observers: none of them criminalizes the underlying activity itself. A person who engages in prostitution, who gambles, or who begs is not, as such, committing a crime under these provisions. What the law punishes instead is the person who organizes, profits from, or exploits that situation. For foreign nationals or international counsel dealing with a Turkish criminal investigation touching on any of these three areas, understanding who the law actually targets — and how the aggravating circumstances work — is the starting point for assessing exposure.

## The Underlying Logic: Punishing Exploitation, Not the Underlying Conduct

The three offenses sit together in the Penal Code's chapter on crimes against public morals, but each protects a distinct interest. Article 227, on facilitating prostitution, is primarily concerned with public morality and, where a minor is involved, with the child's sexual development and dignity. Article 228, on providing venues or means for gambling, targets the social and financial harm that organized gambling causes to individuals and families. Article 229, on using vulnerable people for begging, protects the right of people who cannot look after themselves — because of age, disability, or mental incapacity — to live in society without being turned into a source of income for someone else. In each case, the legislature chose to leave the person actually engaged in the conduct outside the scope of criminal liability and instead reaches for the organizer, the facilitator, or the exploiter.

## Facilitating Prostitution: A Reversed Victim-Offender Structure

Article 227 punishes a range of acts — inciting a person into prostitution, easing the path toward it, procuring or housing someone for that purpose, or acting as an intermediary — but the person who actually engages in prostitution is not the one facing prosecution. Where the victim is a child, the law treats the matter with particular severity: encouraging, facilitating, procuring, housing, or acting as an intermediary for a child's prostitution carries a prison term of four to ten years plus a judicial fine of up to five thousand day-units, and preparatory acts toward this offense are punished as though the offense had already been completed — an unusually strict rule that moves criminal liability back to a stage the law normally treats only as an attempt. Because a child's consent to sexual matters has no legal weight given their age, consent is not a defense at all in this branch of the offense.

Where the victim is an adult, the same range of acts — incitement, facilitation, or acting as an intermediary or providing premises — carries a lighter sentence of two to four years' imprisonment and a fine of up to three thousand day-units. The law also treats living off, in whole or in part, the earnings of a person engaged in prostitution as a form of incitement in its own right, even without any direct act of procurement. A separate, lighter provision addresses the production, distribution, or dissemination of images, writings, or recordings prepared for the purpose of facilitating or intermediating prostitution, punishable by one to three years' imprisonment; liability here requires proof that the person acted knowing the material served that purpose, so a purely technical service — such as ordinary web hosting — does not, without more, satisfy this requirement.

## Aggravating Circumstances and the Line With Human Trafficking

Where force, threat, deception, or exploitation of a person's desperate circumstances is used to push someone into prostitution, the penalty under the paragraphs described above is increased by anywhere from half to double. This aggravated form brings Article 227 close to the separate offense of human trafficking under Article 80 of the Penal Code, but the two remain conceptually distinct: in human trafficking, the use of force, threat, deception, or exploitation of vulnerability to seize, harbor, or transport a person for exploitation is itself a constituent element of the crime. In the prostitution offense, by contrast, the base crime does not require any such coercive method at all — facilitation, incitement, or intermediation on its own is enough, and coercion or deception operates only as a factor that increases the sentence for an offense that already exists without it. The penalty is further increased by half where the offense is committed by a spouse, ascendant, in-law ascendant, sibling, adoptive parent, guardian, or another person with a duty of care, education, or supervision over the victim, or by abusing the influence of a public office or service relationship, and likewise increased by half where the offense is committed within an organized criminal group. Legal entities face security measures specific to them. Notably, the law takes a rehabilitative rather than punitive stance toward the person driven into prostitution, who may be made subject to medical or psychological treatment rather than any criminal sanction.

## Providing a Venue for Gambling: Where the Line Between Misdemeanor and Crime Falls

Article 228 does not target the person who gambles at all — playing games of chance is treated only as a minor administrative infraction under the Misdemeanors Law, not a crime. What the Penal Code punishes is the person who sets up the operation: providing the premises or the means for gambling to take place carries a sentence of one to three years' imprisonment together with a judicial fine of at least two hundred day-units. This sharp distinction between playing and organizing reflects the legislature's real target — not the individual player, but whoever turns gambling into a business. The penalty is increased by half where the venue or means is provided for children to gamble. A separate and considerably more serious aggravated form applies where the offense is committed through information systems — in other words, organizing online gambling — which carries three to five years' imprisonment and a judicial fine ranging from one thousand to ten thousand day-units. Where the offense is committed within an organized criminal group, the penalty is increased by half. For clarity in practice, the law defines gambling itself as games played for profit where gain or loss depends on chance; games in which skill predominates fall outside this definition.

## Exploitative Begging: Turning Vulnerability Into a Resource

Article 229 does not criminalize begging as such. It punishes using a child, or a person who cannot look after themselves because of a physical or mental condition, as an instrument for begging, with a sentence of one to three years' imprisonment. Courts read "using as an instrument" broadly: physically forcing the victim to beg is not a prerequisite — steering a vulnerable person toward begging and then living off the proceeds can satisfy the offense just as well. The penalty is increased by half where the offense is committed by a blood or in-law relative up to the third degree, or by a spouse, and increased by a full increment where it is committed within an organized criminal group. The heavier treatment reserved for offenses committed within the family reflects the particular weight Turkish criminal doctrine places on the abuse of a relationship of trust.

## Prosecution, Jurisdiction and Concurrence With Other Offenses

All three offenses sit within the Penal Code's chapter on crimes against public morality and are not subject to a complaint requirement — the public prosecutor opens an investigation of its own motion. Depending on the aggravating factors present and the resulting sentence range, prosecution proceeds before either the criminal court of first instance or the heavy penal court. Where the same conduct also satisfies the elements of a separate offense — such as sexual assault, sexual abuse of a minor, or human trafficking — the rules on concurrence of offenses apply, and the defendant is sentenced separately for each offense rather than having one absorb the other.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a person who works as a prostitute committing a crime in Turkey?**
No. Article 227 does not criminalize the act of engaging in prostitution. It punishes those who incite, facilitate, procure premises for, or act as an intermediary in another person's prostitution, or who live off the proceeds of it.

**Does someone who simply plays cards for money in a private setting face criminal liability?**
No, not under Article 228. Gambling itself is only an administrative misdemeanor. Criminal liability under the Penal Code attaches to the person who provides the venue or the means for the gambling to take place, not to the players.

**How is exploitative begging different from a family member simply asking someone to beg?**
The offense requires that a child, or a person unable to look after themselves due to a physical or mental condition, be used as an instrument for begging. When the offender is a close relative or spouse, the sentence is increased rather than reduced, because the law treats the abuse of a family relationship as an aggravating factor.

**Can facilitating prostitution turn into a human trafficking charge?**
The two offenses are legally distinct, though related. Human trafficking under Article 80 requires elements such as force, threat, deception, or exploitation of vulnerability used to seize, transport, or harbor a person for exploitation. Facilitating prostitution under Article 227 does not require any such coercive method as a base element — coercion or deception only raises the sentence for a crime that already exists independently of it. Whether a given set of facts supports one charge, the other, or both in concurrence depends on the specific acts proven.
