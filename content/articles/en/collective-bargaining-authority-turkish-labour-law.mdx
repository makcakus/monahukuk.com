Collective bargaining is the mechanism through which Turkish labour law replaces individual, one-on-one negotiation with organised, collective representation. But a trade union cannot simply present itself at the table and start bargaining: before any collective agreement can be signed, the union must first establish that it holds "authority" (yetki) to bargain for the specific workplace or enterprise in question. Law No. 6356 on Trade Unions and Collective Labour Agreements sets out this authority test in Article 41, using measurable, numerical thresholds rather than open-ended discretion. For foreign investors and HR managers running operations in Turkey, understanding how this authority is earned — and how it can be challenged — is essential, because no lawful bargaining process can begin until the question of authority is settled.

## What a Collective Bargaining Agreement Actually Covers

Under Article 33 of Law No. 6356, a collective bargaining agreement primarily contains normative provisions — rules governing how individual employment contracts are formed, their content, and how they end. Alongside these, the parties may add contractual (obligational) provisions dealing with their mutual rights and duties toward each other, how the agreement will be monitored and implemented, and how disputes between the signatory parties will be resolved. The law also recognises a separate instrument called a "framework agreement" (çerçeve sözleşme), which operates at the level of an entire line of work (işkolu) rather than an individual workplace. A framework agreement sets general principles on matters such as vocational training, occupational health and safety, corporate social responsibility and employment policy, and it can run for a minimum of one and a maximum of three years. Rather than binding individual employees, it binds the employer and worker constituencies represented by the signatory unions. Neither instrument may contain provisions contrary to the Constitution or to mandatory statutory rules — a reminder that the autonomy of collective bargaining in Turkey is broad, but not unlimited; it always operates within the floor set by public order rules.

## The Authority Test: The Sectoral Threshold and the Workplace/Enterprise Majority

The first paragraph of Article 41 makes authority conditional on two cumulative thresholds. The first operates at the macro level: the union must organise at least 1 percent of the workers employed in the line of work where the target workplace operates. This sectoral threshold is a proxy for genuine, sector-wide representative strength, and it is worth noting that it used to be considerably higher — a 2014 amendment (Law No. 6552) lowered it from 3 percent to 1 percent, meaningfully easing the path to unionisation. The second threshold is specific to the concrete workplace or enterprise being bargained for: the union must represent more than half of the employees at a single workplace, as of the application date, or 40 percent at the enterprise level (i.e., across all the workplaces that make up a given employer's operations in that line of work). The law deliberately sets a lower bar for enterprise-level agreements than for single-workplace agreements: when many workplaces are grouped together as one enterprise, workplaces are treated as a single unit for calculating the 40 percent share, because insisting on an absolute majority across a large, multi-site enterprise would in practice make authority almost unattainable. Where more than one union clears the 40 percent enterprise-level threshold, the union with the highest membership as of the application date is deemed authorised — resolving inter-union competition through a single, objective, statutory criterion rather than negotiation or discretion. The employer side of the authority equation is addressed in the same article: an employers' union is authorised to bargain for the workplaces belonging to its member employers, while an employer who does not belong to an employers' union is authorised to bargain for its own workplace or workplaces.

## Determining and Challenging the Sectoral Statistics

Applying the sectoral threshold in practice depends on reliable data about how many workers are employed in each line of work. For that reason, the Ministry of Labour publishes statistics every January and July showing the total number of workers in each line of work together with the membership figures of the unions active in it. These published statistics remain valid and binding for authority determinations and other transactions until replaced by the next set; critically, a union that has already applied for — or already obtained — an authority certificate is not affected by statistics published afterward, so a bargaining process already under way cannot be derailed by a later data release. If no objection is raised within fifteen days of publication, the statistics become final. If a party believes the statistics do not reflect reality, it may apply to the Ankara Labour Court within that same fifteen-day window, and the court is required to resolve the objection within fifteen days. This compressed timeline is not incidental: no collective bargaining process can lawfully begin until authority has been established, so the law is structured to prevent disputes over the underlying statistics from creating prolonged uncertainty at the threshold stage.

## Benefiting From the Agreement: Membership and Solidarity Dues

Once a collective bargaining agreement is signed by an authorised union, Article 39 of Law No. 6356 provides that, as a rule, only members of the signatory union benefit from it. Those who were already members on the signing date benefit from the agreement's effective date, while those who join afterward benefit from the date their membership is notified to the employer. The law nonetheless opens a route for non-members to share in the agreement's terms: an employee who was not a member of the signatory union on the signing date, or who joins the workplace afterward without becoming a member, or who has left or been expelled from the union, may still benefit from the agreement by paying a solidarity due (dayanışma aidatı), without needing the union's consent. Unlike membership, this route only takes effect from the date the request is made — it does not create retroactive rights. Until 2020, the statute contained a further sentence providing that a solidarity-due request made before the agreement's signing date would only take effect as of that signing date, rather than the earlier request date; the Constitutional Court struck that sentence down (30 December 2020, Case No. 2020/57, Decision No. 2020/83, published 3 March 2021) as an unjustified advantage for the signatory union that discouraged pluralism among unions, leaving the general "effective from the request date" rule to apply without exception. The amount of the solidarity due may not exceed the ordinary membership due and is set out in the union's own bylaws; it cannot be paid to a union whose activities have been suspended. In effect, this mechanism nudges workers toward union membership while still keeping the wider workforce from being entirely excluded from the benefits of collective bargaining.

## Extension (Teşmil): Applying the Agreement Beyond Its Original Scope

Article 40 of Law No. 6356 regulates "teşmil" — an exceptional mechanism that extends a collective bargaining agreement signed by an authorised union to other workplaces in the same line of work that do not otherwise have a collective agreement in place, by decision of the President. Extension is decided at the request of one of the parties in the relevant line of work, or of the Ministry of Labour, after the opinion of the Supreme Arbitration Board (Yüksek Hakem Kurulu) has been obtained, and the decision is published in the Official Gazette together with its reasoning; its effective date cannot be set earlier than the date of publication. If the extended agreement itself comes to an end, the extension decision automatically lapses along with it. Only the normative provisions of the agreement can be extended — the obligational provisions governing the rights and duties between the original signing parties, and any private-arbitration clauses, fall outside the scope of extension. Nor can an extension decision be issued for a workplace where an authority application is still pending or where a valid authority certificate already exists. This design confirms that extension is meant to supplement genuine unionisation and collective bargaining, not to substitute for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a union need both the sectoral threshold and the workplace majority, or just one?**
Both, cumulatively. A union must clear the 1 percent sectoral membership threshold in the relevant line of work and separately meet the workplace majority (more than 50 percent) or enterprise threshold (40 percent) for the specific workplace or enterprise it wants to bargain for. Meeting only one of the two conditions does not confer authority.

**What happens if two unions both clear the 40 percent threshold at enterprise level?**
Article 41 resolves this through membership numbers alone: the union with the highest number of members as of the application date is deemed authorised. The law does not provide for negotiation, proportional representation, or any other tie-breaking mechanism.

**Can a non-union employee still benefit from a collective bargaining agreement?**
Yes, by paying a solidarity due set out in the signatory union's bylaws, without needing the union's approval. This benefit runs only from the date the employee requests it, not retroactively, and — per settled case law — must be requested afresh for each new bargaining period; a request made for an earlier agreement does not automatically carry over to the next one.

**How quickly must an objection to the published sectoral statistics be resolved?**
An objection must be filed with the Ankara Labour Court within fifteen days of publication, and the court is required to decide it within fifteen days. This short, statutorily mandated timeline exists precisely because no collective bargaining process can start until the question of authority is settled.
