TTK Art. 1103-1110 answer a question that matters to anyone doing business with a ship at sea: when does the captain's signature actually bind the shipowner?

## Narrow at Home Port, Broad Away From It

Under Art. 1103, while the ship is at its home port, the captain's transactions do not bind the shipowner at all — except with special authorization or a separately binding cause — because the owner is presumed reachable there. Away from the home port, Art. 1104 flips this: the captain may bind the owner in any transaction connected to outfitting, fuel, crew, seaworthiness, and the safe continuation of the voyage, including making carriage contracts and litigating related matters. For foreign-flagged ships, Art. 1104(3) even lets claims against the owner be directed at the captain.

## Credit Transactions Need Necessity

Art. 1105 narrows this again for borrowing: the captain may take loans or credit only when necessary to preserve the ship or complete the voyage, and only for the amount actually needed. A third party dealing in good faith is protected even if the captain misuses the funds — but not if the third party knew, or was grossly negligent in not knowing, of the misuse. Bills of exchange are stricter still: under Art. 1105(3), the owner is only bound by the captain's bill obligations with express authorization — implied statutory authority is not enough.

## Limits, Termination, and Internal Duties

An owner can narrow the captain's authority (Art. 1106) or bar its use during a notice period after dismissal (Art. 1107), but these limits only bind third parties who actually knew of them. A captain acting outside authority using personal funds is treated as an ordinary third-party creditor of the owner (Art. 1108). Absent personal assumption of liability or an ultra vires act, the captain incurs no personal liability for authorized transactions — though personal fault liability under Art. 1088-1089 always survives independently (Art. 1109). Internally, the captain owes the owner regular reporting and must seek instructions for major decisions (Art. 1110).

## Practical Takeaway

For foreign counterparties extending credit or supplies to a Turkish-flagged ship away from home port, the necessity requirement is strictly enforced by Turkish courts — a captain refueling a seaworthy, voyage-ready ship generally lacks authority to bind the owner to that debt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where does the master's authority to represent the shipowner begin?

It depends on where the ship is. Under Article 1103/1 TTK <b>legal transactions made by the master while the ship is still in its home port do not bind the shipowner</b>, because the shipowner can be reached there. By contrast, under Article 1104/1, while the ship is <b>outside its home port</b> the master is authorised to carry out <b>all transactions and dispositions</b> relating to the equipping of the ship, its fuel and provisions, the crew, the maintenance of seaworthiness and the safe continuation of the voyage.

### May the master borrow on the ship's behalf?

Only within limits. Under Article 1105/1 TTK the master is authorised to borrow money, buy goods on credit and enter into similar credit transactions <b>only where this is necessary in order to preserve the ship or to perform the voyage, and only to the extent required to meet those needs</b>. Two limits operate together: <b>necessity</b> and <b>an amount proportionate to that need</b>. Exceeding them may mean that the transaction does not bind the shipowner.

### May the shipowner restrict the master's authority?

Internally yes; against third parties only to a limited extent. Under Article 1106/1 TTK <b>a shipowner who has restricted the master's statutory authority to represent may invoke the master's non-compliance with those restrictions only against persons who knew of them</b>. A third party in good faith is protected, and under Article 1109 the shipowner acquires rights and incurs obligations from transactions the master makes within their statutory powers.
