This article covers the final three provisions of the collision chapter: the master's post-collision duty to assist, two reserved-rights carve-outs, and the applicable prescription periods (TTK Art. 1295-1297).

## The Master's Duty to Assist

Art. 1295(1) requires the master of every ship involved in a collision to render assistance to the other ship, its crew, and its passengers, provided this is possible without seriously endangering the master's own ship, crew, and passengers. This reflects a long-standing maritime and humanitarian tradition of rendering aid at sea, echoed in international instruments like SOLAS. The duty is not absolute — it is capped by the safety of the assisting vessel itself. Art. 1295(2) adds a duty to identify: where possible, the master must give the other ship its name, port of registry, and ports of origin and destination, so the parties can later identify each other for any evidence-preservation or litigation purposes.

## Shipowner Not Liable for a Breach of This Duty Alone

The most notable feature is Art. 1295(3): the shipowner is *not* liable solely because the master breached this assistance/identification duty. This is a deliberate exception to the general rule that a shipowner answers for crew fault. The logic is that this duty is separate from the collision itself, and the law wants to encourage masters to render aid without fear that a good-faith but imperfect rescue effort will expose their shipowner to liability — a policy akin to "Good Samaritan" protections in other legal systems.

## Reserved Provisions

Art. 1296 preserves two things from being overridden by the collision chapter: rules on limitation of a shipowner's liability, and any obligations arising from carriage contracts or other contracts. Collision rules govern fault-based liability arising from the collision itself; they do not touch a shipowner's separate right to limit liability, nor do they alter contractual obligations between the parties.

## Prescription: Two Years, With a Separate One-Year Recourse Period

Under Art. 1297(1), any claim for damages arising from a collision prescribes in two years from the date of the collision — longer than the one-year period for general average claims, but shorter than the ten-year period for passenger death or injury claims. Notably, this two-year period applies uniformly regardless of whether the loss is to cargo or to a person, and it runs from the date of the collision itself rather than from a later "arrival" date, since a collision is typically an immediately obvious event. Separately, Art. 1297(2) gives recourse claims *between shipowners* (arising from the fault-based apportionment rules discussed earlier in this series) their own, shorter one-year period, running from the date payment was actually made — reflecting that a recourse right only arises once one shipowner has paid the injured party.

With this provision, the collision chapter of the TTK (Art. 1286-1297) is complete.
