Foreign property owners and tenants in Turkey are often surprised that Turkish criminal law treats forcing open a locked door and simply refusing to leave after being asked to go as the same offense, punished within the same statutory range. Article 116 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) criminalizes both entering someone's home without valid consent and remaining there after consent has been withdrawn. The reason the law bundles these two very different-looking behaviors together is that neither targets the door, the lock, or the walls — both target the resident's authority to decide who is present in their own living space at any given moment. Understanding how this offense is structured, what counts as valid consent, and how it interacts with the workplace variant of the same provision is important for anyone renting, owning, or sharing property in Turkey, including landlords and shared-household situations that commonly arise in Antalya's expatriate community.

## What the Law Actually Protects

Article 116 does not protect a building or a set of keys; it protects the constitutionally guaranteed right, recognized under Article 21 of the Turkish Constitution, of a person to keep their private living space free from outside interference. Because the interest at stake is control over the space rather than ownership of it, title deeds and lease agreements are not the decisive factor. A tenant who has no ownership interest whatsoever can still be the victim of this offense, while a landlord who owns the property outright can become the offender if they enter a rented unit against the tenant's wishes. The relevant question is always who is lawfully and actually living in that space at the time, not whose name appears on the property register.

## Home, Appurtenances and Workplace: Three Different Levels of Protection

The statute distinguishes between three categories of premises, each carrying a different level of protection. A "home" is any enclosed space where a person maintains their life, even temporarily — a rented holiday apartment or a hotel room can qualify just as much as a permanent residence; continuous, year-round occupancy is not required. An "appurtenance" is a space attached to and functionally tied to the home, forming a practical unit with it, such as an enclosed garden, a garage, or a storage shed — the test is whether the area still falls within the home's zone of privacy.

Workplaces receive a separate and lighter regime under paragraph 2. The law draws an important line here: premises that the public may freely enter without needing explicit permission — a shop open during business hours, for instance — fall outside the offense entirely, since walking into a place open to the public is not a violation. An office, warehouse, or closed production facility where entry depends on permission, however, remains protected. The penalty for the workplace variant is capped at imprisonment of six months to one year, or a judicial fine, which is noticeably lighter than the six months to two years of imprisonment set for violations of a home.

## The Two Ways the Offense Can Be Committed

The offense consists of two alternative forms of conduct: entering a space against the resident's will, or remaining there after having entered with consent once that consent is withdrawn. The first is an instantaneous act; the second is an omission — a failure to act on a demand to leave. Both, however, are treated as continuing offenses that persist for as long as the unlawful presence continues. This has practical consequences: the statute of limitations only starts running once the unlawful presence ends, jurisdiction can be established in any location where the continuing presence occurred, and anyone who joins in while the unlawful presence is ongoing can be treated as a participant in the offense.

For the "entering" form to be complete, the offender's entire body must have passed into the space. Partially opening a door, reaching through a window, or climbing a garden wall to look inside does not, by itself, complete the act — depending on the facts, such conduct may instead be treated as an attempt or as an entirely different offense. For the "remaining" form, the offense only crystallizes once the person entitled to the space has clearly communicated a wish for the other person to leave, and the person present resists that demand by staying anyway; silence or ambiguity on the resident's part is not enough.

## Whose Consent Counts, and the "Legitimate Purpose" Limitation

In practice, the outcome of most cases turns on whether valid consent existed. Consent here does not operate as a justification that excuses an otherwise criminal act — it removes the conduct from the definition of the offense altogether, meaning that if valid consent existed, no crime was ever committed in the first place. Consent need not be given explicitly, but consent obtained through deception, force, or threats is treated as no consent at all.

The law makes special provision for spaces shared by more than one person, such as a home occupied by a married couple or a workplace used jointly by several people: if any one of the co-occupants has given consent, the offense does not apply. This convenience, however, is limited by an important qualifier — the consent must be given for a legitimate purpose. A co-occupant cannot lawfully authorize a third party's entry for the specific purpose of undermining another occupant's legally protected interests; permission granted for that kind of purpose does not remove the criminal character of the entry. Whether a purpose is legitimate is assessed by weighing both the scope of the consenting occupant's authority over the space and the actual reason the visitor came in.

## Intent: Ordinary Awareness Is Enough

This is a crime that can only be committed intentionally; there is no punishable negligent version. It is sufficient that the offender knew the place belonged to someone else, or was a protected workplace, and knew that the person entitled to the space had not consented, yet acted anyway. No special motive is required — curiosity, anger, debt collection, or jealous surveillance of a partner are all irrelevant to whether the offense is made out. On the other hand, a genuine mistaken belief that the space was the offender's own, or that consent existed, can negate intent under the general rules on mistake of fact.

## Aggravating Circumstances

Where the act is committed by force or threat, or takes place at night, the penalty rises to imprisonment of one to three years. Nighttime is treated as an aggravating circumstance because residents are typically more vulnerable and the sense of fear is heightened during those hours.

A separate, broader enhancement applies under Article 119, which covers this offense alongside several others (obstructing education, obstructing the activities of public bodies or professional organizations, and interference with political, religious or labour rights). Under that provision, the sentence is increased by half again if the act is committed with a weapon, by someone who has disguised their identity, through an anonymous letter or special signs, by more than one person acting together, by exploiting the intimidating power of an actual or presumed criminal organization, or by abusing the authority conferred by a public office. If the act also results in an aggravated form of intentional bodily harm, the provisions on that separate offense apply in addition.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence With Other Offenses

Because the act of entering can be broken down into stages, an attempt is possible if the offender is stopped partway through. The "remaining" variant, by contrast, is an omission and does not admit of an attempt in the same way. This is not a crime that can only be committed by a specially defined category of offender, so any form of participation by multiple people is possible — and, as noted above, group action is itself an aggravating factor under Article 119.

A recurring question is how this offense interacts with other crimes committed on the same occasion. Where someone enters a home in order to commit theft, two separate legal interests have been violated, so liability generally attaches to both offenses. By contrast, in the crime of aggravated robbery, entering a home is already treated as a qualifying circumstance built into that offense, so it is not punished separately on top of it. Repeated entries into the same home on different occasions may be treated as a single continuing course of conduct where the conditions for that are met.

## Complaint, Mediation and Court Procedure

The basic forms of the offense — entering without consent and unlawfully remaining, under paragraphs 1 and 2 — can only be prosecuted following a complaint from the victim, and that complaint must be filed within six months of learning the identity of the offender and the act. The aggravated form involving force, threat, or nighttime commission is prosecuted by the authorities on their own initiative, without requiring a complaint. Where a complaint is required, mediation is mandatory at both the investigation and prosecution stages, and a successful mediation results in dismissal of the case. Given the statutory sentencing range, trials are heard before the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a landlord be prosecuted for entering a property they own?**
Yes. Ownership of the property is not the decisive factor — what matters is who is lawfully occupying the space. A landlord who enters a rented unit against the tenant's wishes, without the tenant's valid consent, can be treated as the offender under Article 116.

**Does inviting someone in and later asking them to leave create criminal exposure if they don't go?**
Yes. Remaining in a home after being clearly asked to leave is treated the same as entering without consent in the first place, provided the resident's wish to have the person leave was clearly communicated and the person nonetheless stays.

**If one spouse or co-occupant allows someone in, is that enough to prevent the offense?**
Generally yes, but only if the consent was given for a legitimate purpose. Consent given by a co-occupant specifically to harm the legally protected interests of another occupant does not remove the criminal character of the entry.

**Is walking into an open shop during business hours a crime under this article?**
No. Premises that the public may enter freely without needing specific permission fall outside the offense. The protection applies to workplaces where entry is conditioned on permission, such as private offices or closed production areas.
