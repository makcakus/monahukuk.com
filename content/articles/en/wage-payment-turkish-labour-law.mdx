Wages sit at the economic core of every employment relationship, and Turkish law regulates not only how much an employee is owed but *how* that amount must reach them. Article 32 of the Labour Code (Law No. 4857) is the anchor provision, but it does not operate alone: related articles on the right to refuse work when wages are unpaid, and on the statutory minimum wage, complete the picture. For foreign employers building a workforce in Turkey — whether in tourism, construction or manufacturing around Antalya — these rules are mandatory and cannot be modified by contract in the employee's disfavour. This article explains what counts as "wage" under Turkish law, how it must be paid, what happens when it isn't, and how the minimum wage and limitation periods fit into the framework.

## What Counts as "Wage" Under Article 32

Article 32 defines wage broadly: it is not limited to the base salary but extends to any payment made to an employee in return for their work, whether paid by the employer or by a third party. Premiums, bonuses and any other payment of a similar monetary nature fall under the same protective regime as ordinary wages. This wide definition exists precisely to stop employers from relabeling wage components — as a "premium", an "incentive", or a "travel allowance" — to sidestep the mandatory rules that follow. Turkish courts frequently have to determine whether a payment carrying a different name is, in substance, wage; the label an employer gives a payment does not control its legal treatment.

## Mandatory Bank Payment and Prohibited Payment Methods

As a rule, wages must be paid in Turkish currency, and Article 32 expressly rules out payment through a promissory note, coupon, or any other instrument purporting to represent legal tender — an employee must receive either cash or a genuine deposit into a bank account, never a substitute instrument that could erode the real value of their pay. Following the 2008 amendment (Law No. 5754), employers meeting criteria set jointly by the labour and finance authorities — based on factors such as tax status, business size, headcount, and the province where the workplace is located — are required to pay wages, premiums, bonuses and similar entitlements exclusively into a specially opened bank account, and may not use any other payment channel once that obligation applies to them.

A related, more old-fashioned protection is the ban on paying wages inside taverns and similar entertainment venues, or in retail shops — except to staff actually employed there. Historically, this rule was meant to stop employers from paying wages in places designed to encourage employees to spend that money immediately and uncritically.

On evidentiary questions, an unsigned payroll record does not by itself defeat proof of payment where a matching bank transfer exists; conversely, if an employee claims to be owed more than the payroll shows, that employee bears the burden of proving the additional amount.

## Payment Timing and Full Settlement on Termination

Wages must be paid at least once a month. The parties may shorten this cycle to as little as one week by individual or collective employment agreement, but they cannot lawfully lengthen it beyond a month — this rule is mandatory, and an employer may not unilaterally extend the payment interval.

Article 32 also imposes a distinct safeguard when employment ends: on termination, the employee's wage together with all other monetary entitlements arising from the contract or from statute must be paid in full. In practice this covers items such as severance pay, notice pay, unused annual leave pay, and overtime pay. Where an employer pays these amounts late or short, the shortfall can trigger not only interest and enforcement claims but, in serious or persistent cases, may itself justify the employee's termination for just cause.

## The Right to Refuse Work When Wages Go Unpaid

Article 34 gives employees a powerful remedy for unpaid wages. If wages are not paid within twenty days of the due date, and there is no force majeure justifying the delay, the employee may refuse to perform their work. Crucially, the law states that even if a large number of employees exercise this individual right at the same time, their collective refusal is not to be characterised as a strike — each employee is exercising a personal statutory right, not participating in an organised industrial action. While an employee is lawfully refusing work on these grounds, the employer cannot terminate their contract on the basis of that non-performance, nor may the employer bring in another worker, or a third party, to do that employee's job in the meantime.

As a further deterrent, unpaid wages under these circumstances accrue interest at the highest rate applicable to bank deposits — a notably higher rate than ordinary statutory interest. Exercising the right to refuse work does not prevent an employee from also invoking their right to terminate the employment contract for just cause; in practice, employees facing persistent, systematic non-payment often choose the termination route so as to also claim severance pay.

## Minimum Wage and the Five-Year Limitation Period

Article 39 requires the Minimum Wage Determination Commission, operating under the Ministry of Labour and Social Security, to set minimum wage floors at least once every two years for all employees, whether or not they fall within the scope of the Labour Code; in practice, this determination is typically made annually. Commission decisions are final and take effect upon publication in the Official Gazette — meaning the minimum wage figure cannot later be challenged administratively or judicially with retroactive effect.

Where an employment contract defines the wage as "the minimum wage plus a stated percentage," every increase in the statutory minimum wage automatically raises the contractual wage as well; an employer's failure to pass that increase through can give rise to a wage-difference claim.

Finally, under the closing provision of Article 32, wage claims are subject to a special five-year limitation period, distinct from the Labour Code's general limitation rules. In disputes over the amount owed, the burden of proof generally falls on the employee, and a signed payroll record without any objection is treated as conclusive evidence unless disproved; where no payroll record exists, or where the employee signed with a reservation, the wage amount may be proven by any evidence, including witness testimony.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can an employer pay part of an employee's salary in cash and part by bank transfer?**
Employers who fall within the mandatory bank-payment scope set by the relevant ministries must pay the covered wage, premium and bonus amounts exclusively through the designated bank account, and may not split payment through other channels once that obligation applies to them.

**Can an employment contract set the pay cycle at two months instead of one?**
No. Article 32 allows the payment interval to be shortened to as little as a week by agreement, but it may not be extended beyond one month; any clause purporting to do so is unenforceable.

**If my employer is 25 days late paying my wages, what can I do?**
Once wages remain unpaid for more than twenty days from the due date without force majeure, Article 34 entitles you to refuse to work without risking termination or replacement, and the unpaid amount accrues interest at the highest bank deposit rate. You may also be entitled to terminate your contract for just cause and claim severance.

**How long do I have to bring a claim for unpaid wages?**
Wage claims are subject to a special five-year limitation period under Article 32, running separately from other limitation periods that may apply to different types of employment claims.
