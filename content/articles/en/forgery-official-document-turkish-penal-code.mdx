Foreign nationals and international businesses drawn into Turkish criminal proceedings over a disputed contract, invoice, permit, or notarized instrument are often surprised to discover that the outcome frequently turns not on who signed what, but on a single technical question: could the document, examined with reasonable care, actually have passed for genuine? Article 204 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") criminalizes forging or falsifying an official document, or knowingly using one that has been forged or falsified, but Turkish courts will not convict unless the document possessed what is known as "capacity to deceive." Understanding this threshold, along with the related offenses that surround it, is essential for anyone facing or defending against a forgery allegation in Turkey.

## The Interest Protected: Public Trust, Not Individual Harm

Article 204 sits within the part of the Turkish Penal Code dealing with offenses against public trust, and that placement matters. The law does not protect a particular victim's property or reputation; it protects the reliance that the public and state institutions place on official documents as accurate records that can be used as a basis for legal transactions. An "official document," for these purposes, is a written instrument produced by a public official in the course of that official's duties and capable of producing legal effect. Whether a given paper qualifies as an official document in the first place — a question that depends on the issuing authority's competence and the formal requirements the document must satisfy — is treated by Turkish courts as a threshold issue to be resolved before the forgery analysis even begins. In its ordinary form under the first paragraph of Article 204, this is a general offense: anyone, not only a public official, can commit it.

## Three Ways to Commit the Basic Offense

The first paragraph of Article 204 combines three distinct forms of conduct into a single offense, any one of which is sufficient for liability. The first is fabricating a document that purports to be official but was never genuinely issued by a competent authority. The second is altering the content of an authentic, properly issued document after the fact so that it no longer reflects what was originally recorded. The third is knowingly using a forged or altered official document — presenting it, relying on it, or invoking it in a legal transaction — even by a person who played no part in creating the forgery. Because using a forged document is itself a separate basis for liability, a defendant who received a falsified document from someone else and then submitted it can still be prosecuted under this provision. None of the three variants requires proof that anyone was actually deceived or that concrete harm resulted; Turkish doctrine treats the offense as one of abstract danger, meaning the law presumes a sufficient threat to public trust once a document with the capacity to deceive exists, without requiring evidence that a specific person was in fact misled.

## The Capacity-to-Deceive Test

The single most litigated element of Article 204 is whether the document had the capacity to deceive — often referred to in Turkish practice by its traditional term, iğfal kabiliyeti. The standard is not whether the forgery would fool someone glancing at the document carelessly; a document only meets this threshold if it could pass for genuine even when examined with the level of attention and diligence ordinarily expected under the circumstances. Turkish courts assess this by reference to the specific facts of each case: the type of document, the practices of the institution that would normally issue it, the materials and techniques used to produce the forgery, and the degree of care the person examining it could reasonably be expected to exercise. In practice, the quality of a forged signature or seal, how closely the paper stock and printing resemble a genuine original, and the technology realistically available at the time the document was supposedly produced all factor into the analysis. A document that is crude or obviously fake on inspection lacks the necessary capacity to deceive, and using it will not support a conviction under Article 204 — although it may still support liability for a different offense, such as fraud, depending on the facts. Because this determination is technical, Turkish courts frequently resolve it only after obtaining an expert forensic examination of the original document.

## Aggravated Liability for Public Officials and Qualified Circumstances

The second paragraph of Article 204 creates a separate, more serious offense that applies only to a public official who is authorized, as part of that official's own duties, to issue the type of document in question. This is what Turkish law calls a special or "own" offense — only someone holding that specific authority can be the perpetrator, unlike the general offense in the first paragraph. The scope of conduct covered is also broader: alongside forging or deceptively altering a document, this paragraph also reaches a public official who issues a document containing false content, even without fabricating the physical instrument itself. Because a public official's abuse of that authority strikes more directly at public trust, the penalty range is correspondingly higher than under the basic offense. A separate qualified circumstance applies where the falsified document is one that Turkish law treats as legally valid until its falsity is formally established — instruments with heightened evidentiary weight — in which case the penalty otherwise applicable is increased by half.

Two related offenses often arise alongside Article 204 in practice. Article 205 punishes destroying, suppressing, or concealing a genuine official document — a different kind of attack on public trust, since the content is not altered but the document's very existence or availability as evidence is eliminated; a public official who commits this act faces an increased penalty. Article 206 addresses yet another scenario: the offender does not create or alter any document personally, but induces a public official who is unaware of the deception to record false information supplied by the offender in an otherwise genuine official document. Liability there attaches to the person who made the false statement, not to the public official who acted in good faith.

## Certain Private Instruments Treated as Official Documents

Article 210 extends the Article 204 framework to specific categories of instruments that would otherwise be classified merely as private documents — negotiable instruments payable to order or to bearer, documents representing goods, shares, bonds, and wills. Forgery involving any of these is punished under the more severe official-document provisions rather than under the comparatively lighter rules that apply to ordinary private-document forgery. The same article separately addresses a healthcare professional who issues a report containing false content, treating this as a distinct and lesser offense carrying a modest custodial penalty; however, if the false report secures an unjust benefit for someone or causes harm to the public or to individuals, the applicable penalty rises to match the standard official-document forgery range.

## Fault, Mitigation, and Sentencing Considerations

Article 204 requires only general intent — the offender must knowingly forge, alter, or use a document known to be forged or falsified, with no additional special purpose required. Turkish law does, however, provide for a significant mitigation: where the forgery was carried out only to document or prove a claim or a state of affairs that genuinely existed, the resulting penalty is reduced by half, reflecting the different culpability of someone documenting a real underlying right rather than fabricating one. An attempt is legally possible for the "use" variant of the offense, where the act of presenting or relying on the document can be interrupted partway through. Anyone who instigates or knowingly assists the actual forger, without personally creating the document, can still be held liable under Turkey's general rules on participation in an offense. Where a forged document is used as the instrument for committing an entirely separate crime, Turkish law applies both offenses cumulatively rather than treating the forgery as merged into or absorbed by the other crime.

Because forgery of an official document is classified as an offense against public trust rather than against a private individual, it is prosecuted by the public prosecutor on its own initiative; the case cannot be closed by mediation or withdrawn by a private complainant, and ordinary statute-of-limitations rules apply based on the maximum sentence attached to the relevant provision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a badly forged document that nobody was actually fooled by still count as forgery under Article 204?**
Generally, no. If the document is crude enough that a person exercising reasonable care would recognize it as fake, Turkish courts treat it as lacking the capacity to deceive, which is a required element of the offense. Using such a document may still expose the user to liability for a different crime, such as fraud, but not for forgery of an official document.

**Can someone be convicted under Article 204 for using a forged document even if they did not create it?**
Yes. The offense expressly covers knowingly using a forged or altered official document, regardless of who produced the forgery. Liability attaches to anyone who, aware of the falsity, presents or relies on the document in a legal transaction.

**Why do penalties differ so much depending on who committed the forgery?**
Article 204 sets a lower range for the general offense, committed by any person, and a significantly higher range where the offender is a public official who abused the specific authority to issue that type of document. Turkish law treats the latter as a more serious breach of public trust because it comes from within the very institution the public relies on.

**Is expert examination of the document always required?**
It is not an absolute legal requirement in every case, but because the capacity-to-deceive determination is technical, Turkish courts frequently order a forensic or expert examination of the original document whenever there is genuine doubt, and appellate decisions have overturned convictions and acquittals reached without such an examination where the point was genuinely disputed.
