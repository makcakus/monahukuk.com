TTK Art. 1193-1195 fill in the gaps left when a freight contract doesn't specify — or only partly specifies — how much is owed, across the three common freight structures: per unit, per time, and unspecified.

## Freight by Measure, Weight, or Count: Delivered Quantity Governs

Where freight is set per unit of measure, weight, or count, any doubt is resolved in favor of the quantity actually *delivered* to the consignee rather than the quantity loaded. This absorbs the ordinary shrinkage or loss that occurs between loading and discharge — particularly common with bulk cargo — into the freight calculation automatically, without turning every discrepancy into a separate dispute over whether the shrinkage was the carrier's fault. This is a default rule only: an explicit contractual term (e.g., "loaded quantity is final") overrides it.

## Time-Based Freight: A Clock Tied to the Ship Being "in Service"

Where freight runs on time rather than quantity, Art. 1194 ties it tightly to the ship's period of active service. It starts on a contractually fixed loading date, or the day after notice of readiness under Art. 1152; for ballast voyages, it starts the day after notice of readiness for the voyage, or from actual departure if no such notice was given at least a day before departure. It stops accruing during any demurrage period (since demurrage already compensates for that delay separately) and definitively after discharge is complete. If the voyage is delayed or interrupted through no fault of the carrier, however, time-based freight keeps accruing through the interruption — reflecting that the ship remains committed to the charterer even during a fault-free delay.

## When Freight Was Never Agreed

If the amount was never specified at all, the freight prevailing at the time and place of loading applies — a standard gap-filling approach that typically requires expert evidence of prevailing local rates. Where a rate was agreed for a specified quantity but the cargo actually tendered exceeds it, the excess is charged proportionally at the same agreed rate rather than triggering a fresh market-rate inquiry — respecting the pricing benchmark the parties already agreed on.

## Practical Takeaway

These provisions are largely interpretive defaults that yield to explicit contract language; disputes over freight *amount* tend to be resolved through expert calculation rather than litigated case law, unlike freight *maturity* disputes, which are far more frequently contested in court.
