Foreign employers and HR teams operating in Turkey are often surprised to learn that they cannot simply respond to a labour claim in court — in most cases, the employee (or the employer) must first go through a mediator before a lawsuit can even be filed. Since 1 January 2018, Law No. 7036 on Labour Courts has made mediation a **precondition for filing suit** (dava şartı) in the great majority of individual and collective labour disputes in Turkey. Skipping this step, or handling it incorrectly, does not simply delay a case — it can result in the lawsuit being dismissed on procedural grounds before a judge ever examines its merits. This article explains which disputes are subject to mandatory mediation, how the process works, what happens if it is mishandled, and how Turkish labour courts fit into the picture.

## What Disputes Require Mediation First

Article 3(1) of Law No. 7036 casts a wide net: any lawsuit for a receivable or compensation claimed by an employee or an employer — whether based on statute (most commonly the Labour Law No. 4857, covering severance pay, notice pay, overtime and similar claims) or on an individual or collective labour agreement — as well as reinstatement claims, must be preceded by an application to a mediator. A 2023 amendment extended this list further, bringing actions for annulment of an objection to a claim, negative declaratory actions and actions for recovery of payments related to the same receivables and compensation under the same requirement. In practical terms, mediation is now a mandatory first step for nearly every category of labour-receivable litigation in Turkey.

The law is equally clear on who counts as an employee for this purpose: Article 3(20) confirms that journalists covered by the Press Labour Law No. 5953 and seafarers covered by the Maritime Labour Law No. 854 are treated as employees under this provision, so they too must apply to a mediator before suing.

There is one significant carve-out. Article 3(3) excludes material and non-pecuniary damages arising from a workplace accident or occupational disease — together with related declaratory, objection and recourse actions — from the mandatory mediation requirement. The legislative rationale is to avoid delaying victims of workplace accidents in seeking redress. That said, parties remain free to use **voluntary mediation** for these claims if they wish, and Turkish Court of Cassation case law confirms that an agreement reached through voluntary mediation is just as binding as one reached under the mandatory procedure — a party who has settled voluntarily cannot later reopen the same claim in court.

## How the Mediation Process Works

The process begins when the claiming party — typically the employee — applies to the mediation office in their place of residence or where the work was performed. The office assigns a mediator from the roster held by the relevant bar-affiliated mediation board, unless the parties agree on a specific mediator from that list themselves. Once appointed, the mediator must conclude the file within three weeks, extendable by up to one further week where necessary.

Parties may attend sessions in person, through their legal representatives, or through counsel; an employer may also be represented by an employee holding written authority to do so. Attendance at the first session is not optional in a meaningful sense: if a party fails to attend without a valid excuse, mediation ends there, and that party — even if ultimately successful in any later lawsuit — will bear part of the other side's litigation costs and a portion of the attorney's fee awarded against them. This is a deliberate deterrent designed to make sure parties engage with the process rather than treat it as a formality to be brushed aside.

Importantly, the running of any statute-of-limitations period or any mandatory time limit for filing suit is suspended from the date of application to the mediation office until the date the final minutes are drawn up. This procedural safeguard ensures that going through mediation in good faith never costs a party their underlying claim.

## The Requirement to Attach the Final Minutes to the Complaint

If mediation ends without agreement, the mediator draws up a final report (the "final minutes"). Under Article 3(2), the claimant must attach the original of this document, or a copy certified by the mediator, to the complaint when filing suit. If this is omitted, the court sends the claimant a formal notice granting a strict one-week period to submit the final minutes, warning that failure to do so will result in dismissal on procedural grounds; if the deadline passes without compliance, the case is dismissed without even being served on the opposing party. Turkish Court of Cassation precedent has clarified that this one-week period is a genuine procedural safeguard — a court cannot dismiss the case outright without first granting it — but has equally confirmed that dismissal is proper once the period has been given and ignored.

An even harsher consequence applies where it turns out that no mediation application was made at all before the lawsuit was filed: in that scenario the court dismisses the case immediately for lack of a lawsuit precondition, without sending any warning or granting any grace period.

## The Multiple-Defendant Problem: Mandatory Joinder

One of the most litigated issues in practice arises when a dispute involves more than one potential defendant — the classic example being a principal employer/subcontractor relationship. Turkish Court of Cassation case law holds that where the case involves parties who must necessarily be joined together as co-defendants (compulsory joinder), a mediation process conducted against only one of them does not fully satisfy the lawsuit precondition. The reasoning is that the party who was never brought into the mediation might have contributed to a settlement had they participated, so the precondition must be fulfilled separately as to each mandatory co-defendant. The same approach has been applied consistently to reinstatement claims involving a principal employer and subcontractor: unless mediation has been sought against both employers, the precondition is not considered satisfied. The practical lesson for counsel is to identify every party who could potentially be a co-defendant before filing a mediation application, and to name and pursue mediation against each of them separately — otherwise the resulting lawsuit risks outright procedural dismissal.

## The 2025 Annulment of the Joint-Participation Rule for Subcontractor Reinstatement Settlements

Alongside the case-law-driven "mandatory joinder" principle described above, Article 3 of Law No. 7036 used to contain paragraph (15), a specific rule addressing settlements in principal employer/subcontractor reinstatement mediations: it provided that where mediation was sought over a reinstatement claim in a principal employer-subcontractor relationship, a valid settlement required both employers to attend the mediation sessions jointly and to reach aligned positions.

On 3 June 2025, the Constitutional Court (Case No. 2024/157, Decision No. 2025/121, published in the Official Gazette on 17 October 2025) annulled this paragraph on referral from an Adana labour court. The Court held that the rule placed a disproportionate burden on employees' constitutional right of access to court: an employee cannot always be expected to know that a subcontracting relationship exists, let alone identify every party to it, before applying to mediation, and Turkish civil procedure already offers a remedy for a claim mistakenly directed at the wrong party through the party-substitution mechanism in Article 124 of the Code of Civil Procedure. Requiring both employers to jointly participate simply to reach a valid settlement, on pain of the employee's claim otherwise failing on a technicality, was found to strike an unreasonable balance between that procedural aim and the employee's substantive right to a hearing.

This annulment removes the specific statutory requirement for a *binding settlement* in these cases; it does not, on its own, disturb the separate Court of Cassation line of authority — discussed above — holding that a principal employer and subcontractor are mandatory co-defendants for standing purposes, so mediation should still, as a matter of prudent practice, be pursued against every identifiable employer. Given how recently this provision was struck down, practitioners should treat this as a developing area and confirm the current state of Court of Cassation practice before relying on it in a specific case.

## Jurisdiction of Labour Courts

Article 5 of Law No. 7036 defines what labour courts handle by reference to four underlying statutes: the Labour Law No. 4857, the service-agreement provisions of the Turkish Code of Obligations No. 6098, the Press Labour Law No. 5953, and the Maritime Labour Law No. 854. This means that employees who fall outside the Labour Law's direct scope but whose relationship is governed by the Code of Obligations' service-agreement rules — such as domestic workers, employees of very small agricultural operations, or apprentices — still bring their disputes before specialised labour courts rather than general civil courts. Article 5(1)(b) also assigns labour courts jurisdiction over disputes arising from labour and social security legislation to which the Social Security Institution or the Turkish Employment Agency (İŞKUR) is a party, though objections to administrative fines are expressly excluded from this. A residual clause in Article 5(1)(c) preserves jurisdiction for any other dispute that a separate statute specifically assigns to labour courts.

## Procedure and Appeal Route

Labour courts apply the simplified procedure (basit yargılama usulü), designed to resolve labour disputes with fewer formal steps and greater speed than the standard civil procedure. Article 8 of the law sets monetary thresholds below which a regional appellate court's decision is final and cannot be taken further to the Court of Cassation; these thresholds are periodically updated, so the current figure must always be checked at the time a case is filed. Reinstatement lawsuits are subject to a further acceleration rule: they must be concluded urgently, and the statutory time limits for the employee's post-judgment application to the employer, and for any compensation owed if the employer fails to reinstate, begin to run without waiting for the decision to become final. Because of this, managing the handoff between the mediation stage and the court stage correctly is critical to preserving an employee's reinstatement and related compensation rights.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to go through a mediator before suing for unpaid wages or severance in Turkey?**
Yes. Claims for statutory or contractual labour receivables and compensation — including severance pay, notice pay and similar amounts — fall within the mandatory mediation requirement under Article 3(1) of Law No. 7036, and the lawsuit cannot proceed without first attempting mediation.

**Are workplace accident compensation claims covered by mandatory mediation?**
No. Article 3(3) excludes material and non-pecuniary damages from a workplace accident or occupational disease, along with related declaratory and recourse actions, from the mandatory mediation requirement. Parties may still choose voluntary mediation for these claims, and any resulting settlement is binding.

**What happens if I file a labour lawsuit without going to mediation first?**
The court will dismiss the case for lack of a lawsuit precondition, without any warning period and without serving the complaint on the other side. This makes it essential to complete mediation before filing, not after.

**In a subcontractor dispute, is it enough to mediate with just one of the companies?**
No. Where the principal employer and the subcontractor must be joined as co-defendants, Court of Cassation precedent requires that the mediation precondition be satisfied separately against each of them. Mediating with only one exposes the case to dismissal.

**Is it still true that a reinstatement settlement against a subcontractor is invalid unless the principal employer also joined the mediation?**
That specific rule, Article 3(15) of Law No. 7036, was annulled by the Constitutional Court in 2025 (Case No. 2024/157, Decision No. 2025/121) as a disproportionate burden on employees. The separate Court of Cassation case law requiring both employers to be pursued as mandatory co-defendants is unaffected by this annulment, so mediating against both remains the prudent course.
