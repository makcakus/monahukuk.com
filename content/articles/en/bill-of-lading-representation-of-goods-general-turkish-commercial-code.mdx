TTK Art. 1234 is a single sentence carrying enormous doctrinal weight: it makes a bill of lading a legal stand-in for the goods themselves, not merely a receipt or a claim ticket.

## The Rule: Transferring the Bill Transfers the Goods

Once cargo has been received by the captain or another carrier representative, Art. 1234 provides that surrendering the bill of lading to the person entitled to claim delivery under it (subject to the multiple-holder rules discussed separately) produces the same legal effects as transferring possession under the Turkish Civil Code's general property-possession rules. In practice, this means transferring a document representing goods held by a third-party custodian is treated as equivalent to handing over the goods themselves — the concept commonly known internationally as a "document of title." This is distinct from the delivery rules already covered: those govern how a holder collects cargo from the carrier, while Art. 1234 governs how rights to the cargo change hands between third parties who never touch the ship or the goods at all.

## Why This Underpins International Trade

Without this rule, a cargo in transit for weeks would be legally frozen — no sale, pledge, or security interest could attach to goods nobody can yet touch. Art. 1234 removes that bottleneck: a seller who has loaded goods and holds the bill can endorse it to a bank under a letter of credit, which can in turn re-endorse it to a buyer, who might resell it again while the cargo is still at sea. At each link in that chain, the paper carries the underlying rights with it. This is precisely why banks accept a bill of lading as security in documentary transactions — the bank never sees the cargo, but the bill gives it a real legal claim to it.

## Protection for Good-Faith Transferees

Because the rule incorporates the Civil Code's possession-transfer provisions, it also extends a form of protection to someone who acquires the bill in good faith, relying on its facial validity and an apparently proper chain of endorsement — without which negotiability would be theoretical rather than usable in practice. This general rule is expressly subject to the separate multiple-original provisions, since more than one bill circulating for the same cargo complicates who acquires priority.

## Practical Note

Turkish courts and tax authorities have confirmed this rule extends to determining when ownership of imported cargo passes for accounting purposes, and equally that no transfer occurs — and title stays with the seller — where the bill of lading was never actually delivered, such as when a buyer failed to pay.
