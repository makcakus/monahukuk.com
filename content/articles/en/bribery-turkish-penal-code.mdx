Foreign businesses operating in Turkey, and their counsel, are often surprised by how early Turkish criminal law treats a bribery case as complete. Under Article 252 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), bribery is not built around the moment money or a benefit actually changes hands — it is built around the moment the two sides reach an understanding. That single design choice shapes almost everything else about how bribery cases are investigated, charged and defended in Turkey, and it is why parties involved in a public tender, a licensing process, or any interaction with a Turkish public official need to understand the offense before, not after, a problematic conversation takes place.

## What Article 252 Protects and Why It Matters

Bribery is classified among offenses against the reliability and proper functioning of public administration, not among offenses against individual property or persons. The rule exists to keep official decision-making free from being purchased, and the harm the law targets is damage to public trust in the administration itself, not damage to a private victim. This has a very practical consequence: there is no injured party whose complaint is needed to start a case. Prosecutors can and do open bribery investigations on their own initiative whenever they learn that a public function may have been bought, regardless of whether either side involved ever complains.

## A Two-Sided Offense: Both Parties Are Principals

Article 252 does not describe bribery as something one person does to another. It describes a bargain reached by two willing sides, and it punishes both of them under the same sentencing range — a prison term of four to twelve years. The person who provides the benefit is addressed in the first paragraph of the article, and the public official who accepts it is addressed separately in the second paragraph, but the ranges are identical. This structure means the offense requires two distinct wills to converge: the person offering an advantage and the official accepting it. Without both sides, there is no completed bribery — though, as explained below, Turkish law separately punishes the situation where only one side ever commits.

## The Elements: Who Can Commit It and What It Requires

Anyone can be liable as the party offering the benefit, but liability on the receiving end is restricted to a person holding public-official status, which makes the receiving side of the offense a specific-capacity crime. The object of the offense — the "benefit" — is read broadly: it need not be cash, and can be any advantage, service or favor with economic value. The essential feature that defines bribery is the exchange itself: the benefit must be given in return for the official doing, or refraining from doing, something connected to the performance of that official's duties. It does not matter if the benefit is not delivered to the official personally — the law also covers benefits routed to a third party the official designates, or benefits that indirectly reach another person or a legal entity, and in that case the person or entity that accepts the indirect benefit is treated as a co-principal in the offense as well.

## The Bribery Agreement: When the Offense Is Deemed Complete

The most distinctive feature of Turkish bribery law is that the law pulls the completion point backward. Once the two sides reach an agreement on the exchange, the offense is treated as fully completed even if no benefit has actually been handed over yet. This turns bribery away from the ordinary model of a crime that is only finished once a result occurs, and instead makes the moment of agreement the center of gravity for the whole offense. A different, lesser situation arises where only one side has expressed a position and the other side never agreed to it — for example, an official's demand is refused, or an offer made to an official is never accepted. In that one-sided scenario there is no agreement and therefore no completed bribery; instead, the law provides for a reduced penalty, cut in half from the ordinary range, treating the failed offer or demand as a distinct and less serious event from a completed bargain.

## Distinguishing Bribery From Extortion by a Public Official

The dividing line between bribery and the separate offense of extortion by a public official turns on how the benefit came to be provided. In extortion, the official exploits the power of the position to pressure or manipulate the other person into handing over a benefit; that person's will is not free, and that person is treated as a victim, not an offender. In bribery, by contrast, both sides negotiate and agree as equals, acting on their own free will, which is exactly why the private party is also treated as a principal offender rather than a victim. Establishing which pattern applies in practice requires looking at who initiated the request, whether real freedom of choice existed, the prior relationship between the parties, the size of the benefit, and whether the request involved any element of pressure or insistence. It also matters that the benefit in a genuine bribery case must be tied, prospectively, to a specific act or omission connected to official duties — a benefit given afterward, purely as a gesture of thanks and with no prior understanding, raises a separate question about whether any agreement existed at all.

## Intermediaries and Aggravating Circumstances

Anyone who relays an offer or demand between the parties, brings about the underlying agreement, or otherwise facilitates the exchange is treated as a co-principal to the offense, regardless of whether that intermediary is a public official — an unusually strict rule, since intermediaries in most other offenses would only be treated as accessories. The penalty is further increased, by one-third to one-half, when the person receiving, demanding or agreeing to the bribe holds a position such as a judge, arbitrator, court-appointed expert, notary, or sworn financial advisor, reflecting the greater harm the law attaches to buying influence over people entrusted with adjudicating disputes or verifying facts. The reach of the article extends still further: it also covers people acting on behalf of certain private-law entities that carry a public character — professional bodies established as public institutions, companies formed with the participation of public bodies, foundations operating within such public bodies, associations working for the public benefit, cooperatives, and publicly traded companies — as well as foreign public officials, foreign judges and jurors, members of international parliamentary bodies, and arbitrators appointed under international arbitration procedures. In these cases Turkish courts may open an investigation even where the underlying conduct occurred abroad, provided Turkey, a Turkish public institution, a Turkish legal entity, or a Turkish citizen has an interest in the matter and the person involved is later found in Turkey.

## Fault, Corporate Consequences and Effective Repentance

Bribery requires direct intent: the offender must knowingly and willingly participate in the exchange, aware that the benefit is being given or received in return for official action or inaction. Because the offense already requires two parties acting in concert, it inherently involves participation by multiple actors, and intermediaries are independently deemed co-principals under the rule described above. Where a legal entity has benefited unfairly through a bribery scheme, Turkish law provides for entity-specific security measures against it. The law also grants a form of effective repentance that can eliminate criminal liability entirely, but only if the relevant party acts before the authorities learn of the matter on their own: an official who has received a bribe avoids punishment by handing the item over intact to the investigating authorities, or by voluntarily reporting an agreement to accept a bribe; a person who gave or agreed to give a bribe avoids punishment by voluntarily notifying the authorities out of genuine remorse; and the same protection extends to any other participant in the offense who comes forward under the same conditions. This leniency, however, does not apply to a person who bribed a foreign public official. Bribery is prosecuted ex officio — it does not depend on a private complaint — and cases are tried before the heavy penal courts (ağır ceza mahkemesi).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a bribery charge require that money or a benefit actually changed hands?**
No. Under Article 252, once the two sides reach an agreement on the exchange, the offense is treated as completed even if the benefit was never actually delivered.

**What happens if a public official asks for a bribe but the other person refuses?**
There is no completed bribery in that scenario because no agreement was reached. Instead, the offense is treated as a one-sided offer or demand, and the applicable penalty is reduced by half compared to a completed bribery.

**How is bribery different from extortion by a public official?**
The key distinction is whether the benefit was given freely. In extortion, the official pressures or manipulates the other party into providing a benefit, and that party is a victim. In bribery, both sides negotiate as equals and agree freely, so both are treated as offenders.

**Can someone avoid punishment by reporting the bribery themselves?**
Yes, under certain conditions. If the person involved — whether the official, the person who offered the benefit, or another participant — voluntarily reports the matter to the authorities before it is discovered independently, Turkish law provides for effective repentance that removes criminal liability. This protection does not extend to bribery of a foreign public official.
