Foreign nationals involved in Turkish traffic accidents, workplace incidents, or medical mishaps that result in a death are often unfamiliar with how Turkish criminal law separates an unintended, careless death from murder. Article 85 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") is the provision that governs deaths caused by negligence rather than intent, and it sits immediately after the article on intentional killing because both protect the same value — human life — while differing sharply in how the offender related to the outcome. Because the majority of fatal traffic collisions, industrial accidents, and medical treatment errors in Turkey are prosecuted under this single article, understanding its structure, its overlap with related offenses, and the sentencing consequences that follow from it is essential for anyone facing or advising on a Turkish criminal case arising from an accidental death.

## The Legal Interest Protected by Article 85

The value Article 85 protects is the same as the value protected by the intentional homicide provision: the right to life. Turkish law extends criminal protection over this right not only against people who deliberately attack others, but also against people whose carelessness in ordinary life or professional activity ends someone's life. By criminalizing careless conduct that results in death, the legislature pushes individuals — drivers, employers, medical professionals, and ordinary citizens alike — toward maintaining a baseline standard of care in situations where their conduct could endanger others.

## Who Can Commit the Offense, Who Can Be the Victim, and What Must Be Proven

Negligent homicide is not limited to a particular category of offender; in principle, anyone can commit it, provided that the person was under some duty of care in the specific situation — a duty that can arise from statute, professional rules, traffic regulations, or the general expectations of ordinary social life. The victim must be a living human being; an unborn fetus cannot be the victim of this particular offense, and no special relationship between the offender and the deceased is required for the offense to apply.

The conduct itself can take almost any form, so long as it breaches a duty of care — it may be an active act (driving above the speed limit, running a red light) or a failure to act (omitting a required safety measure). The offense is only complete once a death has actually occurred; without a resulting death, there is no case under Article 85, however careless the conduct. Just as importantly, there must be an unbroken causal link between the offender's careless conduct and the death. If an unforeseeable event outside the offender's control breaks that chain, the death cannot be attributed to the offender.

## The Mental Element: Negligence, and Its Two Forms

What separates this offense from intentional homicide is the mental state behind it. Under Article 22 of the Penal Code, negligence means failing to foresee a legally defined result that flows from a breach of a duty of care — the offender did not intend the outcome and, in the ordinary case, did not even see it coming. Turkish law recognizes two forms. In ordinary (simple) negligence, the offender did not foresee a foreseeable result at all. In conscious negligence, addressed separately in Article 22(3), the offender did foresee the possibility of the result but did not want it to happen and trusted, mistakenly, that it would not occur. Because continuing risky conduct despite having foreseen the danger reflects a heavier degree of fault, conscious negligence carries an increased sentence compared with ordinary negligence. Where the offender bears no fault at all — for instance, because the outcome was genuinely unforeseeable — there is no negligence, and therefore no offense under Article 85.

## Conscious Negligence Versus Possible Intent: The Decisive Distinction

The most contested and outcome-determining question in this area of law is where to draw the line between conscious negligence (Article 22(3)) and possible intent (Article 21(2)). In both situations the offender foresees that the result — death — might occur; the distinction turns on the offender's internal attitude toward that foreseen possibility. In conscious negligence, the offender does not want the result and genuinely believes, however unreasonably, that it will not materialize. In possible intent, the offender essentially accepts the outcome and proceeds regardless, in effect indifferent to whether it happens. A death caused with possible intent is no longer negligent homicide at all — it is intentional homicide under Article 81, which carries a far heavier penalty. This distinction is precisely why cases involving extreme speeding, drunk driving, or severe and obvious safety violations are litigated so intensely: the classification decides whether the defendant is punished for a careless death or for an intentional killing.

Where fault is established, its degree still matters for the outcome. When more than one person contributed to the accident, Turkish practice distinguishes between primary and secondary fault, and where the victim also contributed to the outcome through their own conduct, courts apply the concept of contributory fault. The resulting fault ratio has a direct bearing both on how the criminal sentence is individualized and on any related civil compensation claim.

## The Aggravated Form and Related Procedural Rules

Article 85(2) provides for a heavier penalty where a single negligent act causes the death of more than one person, or the death of one or more persons together with the injury of one or more others. A single careless act that harms multiple victims is handled through this specific aggravated provision rather than through the general rules on multiple offenses.

Negligent offenses also behave differently from intentional ones in several structural respects. Attempt is conceptually impossible, since attempt presupposes an intent directed at completing a crime, and negligence by definition involves no such directed intent. Complicity in the sense of instigating or aiding another's negligent act is likewise not recognized; where several people's separate careless conduct combines to cause one death, each is held liable individually for their own share of the fault, not as accomplices to one another. And because the rules on continuing (chain) offenses apply only to intentionally committed crimes, they have no application to negligent homicide — cases involving multiple deaths from one act are instead addressed through the aggravated provision described above.

The basic form of the offense (Article 85(1)) carries a prison sentence of two to six years; the aggravated form, involving multiple deaths or death combined with injury, carries two to fifteen years. Where conscious negligence is established, the applicable sentence is increased further under Article 22(3). Procedurally, negligent homicide is prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative — it is not a complaint-based offense, and a victim's family withdrawing a complaint does not end the case. Jurisdiction ordinarily lies with the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi) rather than the heavier assize court, despite the severity of the potential sentence. In practice, the offense arises most often in traffic accidents, workplace accidents, and medical malpractice cases, where expert technical reports are typically decisive in establishing both the fault ratio and the causal link between the conduct and the death.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If a driver caused a fatal accident but never intended for anyone to die, is that automatically negligent homicide rather than murder?**
Not automatically. If the driver merely failed to foresee the risk, or foresaw it but genuinely believed it would not materialize, the case falls under negligent homicide (Article 85), possibly with the increased penalty for conscious negligence. But if the evidence shows the driver foresaw the risk of death and effectively accepted it while continuing the conduct — for example, extreme, deliberate risk-taking — a prosecutor may instead pursue intentional homicide on a possible-intent theory (Article 81), which carries a substantially heavier sentence.

**Can a family stop a negligent homicide prosecution by withdrawing their complaint?**
No. Negligent homicide is prosecuted regardless of the victim's family's wishes; it is not a complaint-dependent offense, so withdrawal of a complaint does not terminate the proceedings.

**Does it matter if the victim also contributed to the accident, for example by their own careless conduct?**
Yes. Turkish practice recognizes contributory fault on the victim's part, and where fault is shared among multiple people, courts distinguish between primary and secondary responsibility. The resulting fault allocation affects both sentencing and any related civil damages claim, though it does not, by itself, eliminate the offender's criminal liability.

**Is negligent homicide handled by the same court as more serious violent crimes?**
No. Despite the potential severity of the sentence, negligent homicide cases are ordinarily heard by the criminal court of first instance rather than the assize court that handles the most serious offenses.
