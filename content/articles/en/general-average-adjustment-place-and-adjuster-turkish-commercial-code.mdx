Following the shipowner's obligation to commission the adjustment, the next two provisions fix where the adjustment happens and who actually performs it (TTK Art. 1279-1280).

## Where the Adjustment Is Made

Art. 1279 sets a two-step rule: the loss is assessed and apportioned at the port of destination, or, if the ship never reaches it, at the port where the voyage actually ends. The destination port is preferred because that is where the parties, the cargo, and the relevant documents (bills of lading, charterparties, invoices) naturally converge. Turkish courts have applied this literally in practice — where a vessel suffered repeated engine failures and never reached its contractual destination, the appropriate court was the one at the port where the voyage in fact terminated, and jurisdiction followed from that location.

This "place" also anchors two other rules covered elsewhere in the series: it is the port from which the ship may not depart without posting security (Art. 1276), and it is the reference point for when the one-year general average prescription period under Art. 1285 starts to run.

## Appointing the Adjuster

Under Art. 1280(1), the adjuster (or adjusters) is appointed by unanimous agreement of the interested parties; failing unanimity, the court at the place of adjustment appoints one. In practice, unanimous appointment through a "terms of engagement" agreement between the shipowner and cargo/insurance interests is the norm — court-ordered appointment appears to be rare.

## Document Flow and the Adjuster's Report

Art. 1280(2)-(3) obliges every interested party to hand over documents needed for the adjustment — charterparties, bills of lading, and invoices in particular — and allows the court, at the adjuster's request, to order production by anyone holding relevant documents. Once the report is ready, Art. 1280(4) requires the adjuster to let interested parties inspect it and, on request and payment of costs, to provide a copy — a transparency step that is a precondition for any later approval of, or objection to, the report.

## Practical Takeaway

Because the appointment mechanism relies heavily on commercial cooperation between sophisticated parties, disputes in this area rarely reach the appointment stage itself — they tend to surface later, once the adjustment has been produced and someone objects to its content.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where is the average adjustment carried out?

Under TTK Article 1279/1, in general average the determination and apportionment of the loss are carried out <b>at the place of destination</b> or, if that place is not reached, <b>at the port where the voyage ended</b>. That place also determines the court with jurisdiction over objection and approval proceedings concerning the adjustment (Articles 1281 and 1284). The rule ensures that the process takes place where the ship, cargo and freight concerned are actually together.

### Who appoints the average adjuster?

Under TTK Article 1280/1, the adjustment is carried out by one or more adjusters appointed <b>unanimously by the interested parties</b>. If unanimity cannot be achieved among them, the adjuster is appointed by the <b>court</b> at the place specified in Article 1279. The adjuster is not a court-appointed expert but an independent specialist who draws up the general average account; once the report is approved by the court, it acquires the character of a judgment.

### Must the interested parties provide documents to the adjuster?

Yes. Under TTK Article 1280/2, <b>each of the interested parties</b> is obliged to give the adjuster the documents in their possession that are needed for the adjustment — in particular <b>charterparties, bills of lading and invoices</b>. In return, under Article 1280/4, the adjuster must allow the interested parties to examine the adjustment and, on request, provide a copy against payment of the costs. These reciprocal duties ensure the transparency of the adjustment.
