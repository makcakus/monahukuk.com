TTK Art. 1256 is the centerpiece of the passenger carriage chapter — a ten-subsection provision governing liability for a passenger's death, injury, or baggage loss. Its structure draws on the international Athens Convention relating to the Carriage of Passengers and their Luggage by Sea, though it is not a direct translation. This first of two articles covers subsections (1)-(4): the liability triggers.

## Death or Injury From a Shipping Incident: Three Tiers

Art. 1256(1) applies where death or injury results from a "shipping incident" — a defined term covering wreck, capsizing, stranding, collision, onboard explosion, fire, or defect. Three tiers apply. First, a **baseline liability capped at 250,000 SDR** per passenger per incident, essentially strict (fault-independent) up to that limit. Second, an **exemption**: the carrier escapes liability entirely if it proves the incident resulted from war, terrorism, civil war, insurrection, an exceptional and unavoidable natural event, or a third party's deliberate act intended to cause it — narrow, catastrophic categories outside the carrier's operational control. Third, **unlimited exposure**: if the carrier is at fault, it is liable beyond the 250,000 SDR cap too — and the burden of proving the absence of fault sits with the carrier, not the claimant.

## Non-Incident Death or Injury: Ordinary Fault Liability

Art. 1256(2) covers death or injury *not* caused by a shipping incident — a fall, a medical event, an altercation between passengers. Here the standard flips: the carrier is liable only if at fault, and the claimant bears the burden of proving that fault. The distinction tracks operational control: a genuine shipping incident sits squarely within the carrier's domain (seaworthiness, maintenance, crew competence), justifying a presumption against the carrier; an ordinary onboard mishap is closer to general premises liability, where the traditional claimant-proves-fault rule applies.

## Cabin Baggage vs. Other Baggage

For cabin baggage — items kept under the passenger's own custody — the carrier is liable only for fault, though fault is presumed where the loss results from a shipping incident, rebuttably (Art. 1256(3)). For baggage other than cabin baggage — items surrendered to the carrier's custody — the standard is the strongest of the three: the carrier is liable unless it proves the absence of fault from the outset (Art. 1256(4)), reflecting the same custody-based logic used in cargo liability.
