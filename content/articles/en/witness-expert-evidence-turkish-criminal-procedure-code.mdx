Anyone facing a criminal investigation or trial in Turkey — whether as a suspect, a defendant, a victim, or family member trying to understand what is happening — will eventually run into the same three words: witness, expert, and inspection. Turkish criminal procedure builds its fact-finding almost entirely around these three tools, each governed by its own detailed rules under the Criminal Procedure Code (CMK). A witness statement taken the wrong way, an expert appointed outside the law's narrow conditions, or an autopsy conducted without the required safeguards can each become the reason an otherwise solid case collapses on appeal. This article explains, in plain terms, how each of these evidentiary tools works, what rights and limits attach to them, and why the procedural details matter as much as the substance of what is found.

## Witness Testimony: The Core Evidentiary Tool

A witness is someone who reports to the court information they perceived directly — typically by sight or hearing — about the events at issue. Turkish law treats witness testimony as the most traditional and most frequently used means of proof in a criminal case, and it regulates the summons procedure in detail. As a rule, a witness is formally summoned in writing, and that summons must warn the person of the consequences of failing to appear; in cases involving a detained suspect, the court can go further and order the witness brought in by force if necessary. The law also allows a more practical route — summoning someone by phone, fax, or e-mail — but a summons issued this informal way does not carry the same coercive consequences as a written one. A notable carve-out exists for the President of the Republic, who may decline to testify at his own discretion and, if he does agree, can give a statement at his residence or in writing rather than appearing in person. This exception illustrates a broader point: the duty to testify in Turkish law is not absolute, and it can be adjusted depending on who the witness is.

## Who May Refuse to Testify, and Why

Testifying is, in principle, a civic duty owed to the court. Turkish law relaxes that duty, however, for people who share a close personal bond with the suspect or defendant. A fiancé or fiancée, a spouse (even after the marriage has ended), blood relatives in the direct line of ascent or descent, blood relatives up to the third degree and relatives by marriage up to the second degree, and anyone connected through an adoptive relationship, may all decline to testify. The reasoning behind this list is straightforward: the law does not want to force someone to choose between honesty in court and loyalty to a close relative, or to place a family member in the position of providing evidence that incriminates their own kin.

A separate and conceptually different right to refuse exists for any witness, regardless of family connection: a witness may decline to answer a specific question if answering truthfully would expose the witness — or one of the close relatives listed above — to criminal prosecution. This is the testimonial version of the broader principle against self-incrimination, and it applies to any witness, not just those on the family list. Both types of refusal right share one critical procedural requirement: the witness must be told about the right before being questioned. Failing to give this warning is one of the most common procedural defects encountered in practice, and it is a defect that can, on its own, lead an appellate court to overturn a conviction.

The manner in which testimony is taken also affects its evidentiary weight. As a rule, a witness testifies before a judge or the court and, where appropriate, under oath, which is meant to provide an additional assurance of truthfulness. Children under fifteen and people who lack the capacity to understand the significance of an oath testify without one, though this does not strip their statement of evidentiary value — it simply calls for more careful evaluation. Where a witness's account during the investigation phase conflicts with what they say at trial, the court is expected to re-examine the witness and get to the bottom of the discrepancy; failing to do so leaves the judgment resting on shaky ground. This is also why Turkish procedure places such emphasis on hearing witnesses live and directly during trial preparation and the trial itself, rather than relying purely on paper statements taken earlier in the process.

## Expert Examination: Its Scope and Its Limits

An expert brings specialized or technical knowledge into the proceedings that the judge does not possess — but the boundary of what an expert can be asked to do is drawn just as carefully as the boundary around who can testify. Turkish law only permits an expert opinion to be sought where resolving an issue genuinely requires specialized, particular, or technical knowledge; it expressly forbids resorting to an expert for matters that ordinary general knowledge, or the legal knowledge expected of a judge, can resolve. In other words, legal characterization of the facts remains the judge's job, and an expert cannot be used as a shortcut around that responsibility. For the same reason, someone with a legal education cannot be appointed as an expert unless they can document a separate specialization outside the field of law.

The power to appoint an expert, and to decide — with reasons — that more than one expert is needed, belongs in principle to the judge or the court. During the investigation phase, though, the public prosecutor holds this same power, reflecting the fast-moving, evidence-gathering nature of that stage of the process. Because the rules on witnesses apply by extension to experts (except where a specific rule says otherwise), an expert can likewise be summoned, examined under oath, and, if necessary, compelled to appear. But an expert's report is fundamentally different from a witness's account: it is not a direct observation but the product of a technical evaluation, and the judge remains free to scrutinize that evaluation and, if warranted, order a fresh examination by another expert.

## On-Site Inspection and Autopsy

An on-site inspection (keşif) is the direct, unmediated examination of a disputed place, object, or state of affairs by the judge or another competent authority. As a rule, it is carried out by the judge or the court, a delegated judge, or a judge or court acting on a letter of request; but where any delay would create a risk — most obviously where the scene itself might deteriorate or be disturbed — the public prosecutor may carry out the inspection directly. The law requires the inspection record to note not only what was found, but also which evidence one would reasonably have expected to find, given the nature of the incident, yet could not be located. Recording this kind of negative finding matters just as much as recording a positive one, because it allows a later review of the evidence to check whether the inspection was thorough. An expert is frequently brought in during an inspection, but that appointment still has to follow the same conditions that apply to expert appointments generally — otherwise, the "expert" opinion gathered at the scene is nothing more than the personal view of someone without the proper standing, and it cannot support the judgment.

Autopsy is a specialized form of inspection aimed specifically at determining the medical cause of death, and it plays a decisive role in clarifying suspicious deaths. As a rule, it must be performed by two physicians in the presence of the public prosecutor, ideally one specializing in forensic medicine and the other in pathology (or another relevant field); a physician brought by defense counsel or by a victim's representative may also be present to observe. Only where circumstances genuinely require it can a single physician conduct the autopsy, and in that case the report must expressly state that this exception applied. Where the body's condition allows, the autopsy must cover the head, chest, and abdomen. A physician who treated the deceased in the illness immediately preceding death cannot be assigned to perform the autopsy — a safeguard against any conflict of interest — though that physician may be asked to be present and to explain the course of the illness. Exhumation of a buried body for examination or autopsy is decided by the prosecutor during the investigation phase and by the court during trial, and, unless doing so would compromise the purpose of the inquiry or prove impractical, a close relative of the deceased must be notified promptly. All of these procedures are required to be video-recorded as they take place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If a family member is called as a witness against a suspect, can they simply refuse to testify?**
Yes, within the categories the law lists — spouses (even after the marriage ended), fiancés and fiancées, direct ascendant and descendant relatives, and blood or marriage relatives within specified degrees. This right only functions if the authorities warn the witness of it beforehand; testimony taken without that warning cannot properly be used as the basis for a conviction.

**Can a court rely on an expert opinion for a purely legal question, such as whether someone acted with fault?**
No, as a rule. Turkish law reserves purely legal characterization and questions resolvable through ordinary general knowledge or the judge's own legal training for the judge; an expert may only be appointed where specialized or technical knowledge is genuinely required.

**Who can be present during an autopsy, and can the deceased's own treating doctor perform it?**
The public prosecutor must be present, and a physician brought by defense counsel or a victim's representative may attend as well. The doctor who treated the deceased during the final illness cannot conduct the autopsy, although that doctor may attend and provide information about the illness's progression.

**What happens if an inspection report is incomplete or an autopsy fails to establish the cause of death?**
Turkish appellate practice treats incomplete evidentiary work as a ground for reversal. Where a report itself flags an unresolved question — for example, an inconclusive cause of death — the trial court is expected to pursue that further inquiry before reaching a verdict; deciding the case without doing so is considered an incomplete investigation.
