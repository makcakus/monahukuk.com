Once liability is established under TTK Art. 549-554, Art. 555-561 govern who can sue, what a release actually covers, and how long claims survive.

## Who Can Sue — the Turkish Equivalent of a Derivative Action

Art. 555 lets both the company and any individual shareholder claim compensation for company loss — but a shareholder can only demand payment to the company, not to themselves. This is Turkish law's version of the derivative action, letting a shareholder act where company organs fail to. If the shareholder's claim was reasonably justified but ultimately unsuccessful, courts may apportion costs equitably rather than leaving them entirely on the shareholder (Art. 555/2). In bankruptcy, company creditors gain the same right, with the bankruptcy administration acting first and individual creditors or shareholders able to step in if it doesn't (Art. 556).

## Liability Is Now Differentiated, Not Automatically Joint

Art. 557 marked a sharp break from the old absolute joint-liability model: where multiple people are liable for the same damage, each is jointly liable only to the extent the loss is personally attributable to them based on their own fault. A director who did not cause a particular loss cannot be held liable for it merely by virtue of board membership — courts must determine each defendant's individual share.

## The Limits of a General Assembly Release

Art. 558 makes clear a release resolution cannot later be revoked by the assembly itself (subject to the general grounds for challenging assembly resolutions). Critically, release only covers the material facts actually disclosed to the assembly — anything concealed or misrepresented falls outside its scope and remains open to a liability claim. Art. 559 adds a stricter rule for incorporation and capital increases: liability arising from those specifically cannot be settled or released for four years after registration, and even after that, minority shareholders holding at least 10% can block approval.

## Limitation Periods and Jurisdiction

Claims are time-barred after two years from when the claimant learned of the damage and the responsible party, and in any case five years from the damaging act (Art. 560) — longer criminal limitation periods apply if the conduct is also a crime. Jurisdiction lies with the commercial court at the company's registered office, but this is a permissive, not exclusive, venue rule (Art. 561).
