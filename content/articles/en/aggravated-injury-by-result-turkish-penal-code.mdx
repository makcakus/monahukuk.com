Turkish criminal law treats intentional injury as a graduated offense: the basic act of hurting another person is only the starting point, and the sentence rises sharply once that act leaves the victim with a lasting or life-threatening consequence. Article 87 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) supplies this escalation mechanism. It does not create a separate crime; it attaches heavier punishment to the ordinary intentional-injury offense defined in Article 86 whenever the injury actually produces one of a list of serious outcomes — permanent loss of a bodily function, lasting facial disfigurement, danger to life, a bone fracture, or death. For anyone dealing with a Turkish criminal case involving a physical assault with lasting medical consequences, understanding how this escalation works, and how it is proven, is essential.

## The Interest the Law Protects

At its core, Article 87 protects the same interest as the basic injury offense: bodily integrity and health. But because its trigger is a serious physical outcome rather than the act itself, its protective reach extends further. The listed outcomes touch a person's senses and organ functions, their ability to speak or to have children, their physical appearance, and — where the injury proves fatal — their life itself. Where the victim is a pregnant woman, the provision also protects the normal course of the pregnancy, treating early birth and miscarriage as outcomes of different severity.

## How the Escalation Is Structured

Article 87 does not impose a single fixed increase. It grades the punishment according to how serious the resulting harm is, layering three separate mechanisms on top of the base sentence set for intentional injury under Article 86.

**First tier — sentence doubled by half again.** Where the injury causes permanent weakening of a sense or organ function, lasting difficulty in speech, a permanent mark on the face, a situation endangering the victim's life, or — where the victim is pregnant — premature birth of the child, the sentence otherwise applicable under Article 86 is increased by one-half again (in effect, roughly one and a half times the base term). The law also sets floor sentences for this tier so that judicial discretion cannot push the punishment below a statutory minimum.

**Second tier — sentence doubled.** A materially graver set of outcomes doubles the base sentence: an incurable illness or a vegetative state, complete loss (not merely weakening) of a sense or organ function, loss of the ability to speak or to conceive children, a permanent change to the general appearance of the face, or — for a pregnant victim — miscarriage. Because these outcomes are more severe than the first tier, the statutory minimum sentences attached to this tier are correspondingly higher.

**Bone fractures and dislocations — a separate, proportional rule.** Rather than a fixed multiplier, a fracture or dislocation caused by the intentional injury increases the base sentence by up to one-half, with the exact increase tied to how much the fracture or dislocation affects the victim's vital functions. This makes the medical report's own grading of severity directly determinative of the sentence, rather than triggering an automatic doubling regardless of degree.

**Death as a result — a wholly different sentencing range.** If the injury results in the victim's death, the law abandons the multiplier approach altogether and prescribes its own independent prison term, set at a materially higher range than either of the two injury tiers.

The distinctions between adjoining categories matter in practice: a permanent *weakening* of a function is treated differently from its outright *loss*; a *permanent mark* on the face is treated differently from a *change to the general appearance* of the face; danger to life must be a concrete, demonstrated risk rather than an abstract possibility. Because these distinctions turn on medical fact, forensic reports — and in more complex cases, supplementary review by Turkey's official forensic medicine institute — tend to decide how a case is ultimately classified, even though the legal characterization itself remains for the court to decide.

## Who Can Be Held Responsible, and for What State of Mind

Anyone can commit this offense or be its victim — there is no special status required of either side. What matters is the offender's state of mind toward the injury on one hand, and toward the aggravating outcome on the other, because Turkish law applies a two-tier mental-state requirement here. The underlying act of causing physical harm must be intentional: the offender must have deliberately set out to harm the victim's body. The aggravated outcome itself, however, does not need to be intended — it is enough that the offender could and should have foreseen it, the ordinary standard for negligence under Turkish law. This is the doctrinal core of the provision: liability for the aggravated result cannot be strict or automatic. If the serious outcome was not foreseeable and did not stem from a failure of care, the enhanced sentence does not apply and the offender answers only for the basic injury. Where the offender actually intended the aggravated outcome, liability follows all the more readily — and where the outcome is death and the offender intended to kill, the case is no longer an aggravated-injury matter at all, but intentional homicide.

## Distinguishing Aggravated Injury Resulting in Death From Attempted Murder

The most contested classification question in practice is where to draw the line between an aggravated injury that happens to end in death and an attempted killing. The decisive factor is the direction of the offender's intent, not the outcome. If the offender acted with intent to kill, the offense is attempted murder even if the victim survives; if the offender acted only with intent to injure and death nevertheless followed, the aggravated-injury-resulting-in-death provision applies instead. Courts weigh a cluster of circumstantial factors to infer that intent — the type of weapon or object used, the number and force of the blows, which part of the body was targeted, the relationship between the parties, behavior before and after the incident, and whether the offender stopped the attack voluntarily or was prevented from continuing. Where intent to injure cannot be established at all, the case instead falls under Turkey's negligent-injury provisions.

## Attempt and Multiple-Party Liability

Because the aggravated result requires, at minimum, negligence rather than intent, an "attempt" to bring about that result is not a recognized legal category — a person either causes the aggravated outcome or does not, and if it does not occur, no enhancement applies; the offender remains liable only for the underlying injury, whether completed or attempted. Where several people participate in the underlying assault, each participant's liability for the aggravated result is assessed individually: the outcome must have been foreseeable to that particular participant, and the negligence link must be established separately for each. A participant for whom that link cannot be shown answers only for the basic injury, not the aggravated result. Where a single act produces more than one aggravated outcome, sentencing is anchored to the most serious result, while the remaining outcomes are factored into how the base sentence is set; where multiple victims are harmed by the same act, a separate offense is treated as having occurred for each victim.

## Procedural Features

Because the punishment ranges attached to Article 87 run materially higher than for basic intentional injury, jurisdiction shifts accordingly: cases falling within the death-resulting tier, given the length of sentence involved, are generally heard by the heavy penal courts rather than the ordinary criminal courts of first instance that handle most injury cases. The offense is prosecuted regardless of whether the victim wishes to pursue it — it is not subject to a private complaint requirement, so withdrawal of a complaint does not end the case, and it falls outside the mediation mechanisms available for some lesser offenses. Because the case turns so heavily on forensic classification, an incomplete or internally inconsistent medical report is one of the most common grounds on which convictions are challenged or sent back for further evidentiary development.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Article 87 create a separate crime from ordinary intentional injury?**
No. It is an aggravating-result provision layered onto the basic intentional-injury offense. A court must first confirm that the underlying injury offense is established before considering whether one of the aggravated outcomes applies.

**Does the offender need to have intended the serious outcome for the enhanced sentence to apply?**
No. Intent is required only for the underlying act of injuring the victim. For the aggravated result itself, it is enough that the offender could reasonably have foreseen it — the ordinary negligence standard. If that link cannot be shown, only the basic injury offense applies.

**How is a case classified when the medical evidence is ambiguous, for example when a facial mark has not fully healed at the time of examination?**
Forensic evaluation practice generally requires the healing process to be complete — commonly assessed only after a period of some months — before a mark or functional impairment can reliably be classified as permanent. A premature examination can lead to an incorrect classification and is a frequent basis for challenging a report or seeking supplementary evaluation.

**Can withdrawing a complaint end a prosecution under this provision?**
No. Because the offense is prosecuted regardless of the victim's wishes, withdrawing a complaint does not terminate the case, and the offense is not eligible for mediation.
