When drafting a commercial contract, or choosing a route in a dispute that has already arisen, there are three options: mediation, arbitration and litigation. They are presented as alternatives to one another, but in reality they perform different functions — and the right question is not "which is better?" but "what do I need in this dispute?".

## The Basic Distinction: Who Decides?

The 3rd Civil Chamber of the Turkish Court of Cassation defines alternative dispute resolution as "a family of dispute resolution methods operating as an optional route alongside litigation before the State courts, in which a generally impartial third person participates in order to assist and contribute to the resolution of an existing dispute". Its aim is that "the parties reach a solution by their own will in matters that fall within private law, do not concern public policy, and may be freely disposed of".

Within that definition lies the following distinction:

**In mediation the parties produce the solution.** The mediator does not decide; they establish a communication process so that the parties can produce their own solution. If it emerges that the parties cannot do so, the mediator may make a proposal, but that proposal is not binding.

**In arbitration the arbitrator decides**, and the award is binding like a court judgment. The Assembly for the Unification of Case Law of the Court of Cassation has stated that an arbitral award is binding in the same way as a decision given by the State courts.

**In litigation the judge decides**, and all the guarantees of State justice and the appellate routes apply.

## A Comparison on Eight Criteria

**Who decides?** In mediation, the parties — the mediator does not decide. In arbitration, the arbitrator or arbitral tribunal. In litigation, the judge.

**Is it compulsory?** In mediation, **applying** is compulsory in certain disputes; settling is never compulsory. Arbitration requires a contract or a special statutory provision. Litigation is the general route.

**How long does it take?** In mediation the statutory maximum is three to six weeks. In arbitration the proceedings are measured in months; in domestic arbitration a one-year period is provided as a rule. Litigation, including appeals, can take years.

**Confidentiality.** In mediation confidentiality is protected by statute and its breach is an offence punishable by up to six months' imprisonment. In arbitration confidentiality is secured by contract. In litigation hearings are as a rule public.

**Fees and costs.** In mediation a **fixed** fee is charged on the application for an enforceability annotation. In arbitration the arbitrator's fee and institutional costs arise. In litigation a **proportional** court fee is paid.

**The resulting instrument.** In mediation, a settlement document — in most cases in the nature of a court judgment. In arbitration, an arbitral award. In litigation, a court judgment.

**Appellate review.** There is no appeal against a mediation settlement; only an action for invalidity can be brought. An arbitral award may be challenged by an action for annulment on limited grounds. A court judgment is subject to appeal and cassation.

**If the other party does not participate.** In mediation the process still concludes and final minutes are drawn up; the non-attending party faces a sanction. Arbitration and litigation proceed even if the other party does not take part.

## Arbitrability and Mediability: Not Every Route Fits Every Dispute

Each of the three routes has its own limits, and they do not overlap.

**Mediation** requires three conditions: that the dispute concerns private law, that it arises from a matter or transaction over which the parties may freely dispose, and that it does not involve an allegation of domestic violence.

**Arbitration** is subject to a prohibition in Article 408 of the Turkish Code of Civil Procedure: disputes arising from **rights in rem over immovable property**, or from matters not subject to the will of the two parties, are not arbitrable.

This difference matters: disputes concerning the transfer of an immovable or the creation of a limited right in rem over it are **capable of mediation** under Article 17/B of Law No. 6325, but are not arbitrable.

**Litigation** is as a rule open to every dispute; the only closure is where the statute makes another route compulsory.

## Cases of Compulsory Arbitration

Arbitration too is not always voluntary. The Court of Cassation notes that statutory provisions make recourse to arbitration compulsory for certain disputes; the examples cited include Article 13 of the Notaries Law, Article 51 of the Law on Trade Unions and Collective Labour Agreements, Article 30 of the Insurance Law and Article 68 of the Law on the Protection of Consumers.

The consequence for mediation is this: under Article 18/A/18 of Law No. 6325, where special statutes make recourse to arbitration or another alternative dispute resolution route compulsory, or where **an arbitration agreement exists**, the provisions on mediation as a condition precedent do not apply.

In other words, if your contract contains a valid arbitration clause, the obligation of mandatory mediation falls away.

## When Is an Arbitration Clause Valid?

The criterion applied by the 3rd Civil Chamber is clear: for an arbitration clause or agreement to be valid, the parties must have expressed their intention to arbitrate **clearly and definitively, in a way that gives rise to no argument or confusion**.

In one decision the Chamber considered that a clause providing for prior recourse to mediation might deprive the arbitration clause of the definitive, doubt-free character it must have. Yet the same Chamber upheld as valid a clause reading "if the mediation attempt produces no result, the dispute shall be resolved by the Bar Arbitration Board", on the ground that the parties' intention was clear and left no room for doubt.

The practical lesson: if an arbitration clause is to be inserted into a contract, its relationship with the mediation stage must be written **expressly and in tiers**.

## Med-Arb: Combining the Two

An increasingly common model in practice is the sequential use of both routes. In scholarship and practice this is called **"med-arb" (mediation–arbitration)**; its particular form is known as **"med-then-arb"**.

The 3rd Civil Chamber has expressly found this model lawful. According to that decision, the parties may agree in a dispute resolution agreement that the dispute will first be addressed through mediation and that, failing a result, arbitration will be commenced. In this procedure the recourse to arbitration after mediation is agreed in advance, and **the two processes continue to exist as entirely independent routes, without merging into one another**.

The model also has a statutory footing. Starting from the fact that Article 5/1 of Law No. 6325 frames the exclusion of evidence as applying "when a civil action is brought **or arbitration is commenced**", the Chamber concluded that the legislator expressly contemplated recourse to arbitration after mediation. It also noted that the Turkish Mediators' Code of Ethics provides that a mediator may, where necessary and appropriate, suggest that the parties consider resolving their dispute through arbitration, neutral evaluation or other methods.

The model's only condition: the dispute must be **capable of both mediation and arbitration**.

## Which Route in Which Situation?

**Mediation is suitable where:** the relationship between the parties will continue (neighbour law, condominium, an ongoing commercial relationship); the dispute is largely about figures; speed is critical; confidentiality matters; a solution the court cannot give is sought (division in kind, a time restriction, a payment schedule); and, of course, it is compulsory wherever it is a condition precedent.

**Arbitration is suitable where:** the dispute requires technical expertise and that expertise is sought in choosing the arbitrator; the parties are in different countries and a neutral forum is wanted; recognition and enforcement of the award abroad matters (the New York Convention); confidentiality is wanted but a binding decision is also needed.

**Litigation is necessary where:** the dispute concerns public policy (bankruptcy, determination of service, personal status); the rights of third persons are affected; coercive measures such as interim measures or attachment are needed; the other party shows no willingness to settle; and where the dispute is neither arbitrable nor capable of mediation.

## Comparing the Outcomes

**A mediation settlement.** No action may be brought on the matters agreed. A document signed jointly by the parties, their lawyers and the mediator counts as an instrument in the nature of a court judgment without any annotation; in commercial disputes the signatures of the lawyers and the mediator suffice. There is no appeal; only an action asserting invalidity can be brought, and a claim based on a defect of consent is subject to a one-year preclusive period.

**An arbitral award.** It is binding and final. The remedy is an **action for annulment** on the limited grounds in Article 439 of the Turkish Code of Civil Procedure. The Court of Cassation stresses that the grounds for annulment are exhaustively listed, that it is not possible to examine whether the award is correct on the merits, and that whether the arbitrators applied the law correctly cannot be made the subject of review in an annulment action.

**A court judgment.** It is subject to appeal and cassation; once final it is enforced through judgment-based enforcement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: My contract has an arbitration clause — must I still go to mediation?

Under Article 18/A/18 of Law No. 6325, where an arbitration agreement exists the provisions on mediation as a condition precedent do not apply. But for the arbitration clause to be valid, the parties' intention must have been expressed clearly and definitively; a doubtful clause may result in the mediation requirement being applied after all.

### Q: If we fail to settle in mediation, can we go to arbitration?

Yes. The Court of Cassation has found the med-then-arb model lawful, treating the fact that Article 5/1 of Law No. 6325 provides for the exclusion of evidence in arbitration as well as its statutory footing. It is advisable to set out this tiered structure expressly in the contract.

### Q: Can I arbitrate a dispute over immovable property?

No. Under Article 408 of the Turkish Code of Civil Procedure, disputes arising from rights in rem over immovable property are not arbitrable. By contrast, under Article 17/B of Law No. 6325, disputes concerning the transfer of an immovable or the creation of a limited right in rem over it **are capable of mediation**.

### Q: Which is cheaper?

Mediation is as a rule the least costly: where no settlement is reached the fee for up to two hours is covered from the Ministry's budget, and only a fixed fee is charged for the annotation. In arbitration the arbitrator's fee and institutional costs can reach significant sums. In litigation proportional court fees and litigation costs arise.

### Q: Can I appeal against a mediation settlement?

No. A settlement is a contract, not a court decision; there is no appellate route. Only an action for annulment relying on grounds of invalidity can be brought, and that claim is subject to a one-year preclusive period.
