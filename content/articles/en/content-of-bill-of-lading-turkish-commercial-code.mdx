Having defined what a bill of lading is, TTK Art. 1229 sets out what a well-drafted one should contain — fifteen items that, taken together, fall into four functional groups.

## Four Groups of Required Entries

The cargo-description group covers the general type of goods (as declared by the shipper — not independently verified by the carrier), identifying marks, whether the cargo is dangerous, package count and weight, plus the cargo's apparent external condition on receipt — the reference point for later comparing condition at origin against condition at destination. The party-identity group covers the carrier's name and place of business, the captain's name, the ship's name and flag, the shipper's name, and — only if disclosed by the shipper — the consignee's name. The voyage-logistics group covers the loading and discharge ports, the date cargo was received, and where and when the bill was issued. The legal-and-commercial-terms group covers the signature of the carrier or its agent, whether freight is payable by the consignee (and how much), any agreed delivery date at discharge, any clause expanding liability limits beyond the statutory floor, and any other terms the parties agree to include.

Together these four groups let a downstream holder — a financing bank, a buyer purchasing cargo while still at sea, an insurer — transact with confidence without needing to independently investigate the underlying freight contract.

## A Missing Entry Does Not Invalidate the Bill

The most doctrinally important line in Art. 1229 is its second paragraph: missing one or more of these fifteen items does not stop a document from legally counting as a bill of lading, as long as it still satisfies the narrower core definitional elements — proof of a carriage contract, evidence of receipt or loading, and the surrender-against-delivery feature. Turkish courts apply this distinction in practice: even the carrier's name being entirely omitted does not invalidate the bill, since the law provides a fallback presumption treating the shipowner as the carrier in that situation. Courts have also confirmed the shipper's own signature on a bill is optional — only the carrier's signature is required for the document to function as negotiable security.

## Why This Matters

For anyone drafting, reviewing, or relying on a bill of lading — particularly in letter-of-credit transactions — the practical lesson is that completeness is a quality standard, not a validity gate. A carrier cannot escape liability by pointing to a minor missing field; courts look to the document's substantive function first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What information must appear on a bill of lading?

The entries in Article 1229/1 TTK fall into four thematic groups: <b>relating to the goods</b> (nature, marks, information on dangerous goods, number of packages or pieces, weight or quantity, apparent condition), <b>relating to the parties</b> (the carrier's name and place of business, the loader's name, the consignee and the notify address), <b>relating to the carriage</b> (name of the ship, ports of loading and discharge, freight and payment terms) and <b>relating to form</b> (place and date of issue, number of originals, the carrier's signature).

### Does a missing entry invalidate the bill of lading?

No. Under Article 1229/2 TTK <b>the absence of one or more of the elements listed in the first paragraph does not prevent the instrument from being treated in law as a bill of lading</b>, provided it satisfies the definition in Article 1228/1: that it evidences the conclusion of the contract of carriage, shows that the goods were taken over or loaded, and establishes that the carrier is bound to deliver only against its presentation. Here the law looks to <b>substance rather than form</b>.

### What follows if the apparent condition of the goods is not entered?

It works against the carrier. Under Article 1229/1 the <b>apparent condition</b> of the goods must be shown on the bill of lading. Under Article 1239/2 TTK, if the carrier omits that statement, <b>it is deemed to have stated in the bill that the goods were in apparent good order and condition</b>. Silence thus produces the effect of a clean bill, and the carrier cannot argue that damage visible on delivery existed before loading.
