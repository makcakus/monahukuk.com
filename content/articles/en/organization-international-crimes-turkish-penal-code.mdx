Turkish criminal law does not wait for genocide or crimes against humanity to actually occur before intervening. Article 78 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), positioned immediately after the substantive offenses of genocide (Article 76) and crimes against humanity (Article 77), criminalizes the act of founding, leading or joining an organization whose purpose is to commit those offenses — regardless of whether the organization ever carries out a single act of genocide or an atrocity against a civilian population. The premise behind the provision is that atrocities of this magnitude are rarely the product of isolated, spontaneous decisions; they are almost always the output of an organized, hierarchical structure with continuity over time. By criminalizing the structure itself at the moment it reaches a certain threshold of seriousness, Turkish law pushes criminal liability back to a preparatory stage, well before any underlying atrocity takes place. For foreign counsel encountering this provision, the key is to see Article 78 not as a variant of ordinary organized-crime law but as a distinct, heavily aggravated offense tied to the two gravest crimes in the Turkish Penal Code.

## What Interest the Provision Protects

Article 78 protects several layers of interest simultaneously. At the most direct level, it protects the same values underlying Articles 76 and 77: the continued existence of national, ethnic, racial or religious groups, and human dignity and peace more broadly. Because an organization pursuing genocide or crimes against humanity is itself a source of danger to those values, the law intervenes before any actual violation occurs.

A second layer is public safety and public order. The mere presence of a structured, goal-driven group with an internal hierarchy and a degree of permanence is treated as inherently destabilizing to society, independent of whether the group's aims are ever realized. A third layer reflects Turkey's international-law obligations to prevent atrocity crimes; Article 78 can be read as a domestic implementation of commitments Turkey has undertaken in that field. Together, these three layers explain both the severity of the prescribed penalties and the unusual procedural rules — most notably the absence of any statute of limitations — attached to the offense.

## The Physical Elements of the Offense

### Who Can Commit It

Article 78 is not limited to a special category of offenders; anyone can commit it. The provision does, however, distinguish three roles within the organization, each carrying a different penalty: founding the organization, leading it, and being a member of it. A founder is someone who contributes, through their own will, to bringing the structure into existence. A leader is someone who actually exercises command or issues instructions over the organization as a whole or over part of it. A member is someone who has been absorbed into the hierarchical structure and stands ready to carry out tasks assigned in furtherance of the organization's purpose. Classification depends on the person's actual functional role within the structure, not on any formal title they may hold.

### Who Is Harmed

Because this is a risk-based offense, the law does not require an identifiable, individual victim for the offense to be complete. Since public safety and public order are also protected interests, the offense is understood as harming society as a whole, with the specific group targeted by the organization's aims facing a particularly acute threat. It is not necessary for that target group to be precisely defined at the time the organization is formed — it is enough that the group is capable of being identified.

### The Conduct Itself

The offense can be committed through any one of three alternative acts: founding the organization, leading it, or becoming a member of it. Committing any single one of these acts is sufficient to complete the offense. Where the same person both founds and leads the organization, only one offense is deemed to have occurred, though this fact is taken into account in sentencing.

The defining feature of the offense is that it does not require any of the organization's intended crimes to have actually been committed. It is enough that the organization has been established — meaning the structure has reached a level that the law treats as posing a genuine danger. Because Article 78 itself does not spell out how that threshold is measured, Turkish courts and legal scholarship draw by analogy on the criteria developed for the general organized-crime offense under Article 220: the number of members must be sufficient to realistically carry out the intended crimes (in practice, at least three people), there must be a hierarchical link among the members, the association must have a degree of continuity, and the members must share a common purpose. A loose, temporary or coincidental gathering of people does not amount to an organization in this sense; at most, ordinary rules on complicity in a specific act may apply instead.

## The Mental Element

Article 78 can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligence-based version of the offense. General intent alone, however, is not enough — the text expressly requires the specific purpose of committing one of the offenses set out in the preceding articles (genocide or crimes against humanity). This makes Article 78 a purpose-based, or specially intentioned, offense. The person must know that the organization exists to pursue genocide or crimes against humanity and must join or contribute to the structure while embracing that purpose.

In practice, this mental element operates as the decisive filter for liability. Someone who joins a group without knowing its true purpose, or who believes they are joining an organization pursuing an entirely different goal, cannot be held liable under Article 78. Because subjective purpose is difficult to observe directly, courts typically infer it from objective indicators — the organization's propaganda, its internal rules, its training activities, and any preparatory steps that manifest externally.

## Distinction From the General Organized-Crime Offense (Article 220)

Article 78 stands in a special, self-contained relationship with Article 220, the general offense of forming an organization to commit crimes. Where an organization's purpose is specifically genocide or crimes against humanity, the principle that a special provision displaces a general one means that only Article 78 applies — a defendant is not separately punished under Article 220 as well. Turkish appellate case law has confirmed this same boundary in the context of other special organized-crime offenses.

The distinction matters in practice. The sentencing ranges under the two provisions differ; unlike Article 220, Article 78 offenses are never subject to a statute of limitations; and the text of Article 78 is silent on whether ancillary rules found in the Article 220 framework — such as liability for assisting an organization, propaganda on its behalf, or the effective-remorse discount available under Article 221 — extend to Article 78 by analogy. Because Turkish law prohibits extending criminal liability by analogy against a defendant, commentators who favor a literal reading argue that these ancillary rules cannot simply be imported into Article 78 without a specific legislative basis. It is also worth noting candidly that Turkey's body of case law addressing Article 78 on its own terms remains very limited, so much of this discussion continues to operate at a largely theoretical level.

## Attempt, Complicity and Concurrence

On attempt: because founding an organization and joining one are conduct-based acts rather than result-based acts, attempt is generally difficult to establish. Some commentators argue that attempt may still be conceivable where the steps taken to found an organization can be broken down into distinct stages and the founding effort is left incomplete for reasons outside the defendant's control. Attempt has essentially no practical relevance to the act of leading an organization.

On complicity: this offense is, by its nature, one that requires more than one participant — plurality of offenders is itself a structural element of the crime. As a result, ordinary complicity rules are not applied among the organization's own members. Outside contributors who assist the organization without becoming part of its structure, however, remain subject to the general complicity provisions of the Penal Code.

On concurrence: liability under Article 78 is independent of any crimes the organization actually goes on to commit. If the organization does carry out an act of genocide or a crime against humanity, the offender is punished separately for both the organizational offense and the underlying crime, under the ordinary rules on concurrence of offenses; the two are not merged into a single combined offense. Remaining within the organization as a member over a period of time is treated as a single, continuing offense rather than as a separate offense for each day of membership.

## Penalties and Procedural Features

A person who founds or leads the organization faces a prison sentence of ten to fifteen years; a person who is merely a member faces a prison sentence of five to ten years. Under the second paragraph of Article 78, legal entities can also be subjected to security measures in connection with these offenses, extending the reach of the provision to corporate or institutional structures used as a front.

The third paragraph contains the article's most striking feature: no statute of limitations applies to these offenses. This is understood to cover both the limitation period for prosecution and the limitation period for enforcing a sentence, meaning that no amount of time elapsed since the conduct bars prosecution or punishment. This mirrors the approach taken for genocide and crimes against humanity themselves under Articles 76 and 77, and reflects, in domestic law, the broader international-law principle that atrocity crimes should not be shielded by the passage of time.

The offense is not subject to a victim's complaint — it is investigated and prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative, and it falls outside the scope of mediation or payment-based settlement mechanisms available for lesser offenses. Because founding or leading the organization carries a sentence exceeding ten years, jurisdiction over those acts lies with the heavy penal courts; jurisdiction over membership alone is debated given its lower maximum sentence, but in practice these cases are typically consolidated and heard together in the heavy penal courts as well. These offenses are also listed among those to which Turkey's extraterritorial jurisdiction rules apply: Turkish law applies even where the conduct takes place abroad, and a foreign conviction or acquittal does not, by itself, prevent Turkey from prosecuting the same conduct if the Minister of Justice requests it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Article 78 require that genocide or a crime against humanity actually took place?**
No. The offense is complete once the organization itself has been established at a level the law treats as dangerous, regardless of whether any underlying atrocity is ever carried out.

**How is Article 78 different from the general organized-crime offense in Article 220?**
Article 78 is a special provision that displaces Article 220 whenever the organization's specific purpose is genocide or crimes against humanity. The penalty ranges differ, and unlike Article 220, offenses under Article 78 are never subject to a statute of limitations.

**Can someone who joined an organization without knowing its true purpose be liable under Article 78?**
No. The offense requires the specific intent to further genocide or crimes against humanity. A person who joined without knowledge of that purpose, or who believed the group pursued a different goal entirely, does not meet the mental element required.

**Is there a time limit for prosecuting these offenses?**
No. Article 78 expressly excludes any statute of limitations, for both prosecution and enforcement of sentence, no matter how much time has passed since the conduct occurred.
