A consignee is often a stranger to the freight contract — someone who acquired the bill of lading later through a sale or endorsement. TTK Art. 1240 decides how much of the carrier-shipper financial bargain can be pushed onto that consignee.

## No Payment Obligation Without a Bill Entry

Under Art. 1240(1), if the bill contains no entry stating that the consignee will pay freight, or will pay demurrage accrued at the loading port, the consignee is presumed *not* liable for either. And — mirroring the clean bill doctrine — the carrier cannot rebut this presumption against a good-faith third-party holder, consignee included, no matter what side arrangement existed between carrier and shipper. A bank or buyer taking the bill should be able to calculate its financial exposure from the document alone, without investigating undisclosed agreements.

## Freight Amount Follows the Bill's Figures

Art. 1240(2) provides that where freight is calculated by measure, count, or weight and those figures appear on the bill, freight is computed from those figures unless the bill states otherwise — and, importantly, a reservation entered under Art. 1239(1) about the cargo description does *not* count as such a contrary term. This prevents a carrier's legitimate cargo-description reservation from being misread as reopening the freight calculation itself.

## Charter References Read Narrowly

Art. 1240(3) limits what a reference to the charter party actually pulls into the bill: such a reference does not automatically import the charter's laytime, demurrage period, or demurrage rate provisions. A buyer who later acquires the bill and sees "freight as per charter party" reasonably assumes this concerns freight amount only — not the far heavier, individually negotiated demurrage terms the original shipper may have accepted.

## Why This Matters

For carriers seeking to recover demurrage from a consignee, this means the entitlement must be clearly and expressly stated on the bill itself — Turkish courts have consistently rejected demurrage claims against consignees where the bill was silent or where a "freight collect" notation was stretched to also cover demurrage. For buyers and banks, it means financial exposure under a bill of lading can generally be read directly off its face.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### If the bill of lading says nothing about freight, must the consignee pay?

As a rule no. Under Article 1240/1 TTK, <b>a bill of lading that contains no entry under Article 1229/1(l) stating that the freight is payable by the consignee, or covering demurrage incurred at the port of loading and payable by the consignee, gives rise to a presumption that the consignee is not liable for freight or demurrage</b>. In practice it is therefore essential that a 'freight collect' entry appear expressly on the bill.

### Does a reference to the charterparty also cover demurrage?

No. Under Article 1240/3 TTK, <b>where reference is made to the contract of carriage for the freight, that reference does not extend to the provisions on discharging time, demurrage time and demurrage money</b>. A general clause such as 'all terms and conditions as per charterparty' therefore does not make the charterparty's demurrage regime binding on a holder who is not the shipper; a clear and separate entry is required.

### Is the freight calculated by the quantity shown in the bill of lading?

Yes, as a rule. Under Article 1240/2 TTK, <b>where the freight has been agreed by reference to the measure, number or weight of the goods and these are shown in the bill of lading, the freight is determined accordingly unless the bill provides otherwise</b>. This departs from the 'quantity delivered' rule of Article 1193, which governs between shipper and carrier: as against the holder of the bill, the entry in the document prevails.
