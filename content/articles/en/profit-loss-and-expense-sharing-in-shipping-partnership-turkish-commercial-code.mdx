TTK Art. 1076-1079 turn from the ship manager to the co-owners themselves, covering the financial and personal mechanics that hold a joint shipping ownership together.

## Profit and Loss, Annually, by Share

Both profit and loss are allocated among co-owners strictly in proportion to their share in the ship (Art. 1076(1)) — the same value-weighted logic seen throughout this structure. Accounting and profit distribution happen once a year, at calendar year-end, reflecting that shipping is a continuous operation better assessed on an annual cycle than voyage by voyage.

## Contributing to Expenses — and What Happens If You Don't

Each co-owner must fund expenses, particularly outfitting and repair costs, in proportion to its share (Art. 1077(1)). If a co-owner defaults and the others advance its share on its behalf, the defaulting owner owes default interest from the date of the advance — automatically, without a formal demand. More distinctively, if the paying co-owners insure their resulting insurable interest in the defaulter's share, the cost of that insurance is also charged back to the defaulter (Art. 1077(2)) — a deliberate deterrent against free-riding within the venture.

## Continuity Despite Changes in Co-Owners

A change in any co-owner's personal circumstances — death, transfer, and the like — does not interrupt the venture (Art. 1078(1)), because the arrangement is fundamentally asset-centered: it exists to keep the *ship* running, not to preserve a fixed group of people. Equally, no co-owner can be expelled by the others (Art. 1078(2)) — ownership can only end voluntarily or through the court-supervised exit mechanism covered in our companion article on dissolution.

## The Captain Who Is Also a Co-Owner

Where a co-owner is also the ship's captain and is dismissed from that role without consent, Art. 1079 gives them a distinctive right: to demand that the other co-owners buy out their share at a value set by experts. The logic is straightforward — once dismissed as captain, this person has lost all operational control and the other owners' trust, so forcing them to stay locked into a venture whose direction they can no longer influence would be unfair. This right lapses, however, if asserted after unreasonable delay.

## Practical Takeaway

Foreign investors structuring shared vessel ownership through this entity type should note that expense-sharing defaults carry real teeth — accumulated advances plus interest and insurance costs can become significant leverage against an underfunding co-owner.
