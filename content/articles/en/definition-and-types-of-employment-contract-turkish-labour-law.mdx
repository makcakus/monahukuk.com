Every employment relationship in Turkey rests on a single legal foundation: the employment contract as defined in Article 8 of Labour Code No. 4857. Getting the classification of that contract right is not a formality — it determines which protective rules apply, whether the employee is entitled to job security, how severance and notice periods are calculated, and how compensation claims are assessed if the relationship ends in dispute. For foreign investors and HR teams setting up operations in Turkey, understanding the statutory categories set out in Articles 8 through 14 is a necessary first step before any hiring takes place.

## What Counts as an Employment Contract

Article 8 defines the employment contract as an agreement under which one party (the employee) undertakes to perform work in a subordinate capacity, and the other party (the employer) undertakes to pay wages in return. The decisive element is subordination: the employee performs the work under the employer's instructions, within the time and place the employer determines, and inside the employer's own organisational structure. This is what separates an employment contract from a service (eser) or agency-type (vekâlet) contract, where the party performing the work retains organisational independence. Because Turkish courts look at the substance of the working relationship rather than its label, a contract labelled as a "consultancy" or "service" agreement can still be reclassified as an employment contract if subordination is present in practice — a point foreign employers should keep in mind when structuring engagements with individuals who work exclusively for one company.

## Freedom of Form and the Written-Document Obligation

As a rule, Turkish law does not require an employment contract to take any particular form — an oral agreement is legally valid. There are, however, two important exceptions. First, any employment contract with a term of one year or more must be made in writing; to remove any financial disincentive to doing so, such written contracts are exempt from stamp duty and all other duties and fees. Second, where no written contract exists, the employer must still hand the employee a written document within two months, setting out the general and specific working conditions, daily or weekly working hours, the base wage and any wage supplements, the payment period, the term of the contract if applicable, and the rules the parties must observe on termination. This obligation does not apply to fixed-term contracts of one month or less. In practice, failing to provide this document exposes the employer to an administrative fine and tends to work against the employer evidentially in any later dispute, since the employee's account of the agreed terms becomes harder to rebut.

## Fixed-Term and Indefinite-Term Contracts

Article 11 sets out the central distinction in Turkish employment law: contracts are indefinite-term unless a term is agreed. Indefinite-term contracts are the statutory default, and this matters because most of the Labour Code's protective machinery — job security provisions and notice-based termination rules among them — is built around this contract type. A fixed-term contract, by contrast, may only be agreed in writing and only where an objective reason supports it: the work itself is temporary in nature, tied to the completion of a specific task, or dependent on the occurrence of a particular event. A fixed-term contract entered into solely to keep the employee outside the scope of job-security protection, without such an objective justification, does not meet this requirement and risks being disregarded.

The law also restricts back-to-back renewals: successive fixed-term contracts may not be concluded without an essential reason, and if they are, the relationship is treated as indefinite-term from its inception — regardless of what the individual contracts said. Chained fixed-term contracts retain their fixed-term character only where an essential reason for the repeated renewal genuinely exists, such as seasonal work or a role tied to a project of defined duration. This is a frequent point of dispute before Turkish courts: where a position is, by its nature, a permanent one but has been repeatedly papered over with rolling fixed-term contracts, courts have consistently treated the employment relationship as indefinite-term from day one, with corresponding consequences for severance and notice calculations.

## Equal Treatment Between Fixed-Term and Indefinite-Term Employees

Article 12 addresses a risk that naturally follows from the fixed-term/indefinite-term split: employers treating fixed-term staff less favourably simply because their contracts are for a fixed term. The rule is that a fixed-term employee may not be subjected to different treatment than a comparable indefinite-term employee for that reason alone, unless a justified ground exists. Divisible benefits tied to pay must be paid in proportion to the time actually worked, and seniority-based entitlements must, absent a justified reason, be calculated by reference to the comparable indefinite-term employee. The "comparable employee" is, in the first instance, an indefinite-term employee doing the same or a similar job at the same workplace; if none exists there, a comparable employee at another workplace in the same line of business is used as the benchmark. This provision functions as a sector-specific application of the general principle of equal treatment in Turkish labour law.

## Part-Time Employment: Definition and Proportionality

Article 13 introduces a separate axis of classification, based on working time rather than duration of the contract: a contract is part-time where the employee's normal weekly working hours are significantly lower than those of a comparable full-time employee. Since the Labour Code caps normal weekly working time at forty-five hours, Turkish case law has generally treated working hours at or below roughly two-thirds of full-time hours as the threshold for "significantly lower." As with fixed-term staff, a part-time employee may not be treated differently from a comparable full-time employee for that reason alone without a justified ground, and divisible monetary benefits — bonuses and premiums, for example — must be paid pro rata to hours actually worked. Provisions added to Article 13 in 2016 also give a parent, under defined conditions following the birth of a child, the right to request a shift to part-time work; the employer must accommodate such a request, and it may not be treated as valid grounds for dismissal.

## On-Call Work and Remote Work

Article 14 regulates on-call work as a distinct sub-type of part-time employment: the employee is engaged, under a written contract, to perform work only when the employer calls on them to do so. If the parties have not fixed how many hours the employee will work within a given week, month or year, the weekly working time is deemed to be twenty hours by default, and the employee is entitled to be paid for that time whether or not they are actually called in — a rule designed to stop employers from avoiding pay simply by not issuing calls. The employer must give at least four days' notice before the time the employee is expected to work, unless otherwise agreed, and where daily working hours have not been fixed, each call must involve at least four consecutive hours of work. These minimum periods exist precisely to stop on-call arrangements from becoming an unpredictable, fragmented form of employment for the worker.

The same article, through provisions added by Law No. 6715, also regulates remote work: an employment relationship, established in writing, under which the employee performs their duties from home or through technological communication tools outside the workplace, within an organisational structure set up by the employer. Remote employees benefit from the same anti-discrimination protection as part-time staff relative to comparable employees, and the employer's occupational health and safety obligations continue to apply to them despite the change in location. Correctly classifying the contract type at the outset also matters for how it interacts with other contractual arrangements signed at hiring, such as probationary-period clauses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an employment contract have to be in writing to be valid in Turkey?**
No, not as a general rule — an oral employment contract is legally valid. Writing is only mandatory for contracts of one year or longer, and even then only to satisfy the statutory form requirement; where no written contract exists, the employer must still issue a written statement of terms within two months (except for fixed-term contracts of one month or less).

**Can an employer simply choose to hire someone on a fixed-term contract to avoid job-security obligations?**
No. A fixed-term contract is only valid where an objective reason exists — such as the temporary nature of the work, a specific task, or a defined event. A fixed-term contract used only to sidestep job-security protection, without such a reason, is at risk of being treated as indefinite-term.

**What happens if an employer keeps renewing a fixed-term contract year after year?**
Absent an essential reason justifying repeated renewal (such as genuinely seasonal or project-bound work), successive fixed-term contracts are treated as an indefinite-term employment relationship from its original starting date, which also affects how seniority and severance are calculated.

**Can a part-time or on-call employee be paid less favourably than a full-time comparable employee?**
Not simply because of their working-time arrangement. Divisible monetary benefits must be paid in proportion to hours worked, and any difference in treatment beyond that proportional adjustment requires a justified reason — otherwise it runs counter to the equal-treatment rules in Articles 12 and 13.
