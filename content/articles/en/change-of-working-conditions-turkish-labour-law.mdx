Every employment relationship in Turkey rests on a set of terms the parties agreed at the outset — pay, job title, place of work, working hours and similar elements that together form what Turkish labour law calls "working conditions." An employer's managerial prerogative allows minor operational adjustments to how work is carried out, but any change that alters the core substance of what the employee originally undertook is subject to a separate and stricter regime under Article 22 of the Labour Code (Law No. 4857). For employers operating in Turkey — whether restructuring a branch, adjusting a pay scale, or relocating staff — understanding exactly where ordinary managerial discretion ends and Article 22 begins is essential, because getting the procedure wrong can turn a routine business decision into an unlawful termination.

## What Counts as a "Working Condition" and When a Change Is "Substantial"

Turkish law treats as a "working condition" any right or obligation arising from the employment relationship, whether it is written into the contract itself, set out in a supplementary source such as a personnel handbook or internal circular, or has simply become established through consistent workplace practice. Typical examples include the amount and method of wage payment, working hours, job description, title, and place of work.

Not every adjustment to these elements triggers Article 22, however. The law only applies where the change is "substantial" — meaning it disturbs the fundamental balance of obligations the employee relied on when entering the contract. Routine, day-to-day adjustments that do not touch that balance fall outside the article and remain within the employer's ordinary management rights. In practice, the most frequently disputed scenarios are: transferring an employee to a branch far from their residence, demoting them from their title, cutting their pay, or shifting their working hours to their detriment. By contrast, minor reassignments consistent with an employee's existing job description, reasonable rescheduling of break times, or simply exercising a flexibility clause the contract already contains do not amount to a substantial change and are not subject to Article 22's formal requirements. Because this assessment is inherently fact-specific, the same adjustment might be trivial for one employee but substantial for another depending on their family situation, health, or length of service — which is why courts examine the practical impact on the individual employee rather than applying a fixed checklist.

## The Written Notice Requirement and the Six-Working-Day Acceptance Period

Where a change does qualify as substantial, Article 22 imposes a strict formal procedure. First, the employer must communicate the proposed change to the employee in writing; a change proposed informally or verbally has no binding effect from the outset. Second, even a properly written proposal does not take effect merely because the employee stays silent — the employee must accept it in writing within six working days. If that acceptance is not given within the period, the change does not bind the employee and the contract continues on its original terms.

This six-working-day window is treated as a forfeiture period that the parties cannot shorten by agreement. If the employee purports to accept after the period has already lapsed, that late acceptance is treated as a fresh offer directed at the employer, which the employer is then free to accept or decline.

## If the Employee Refuses: The Employer's Options and the Path to Court

When an employee does not accept the proposed change within the statutory period, the employer effectively has two choices. It can withdraw the proposal and continue the contract on the original terms, or it can terminate the contract — but only by explaining in writing that the change itself rests on valid grounds, or that some other valid reason for termination exists, and by observing the statutory notice period. Importantly, this route does not produce a termination "because of Article 22" as such; it results in an ordinary termination subject to the valid-reason review under Article 18. The employee may therefore challenge the dismissal through a reinstatement claim under Articles 17 to 21, and the court will scrutinise whether the change that was proposed was genuinely justified by a valid business reason.

The law does not give the employee who rejects the change an automatic right to resign for just cause. However, if the employer goes ahead and actually implements the altered condition despite the rejection, that amounts to the previous condition simply no longer being applied — which falls within the "non-application of working conditions" ground for the employee's own just-cause termination right under Article 24. An employee who resigns on this basis cannot claim notice pay but is entitled to severance pay.

## Implied Acceptance Through Continued, Unprotested Performance

A common real-world scenario is that the employer implements a change without any written notice, and the employee, without formally objecting, continues working under the new terms for an extended period. Turkish Court of Cassation case law recognises that even without a written acceptance, conduct that unmistakably shows the employee has embraced the change can be treated as falling within Article 22's second paragraph — a change carried out "by mutual agreement." A demoted employee who continues performing the new, lesser role for a long period without objection is a typical example.

The picture is different where the employee expressly states they do not accept the change but continues working under protest, reserving their objection — in that scenario no implied acceptance arises and the change does not bind the employee. This distinction requires a careful, fact-specific inquiry into whether the employee's conduct genuinely reflects their actual will. Because the burden of proving implied acceptance rests on the employer, it is not enough to show mere silence; the employer must point to concrete facts demonstrating that the employee knowingly and willingly embraced the change — continued work alone, without more, is not treated as sufficient.

## Changing Terms by Mutual Agreement and the Ban on Retroactive Effect

Article 22's second paragraph confirms that the parties may always agree, by mutual consent, to change working conditions at any time. Where a genuine agreement exists, the formal written-notice and six-day-acceptance requirements of the first paragraph do not apply, since the parties have already freely reached a new arrangement between themselves. What such an agreement cannot do, however, is take effect retroactively: any change only produces legal effect from the moment it was notified or agreed. This rule exists to prevent an employer from dressing up a later "agreement" as a device to retroactively extinguish wage or other entitlements that had already accrued, and the same principle protects working conditions in situations tied to a change of employer, such as a workplace transfer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can my employer change my job title or duties without my written consent?**
If the change is substantial — meaning it disrupts the core balance of what you agreed to when you were hired — no. Article 22 requires written notice of the proposed change, and it only binds you if you accept it in writing within six working days. Minor adjustments within your existing job description are different and do not require this procedure.

**What happens if I say nothing after receiving a written change proposal?**
Silence is not acceptance under Article 22. If you do not respond in writing within six working days, the proposed change does not bind you and your contract continues on its previous terms — unless your later conduct (continuing to perform the new terms without any objection, over a significant period) is found to amount to an implied agreement.

**Can I be dismissed for refusing a proposed change to my working conditions?**
Yes, but only through a lawful termination process: the employer must state in writing that the change (or some other reason) constitutes valid grounds for termination and must observe the statutory notice period. You may challenge that dismissal through a reinstatement claim, and a court will review whether the underlying reason was genuinely valid.

**If my employer actually imposes the change despite my refusal, do I have to keep working under it?**
No. If the employer implements the changed condition anyway after you rejected it, this is treated as the employer failing to apply your original working conditions, which can give you grounds to terminate your own contract for just cause and claim severance pay, even though notice pay is not available on this basis.
