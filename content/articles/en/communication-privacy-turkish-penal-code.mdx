Foreign nationals and international counsel dealing with Turkish criminal proceedings are often surprised to learn how strictly Turkish law treats the interception, recording or disclosure of private communications. A person who secretly records a phone call almost always offers the same justification: "I did it to protect myself." Under the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), that justification does not, as a rule, make the conduct lawful. Articles 132 and 133 of the TCK sit within the chapter on offences against private life and the confidentiality of private life, and together they protect two different forms of communication: exchanges carried through a medium (calls, messages, letters) under Article 132, and face-to-face conversation between people physically present with each other under Article 133. Which article applies to a given recording — and therefore what penalty range is in play — depends entirely on how the communication took place.

## What the Law Is Protecting

Article 22 of the Turkish Constitution guarantees freedom of communication, and Article 20 guarantees the privacy of private life; Articles 132 and 133 of the TCK are the criminal-law expression of those constitutional guarantees. The interest being protected is not the accuracy of what was said, nor any commercial value the information might have — it is the individual's exclusive authority to decide who they communicate with, when, and about what. Because of this, even a trivial or seemingly unimportant exchange is protected: what the law shields is the closed circuit of the communication itself, not the importance of its content.

## Two Statutes, Two Kinds of Communication

The dividing line between Article 132 and Article 133 depends on the nature of the communication at issue, not on its subject matter. Article 132 covers communication between specific people carried through some medium — telephone calls, letters, email, instant messaging. Article 133 covers spoken conversation where the parties are physically together and the exchange is not intended to be public. This distinction has real practical consequences: whether an audio recording was made over a telephone line or face to face determines which article applies and, with it, a materially different sentencing range.

Whether a conversation counts as "non-public" turns on the intention of the speakers, not on how many people happened to be within earshot. Two people speaking privately in a crowded café, taking care that their words are meant only for each other, are still having a non-public conversation. Conversely, a statement made loudly and deliberately where anyone can overhear it falls outside the article's protection, because the speaker has accepted that it may be heard.

## Article 132: Three Distinct Forms of Conduct

Article 132 does not describe a single offence — it sets out three separate, graduated forms of conduct. Under its first paragraph, a person who violates the confidentiality of communication between others is liable to imprisonment of one to three years; if that violation takes the form of recording the content of the communication, the sentence is increased by half again. Here, the offender is a third party who is not part of the communication, and the offence is complete once the content has been obtained.

The second paragraph addresses unlawful disclosure of the content of communication between others, punishable by imprisonment of two to five years. Disclosure means making the content available to an indeterminate number of people; it is irrelevant whether the person disclosing it obtained the content lawfully in the first place — unlawful disclosure of even lawfully obtained content is still captured.

The third paragraph deals with the situation most commonly seen in practice: a person who was themselves a party to the communication, and who unlawfully makes its content public without the other party's consent, is liable to imprisonment of one to three years; the same penalty applies where the disclosed material is published through the press or broadcast media. What makes this a crime is not that the person knew the content — they were entitled to know it — but that they gave it publicity without consent.

## Article 133: Recording by a Party, and Interception by a Third Person

Article 133's first paragraph addresses a third party — someone outside the conversation — who, without the consent of either speaker, listens to a non-public conversation using a device or records it with a sound-recording device; this carries imprisonment of two to five years. The second paragraph addresses a participant in the conversation who records it using a sound-recording device without the other speakers' consent; here the penalty is markedly lighter, six months to two years' imprisonment or a judicial fine, reflecting the fact that this person was lawfully privy to the content in any event. The third paragraph addresses disclosure: a person who unlawfully discloses material obtained by recording a non-public conversation is liable to imprisonment of two to five years and a judicial fine of up to four thousand day-units, with the same penalty applying if the material is published through the press or broadcast media.

## Intent and the Role of Lawful-Justification Grounds

Both offences require intent; there is no negligence-based version of either crime. The offender must act knowing that the communication was not directed at them, or that the other party had not consented. Turkish law does not require any particular motive: acting to defend oneself, to expose infidelity, or out of simple curiosity does not remove criminal liability. At the same time, the phrase "unlawfully" appears explicitly in Article 132(2), Article 132(3) and Article 133(3), signalling that grounds excluding unlawfulness carry particular weight in this area of law.

## The Narrow Exception for Evidence at Risk of Being Lost

Turkish Court of Cassation practice draws the most important line in this field: a recording made to preserve evidence that is at genuine risk of being permanently lost, and made in order to be submitted to the competent authorities, can be treated as lawful. For the exception to apply, the recording must be a spontaneous reaction to a sudden, unplanned event, there must be no realistic alternative way of obtaining the evidence, and the recording must be handed over to judicial authorities rather than circulated to third parties. A recording made after advance preparation, taken systematically, or made for the purpose of manufacturing evidence, falls outside this exception. On the criminal-procedure side, Article 206(2)(a) of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CMK) requires evidence obtained through unlawful means to be rejected, and Article 217(2) confines a conviction to evidence obtained lawfully.

## Aggravating Factors, the Complaint Requirement and Jurisdiction

Under Article 137 of the TCK, the penalty for these offences is increased by half where they are committed by a public official abusing the authority conferred by their office, or by taking advantage of the facilities afforded by a particular profession or trade. Under Article 139, prosecution of the offences in this chapter — including Articles 132 and 133 — is, as a rule, conditional on a complaint. Under Article 73, the right to file that complaint must be exercised within six months of learning of the act and the offender. Because these offences are subject to a complaint requirement, they also fall within the scope of mediation (uzlaştırma), and given the applicable sentencing ranges, jurisdiction lies with the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi).

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence

An attempt is possible for the violation offence to the extent the acts of execution can be broken down into stages — for example, where a recording device has been planted but no content has yet been captured. Disclosure offences, by contrast, are complete only once the content actually reaches third parties. Both offences allow for the full range of participation, including instigation and aiding. As for concurrence, where multiple violations against the same victim over time form part of a single criminal plan, the continuing-offence provision of Article 43 applies. Because a violation carried out through recording is specifically addressed in the second sentence of Article 132(1), the same conduct is not separately charged under the general provision on unlawfully recording personal data.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it ever lawful to record a phone call without the other person's knowledge?**
As a rule, no. Recording is treated as an aggravated form of violating communication privacy under Article 132(1). It can be excused only under the narrow exception for evidence that would otherwise be irretrievably lost — a spontaneous, unplanned recording made because no other evidence could be obtained, and handed over to judicial authorities rather than shared with others.

**Does it matter whether the recording was made over the phone or in person?**
Yes, and the distinction determines which statute applies. Communication carried through a medium — a telephone call, message or email — falls under Article 132. A face-to-face conversation between people physically present falls under Article 133, which carries its own, separate penalty structure and treats a participant who records the conversation more leniently than a third party who intercepts it.

**Can someone be prosecuted for playing a recording made by someone else?**
It depends on which paragraph is engaged. Article 132 and Article 133 attach disclosure liability to content obtained through the offences they define; presenting a recording made by an uninvolved third party as evidence does not automatically fall within either article, though the circumstances in which the recording was requested or obtained may support liability under a different provision, such as the offence of violating the privacy of private life.

**Is a good motive, such as proving infidelity, a defence?**
No. Turkish law does not require a specific bad motive for these offences, and a good or sympathetic reason for recording or disclosing — self-defence, curiosity, or proving misconduct — does not remove criminal liability on its own. Only a recognised ground of lawful justification, such as the narrow lost-evidence exception or, in disclosure cases, the exercise of press freedom and the right to inform the public under strict conditions, can do that.
