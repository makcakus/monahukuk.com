Having closed out general average, the series turns to an entirely different kind of maritime institution: collision (*çatma*), governed by TTK Art. 1286-1297. Where general average is a solidarity regime — parties who survive a common peril together share the cost of that survival — collision is a fault-based tort regime: two ships collide, and liability follows fault. This article covers the scope of the collision chapter (Art. 1286) and the no-fault collision rule (Art. 1287).

## What Counts as a Collision

Art. 1286(1) defines "collision" as the striking of two or more ships, and applies the chapter's rules to resulting damage to the ships, to people on board, or to cargo. Only a genuine ship-to-ship collision counts — a single ship striking a fixed structure like a dock or pier falls outside the chapter and is governed by general tort law instead, as Turkish courts have confirmed directly.

Art. 1286(2) then extends the regime significantly: where a ship causes damage to another ship, or to people or cargo aboard it, through a maneuver (or failure to maneuver) or a breach of navigation rules — without actual physical contact — the collision rules still apply. This closes an obvious gap: a vessel that forces another to take evasive action, causing it to run aground or strike a third object, should not escape liability merely because contact never occurred.

## No-Fault Collision

Art. 1287(1) sets out the flip side: where a collision results from an unforeseeable event, force majeure, or a cause that cannot be determined, each party bears its own loss — no one can claim against anyone else. This reflects a classic tort principle: without fault (or an inability to prove it), there is no basis for a claim. Notably, where the cause simply cannot be established, the burden falls on the party alleging fault — failure to prove it means the loss stays where it fell, rather than being shifted or shared. Art. 1287(2) confirms the same rule applies even if one or both vessels were at anchor at the time.

## Practical Takeaway

For shipowners and insurers, the key point is that Art. 1286 has real teeth as a scope limitation — collisions with fixed shoreside structures are excluded entirely — while Art. 1287's no-fault rule places the practical burden of proving fault squarely on the party seeking compensation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do the collision provisions apply even if the ships did not actually strike each other?

Yes. Under Article 1286/2 TTK the collision provisions also apply where a ship causes loss to another ship, or to persons or goods on board it, <b>without a collision</b>, by <b>performing or failing to perform a manoeuvre or by not complying with the rules of navigation</b>. Damaging a boat by wash, or a wrong manoeuvre leading the other ship to take avoiding action and run aground, fall within this.

### Who bears the loss in a collision without fault?

Under Article 1287/1 TTK, where the collision is due to <b>an unforeseen event or force majeure</b>, or <b>its cause cannot be established</b>, the loss suffered by the colliding ships, by the persons on board or by the goods <b>is borne by the person who suffered it</b>. The loss therefore lies where it falls unless fault is proved; no one may claim compensation from anyone else. This is why proof of fault is decisive in collision actions.

### Does striking a ship at anchor create a presumption of fault?

No. Under Article 1287/2 TTK, Article 1287/1 applies also where the unforeseen event, force majeure or inability to establish the cause occurs while all or one of the ships <b>was at anchor at the time of the accident</b>. This rule must be read together with Article 1294, under which no presumption is taken into account in establishing fault in a collision: being at anchor does not mean the other side was at fault.
