Many foreign employees working in Turkey are unaware that trade union membership is open to them on largely the same basis as for Turkish workers. The right to join a union is not reserved for nationals — it extends to any person working legally in the country under a valid work permit. Understanding where the boundaries lie is important before you take any steps.

## Who Can Join a Trade Union in Turkey?

Article 17 of the Trade Unions and Collective Bargaining Agreements Law (Sendikalar ve Toplu İş Sözleşmesi Kanunu — Law No. 6356) sets out the eligibility rule: any person who has reached the age of fifteen and qualifies as an *işçi* (worker) under the law may join a trade union. The criterion is legal employment status, not nationality. A foreign national holding a valid work permit and employed by a registered employer satisfies this test.

The membership process is electronic. Joining is done through the e-Government portal (e-Devlet), and the union has thirty days to formally reject the application. If no rejection is issued within that period, the application is deemed accepted by operation of law.

Unions in Turkey are organised by sector — metalworking, construction, healthcare, hospitality, and so on. You approach the relevant sector union's branch, complete the electronic application, and pay the applicable dues. Your nationality creates no additional barrier at the membership stage.

## Collective Bargaining Agreements and Foreign Employees

One of the most tangible benefits of a unionised workplace is the collective bargaining agreement (*toplu iş sözleşmesi*). These agreements set standards above the statutory baseline — better wages, longer notice periods, additional annual leave, or enhanced severance terms. Crucially, a valid collective bargaining agreement applies to all employees at the covered workplace, whether or not they are personally union members.

If your workplace is covered by such an agreement, you benefit from its terms automatically. Your employer cannot apply a lower standard simply because you are a foreign national or have not personally joined the union. This also means that even in workplaces where foreign workers are a minority, they share in whatever improvements the union has negotiated. ## Protection Against Anti-Union Dismissal

Article 25 of Law No. 6356 provides comprehensive protection for workers against discrimination based on union membership. The key rules are:

- Hiring cannot be made conditional on joining — or not joining — any specific union.
- An employer cannot treat union members and non-members differently in terms of working conditions or termination.
- An employee cannot be dismissed or otherwise penalised for joining a union, leaving a union, or taking part in lawful union activities during or outside working hours.

If an employer dismisses a worker for union-related reasons, the employer faces a mandatory *sendikal tazminat* (anti-union compensation) of **no less than one year's wage**. Importantly, this compensation is payable whether or not the employer reinstates the worker — it is not a substitute for the regular severance and notice pay the worker is also entitled to.

On the question of who must prove what: when the reason for dismissal is disputed, the burden of proving the stated reason falls on the employer. The worker, however, must show that the dismissal was connected to union activity if they want to invoke Article 25.

## Practical Steps if You Believe You Were Dismissed for Union Activity

Proving the link between a dismissal and union membership is rarely straightforward — employers seldom document the real reason. If you suspect that your termination was connected to your union activity, acting promptly is critical. Turkish labour law imposes strict statutory deadlines for bringing a wrongful dismissal claim, and missing them forfeits your right to challenge the decision entirely.

Document everything: any communications, warnings, or changes in treatment that followed your union membership or activity. Keep copies of payslips, your employment contract, and any written correspondence. A labour lawyer can advise you on the strength of your case and the right procedural route before those deadlines expire.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: I have a work permit. Do I need anything else to join a union?
A valid work permit and active employment with a registered employer are the only requirements under Article 17 of Law No. 6356. Membership is completed through e-Devlet; there is no additional approval process for foreign nationals.

### Q: Does my union membership end if I change employers?
Union membership is personal, not tied to a specific employer. However, if you move to a different sector, you may need to transfer to the relevant sector union. Check with the union directly when changing jobs.

### Q: Can I benefit from a collective bargaining agreement without joining the union?
Yes. If your workplace is covered by a collective bargaining agreement, its terms apply to you regardless of your personal membership status. Joining the union additionally gives you access to legal advice, representation, and a voice in union activities — but the core contractual protections apply either way.

### Q: My employer threatened to fire me if I join a union. What are my rights?
Under Article 25 of Law No. 6356, conditioning employment on union membership or non-membership is unlawful. If you are dismissed for joining a union, you are entitled to sendikal tazminat of at least one year's wage in addition to any other severance entitlements. Document the threat, preserve all communications, and seek legal advice without delay.
