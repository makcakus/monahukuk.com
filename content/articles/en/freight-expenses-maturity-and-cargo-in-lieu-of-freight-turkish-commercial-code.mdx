TTK Art. 1196-1198 close out the freight chapter by defining what a carrier cannot charge beyond freight, when freight becomes due, and what kind of debt freight actually is.

## No Hidden Surcharges

Art. 1196/1 bars the carrier from demanding anything beyond agreed freight under any label — "earnest money, premium, tip, or similar." The open-ended phrasing blocks any informal surcharge the carrier might invent, not just the three named examples. Agreed freight is the carrier's sole monetary entitlement for that carriage unless the contract separately and explicitly provides for something more.

## Ordinary Shipping Costs Fall on the Carrier by Default

Absent a contrary agreement, ordinary and extraordinary shipping costs — pilotage, port dues, lighthouse fees, towage, quarantine, ice-breaking, and similar — fall on the carrier alone, even where the freight contract's own terms wouldn't otherwise require it. These are treated as costs inherent to running a ship, already priced into freight, and cannot be separately billed to the shipper or consignee. The exception: general average expenses and costs of protecting, safeguarding, or salvaging the cargo are excluded from this default, since those benefit cargo interests specifically or get shared across all interested parties under the separate general average regime.

## When Freight Becomes Due

Freight matures at whichever comes first: the moment delivery is requested, or, in any event, the end of discharge time. Turkish courts apply this consistently — freight maturity is unaffected by cargo damage claims, which must be pursued as a separate action; a shipper cannot avoid paying freight by pointing to spoiled or damaged cargo. Where the voyage never completes at all (e.g., the ship must turn back due to a breakdown), courts look to the contract's own maturity terms instead, since neither of Art. 1197's triggers actually occurred.

## Freight Cannot Be Paid in Cargo

A carrier can never be forced to accept cargo — damaged or not — as a substitute for cash freight. This confirms freight is a genuine monetary debt with no implicit in-kind substitute, and mirrors Art. 1196's anti-surcharge rule from the other direction: neither party can unilaterally alter freight from its agreed, purely monetary form.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### May the carrier claim additional charges beyond freight?

No. Under TTK Article 1196/1 <b>the carrier may make no further claim beyond freight under such headings as earnest money, premium, gratuity or the like.</b> The rule ensures that freight is a single, transparent consideration. Demurrage, despatch money and the expenses listed in Article 1201 are, however, separate items from freight and fall outside this prohibition.

### When does freight fall due?

Under TTK Article 1197/1 <b>freight falls due at the moment delivery of the goods is requested and, in any event, at the end of the discharging period.</b> Two criteria operate together: if a request for delivery is made earlier, that moment governs; if not, the end of the discharging period does. The moment of maturity matters in practice because it also determines when the carrier may exercise the lien under Article 1201.

### Can the charterer abandon the goods in lieu of freight?

No. Under TTK Article 1198/1 <b>the carrier cannot be compelled to accept the goods in lieu of freight, whether or not they are spoiled or damaged.</b> Freight is a <b>monetary debt</b> and cannot be performed by abandoning the goods. The rule is unchanged even if the value of the goods has fallen below the freight; the charterer must pay the freight in money.
