When cargo — not the ship — is lost before a voyage starts, the outcome under TTK Art. 1214-1215 turns entirely on one distinction: was the cargo individually identified in the contract, or only described generically by type and quantity?

## Specifically Identified Cargo: Total Loss Ends the Contract, Partial Loss Gives Options

Under Art. 1214, if cargo specifically identified in the contract (a particular consignment, a specific container) is entirely lost to an unforeseen event, the freight contract lapses with no damages owed by either side, aside from claims already accrued. A partial loss is different: the shipper gets a real choice — pay half the agreed freight and cancel, or (without making the carrier worse off) load substitute cargo instead. Critically, doing nothing before the ship leaves port is not a safe default: failing to choose means the shipper owes full freight. If substitute cargo is chosen, the shipper bears the extra loading costs and any resulting delay damages.

## Generic Cargo: Loss of a Source Doesn't Kill the Contract

Art. 1215 reaches the opposite result for cargo described only by type and kind (e.g., "500 tons of wheat"): even total loss before delivery for loading does not end the contract, because the shipper can in principle source equivalent replacement goods — a rule mirroring the classic civil-law idea that generic goods "cannot perish." Once such cargo is actually delivered for loading, though, it becomes specifically identified and falls under Art. 1214's regime from that point forward. A narrow grace window softens the transition: if generic cargo already loaded (or handed to the captain) is destroyed before loading time expires, the shipper can avoid termination by promptly offering and beginning delivery of substitute goods — at its own cost, on a tight timeline, and bearing any resulting delay damages to the carrier.

## Why the Distinction Matters

For exporters and freight buyers, this means the contract's own drafting — whether cargo is described as a specific, identified consignment or as a fungible quantity of goods — materially changes what happens if something goes wrong before loading. Reported Turkish case law specific to this exact loss-before-loading scenario is sparse, suggesting these disputes are usually resolved contractually rather than litigated to appeal.
