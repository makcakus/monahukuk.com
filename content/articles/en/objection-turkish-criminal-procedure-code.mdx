Not every decision issued in a Turkish criminal case is a final judgment on guilt or innocence. Detention orders, judicial supervision measures, search authorizations, the return of an indictment, or a decision to defer the announcement of a verdict are all interim rulings issued while a case is still moving forward, and a person affected by one of them often cannot afford to wait until the case concludes to challenge it. Turkish criminal procedure addresses this need through a distinct remedy called "itiraz," regulated in Articles 267 through 271 of the Criminal Procedure Code (CMK). Unlike an appeal ("istinaf") or a further appeal to the Court of Cassation, an objection is not aimed at a final verdict — it targets specific interim decisions issued by a single judge, or, in limited cases set out expressly by law, by a court panel. For foreign nationals and their counsel navigating Turkish criminal proceedings, understanding when this remedy is available, how quickly it must be used, and what it can and cannot achieve is often more urgent than understanding the appeal process itself.

## Which Decisions Can Be Challenged Through an Objection

Article 267 establishes the basic architecture of the remedy: as a rule, an objection may be filed against decisions issued by an individual judge, while decisions issued by a court panel may only be challenged this way in the specific instances the law names. This structure reflects the underlying logic of the objection procedure — it is designed for rulings that steer the course of a case rather than decisions that resolve it. The Criminal Procedure Code expressly opens the objection route for a substantial list of interim rulings, including decisions on lack of subject-matter jurisdiction (Art. 5), lack of territorial jurisdiction (Art. 18), rejection of a request to disqualify a judge (Art. 28), refusal of a request to restore a missed procedural deadline (Art. 42), disciplinary detention of a witness (Art. 60), placement under observation (Art. 74), physical examination orders (Art. 75), pre-trial detention (Art. 101), continuation of detention or release (Art. 104), judicial supervision measures (Art. 111), return of an indictment to the prosecutor (Art. 174), and a decision to suspend proceedings (Art. 223). Deferral of the announcement of the verdict is **no longer among them**: Article 231(12), as amended by Law No. 7499 of 2 March 2024 and Law No. 7589 of 16 July 2026, now provides for a first-instance appeal (“istinaf”) against a HAGB order, subject to Article 272(3). Certain other statutes, such as the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law and the Misdemeanours Law, also create their own occasions for objecting to court decisions. What the objection route does not reach is the final judgment itself: a verdict on guilt, acquittal, or sentence can only be challenged through an appeal or a further appeal to the Court of Cassation. This is the fundamental dividing line between the two remedies — the objection procedure gives quick relief against decisions taken along the way, while the appeal and cassation routes only become available once a verdict has actually been rendered. As a practical matter, the two paths never compete for the same decision in the same case: each is directed at a different type of ruling.

## Deadlines, Filing and Which Authority Reviews the Objection

Article 268 sets out the filing mechanics. Unless another provision states otherwise, an objection must be lodged within two weeks of the date the person concerned learned of the decision, either by a written petition or by an oral statement recorded by the court clerk and confirmed by the presiding judge, submitted to the same authority that issued the challenged decision (a detained person may also make this statement to the director of the penal institution). That authority gets the first opportunity to reconsider: if it agrees the objection is justified, it can simply correct its own decision. If not, it must forward the file within three days to whichever body is designated by law to examine objections against decisions of that kind. Article 268 lays out this chain of reviewing authorities in detail — objections against a peace criminal judgeship generally go to the next-numbered peace criminal judgeship in the same location (or to the peace criminal judgeship attached to the relevant assize court where there is only one); objections against a peace criminal judgeship's detention or judicial-supervision decisions go to the local criminal court of first instance; objections against that criminal court's own decisions go to the assize court in its jurisdiction; and objections against decisions issued by a rapporteur judge or by regional appellate or Court of Cassation criminal chambers acting as a first-instance court follow their own designated review path within that same court structure. The rationale behind this layered scheme is functional rather than bureaucratic: because many of the decisions eligible for objection — detention and judicial supervision above all — bear directly on personal liberty, the law wants a review mechanism that moves fast. Giving the original decision-maker a chance to self-correct avoids sending files upward unnecessarily, while the three-day forwarding deadline keeps the process moving when escalation is required. Article 260 identifies who may exercise this right: the public prosecutor, the suspect or accused, anyone recognized as an intervening party, and even a person harmed by the offence whose request to intervene has not yet been decided or has been rejected.

## No Hearing, as a Rule — The Key Difference From an Appeal

The feature that most sharply separates the objection procedure from an appeal or cassation is set out in Article 271: except where the law specifically requires otherwise, an objection is decided without a hearing, based on the case file. The reviewing authority may choose to hear the public prosecutor and then defence counsel or legal representative if it considers this necessary, but that exchange does not amount to a hearing in the procedural sense — whether to request written submissions, order further investigation, or conduct an inquiry of its own is left to the reviewing authority's discretion. The law also requires the decision to be issued as promptly as possible, reinforcing the emphasis on speed. Decisions issued on objection are, as a rule, final and cannot be challenged further; the one exception is a detention order issued for the first time by the reviewing authority itself, which can in turn be objected to — otherwise a person could be deprived of liberty by a ruling with no further avenue of review at all, so this exception exists specifically to preserve access to a remedy. This paper-based procedure is simultaneously the strength and the limitation of the objection route: it allows urgent matters such as detention to be resolved within days, but the absence of live testimony or face-to-face argument makes it less suited to disputes turning on complicated factual questions than a full appeal would be. Lawmakers have offset this by giving the reviewing authority the power to gather evidence and conduct its own inquiries — requesting relevant documents, taking written statements, and the like — when it considers this necessary, so an objection review is not confined to a passive read of the existing file.

## How Far the Reviewing Authority's Scrutiny Extends

For years, Turkish courts debated exactly how far a reviewing authority's power extends when examining an objection. The earlier position of the Court of Cassation, particularly in cases involving deferral of the announcement of a verdict, was that review had to stay narrowly formal — limited to checking whether the objective and subjective conditions listed in the statute were satisfied. That reading drew sustained criticism for undercutting the right to an effective remedy, and in 2013 the General Assembly of Criminal Chambers of the Court of Cassation reversed course, holding that a reviewing authority must examine both the factual and the legal dimensions of a case — including, where necessary, whether the offence was actually established and correctly characterized — rather than confining itself to a checklist review. That broader approach has held steady in the years since and has effectively become the accepted reading of the remedy. In practice, this means the reviewing authority can function almost like a first-instance body: hearing the parties where appropriate, ordering further investigation to fill gaps, and reaching its own view on whether the offence occurred and how it should be classified. That power has a limit, however. Under Article 271(2), if the reviewing authority finds the objection justified, it is only empowered to rule on "the subject matter of the objection" — the authority to issue the final substantive ruling that replaces the decision it has set aside generally remains with the body that issued the original decision. The distinction matters: the reviewing authority's job is to supervise the decision under challenge, not to take over and conclude the underlying case itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can an objection be filed against a final verdict of guilt or acquittal?**
No. The objection procedure under Articles 267-271 CMK applies to interim decisions issued during a case — such as detention, judicial supervision, or a suspension order — not to the final judgment. Note that deferral of a verdict's announcement (HAGB) is now challenged by first-instance appeal under Article 231(12), not by objection. A verdict can only be challenged through an appeal or a further appeal to the Court of Cassation.

**How much time does a person have to file an objection, and where is it filed?**
An objection must be filed within two weeks of learning of the decision, either as a written petition or as an oral statement recorded by the clerk, submitted to the same authority that issued the challenged decision. A detained person may instead make this statement to the director of the penal institution.

**Does the reviewing authority hold a hearing before deciding an objection?**
Generally not. Under Article 271, an objection is decided on the file without a hearing, though the reviewing authority may choose to hear the public prosecutor and then defence counsel if it considers this useful, and it may order further investigation on its own initiative.

**Is a decision issued on an objection final, or can it be challenged again?**
As a rule it is final. The one exception is a detention order issued for the first time by the reviewing authority itself — that particular decision can be objected to again, precisely so that a person is never left without any avenue to challenge a decision restricting their liberty.
