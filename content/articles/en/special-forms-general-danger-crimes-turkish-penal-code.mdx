The Turkish Penal Code's chapter on offenses that endanger public safety contains a distinct family of provisions — Articles 172 through 178 — that differ from the chapter's better-known offenses, such as arson or endangering traffic safety, in one important respect: liability is not triggered by a random dangerous act, but by the breach of a special duty that the offender already carries in a particular field of activity. A person handling radioactive material or nuclear energy, a person manufacturing or storing hazardous substances without authorization, a caregiver responsible for a mentally ill individual, a person running a construction or demolition site, a person controlling an animal in their custody, and a person carrying out work in a public place — each of them is under an obligation not to expose the public to risk within their own sphere of activity. For foreign businesses and counsel navigating Turkish criminal exposure, understanding how these seven offenses are structured, and where the thresholds of liability actually sit, is essential background before assessing risk in regulated or supervisory activities in Turkey.

## A Common Thread: Danger Arising From Breach of a Special Duty

What unites Articles 172 to 178 is not a shared danger source but a shared mechanism: each offense penalizes the violation of a duty tied to a specific role — handling nuclear or radioactive material, operating without the required permit, supervising a person or an animal, running a construction site, or securing a public space. Beyond that common mechanism, the provisions are not uniform in how they define criminal exposure. Some paragraphs require that a concrete danger actually materialize; others require a serious consequence, such as an explosion; and at least one provision, Article 172(1), requires neither a concrete danger nor any resulting harm, treating an act capable of achieving the offender's purpose as sufficient on its own. Article 174 follows yet another technique altogether, penalizing the breach of an administrative permit requirement without any inquiry into concrete danger or consequence.

## The Value Being Protected

All seven provisions protect public safety in a general sense, but each one gives that abstract interest a different concrete shape. Article 172 and Article 173 address nuclear and radioactive risks; Article 174 addresses the risk created by unauthorized manufacture, transport or storage of hazardous or explosive substances; Article 175 addresses the risk posed by an individual who has lost control of their own conduct due to mental illness; Article 176 addresses the physical risks of construction and demolition work; Article 177 addresses the danger created by releasing or losing control of a supervised animal; and Article 178 addresses the danger created by excavations, pits or objects left in places open to public passage. It is worth noting that the drafting technique is not identical across the group: Articles 175 and 177 do not treat the breach of the duty as sufficient by itself — they additionally require that the conduct be capable of endangering the life, health or (in Article 175 only) property of others. Articles 176 and 178, by contrast, contain no such concrete-danger requirement in their text, adopting instead the narrower technique described below, under which breach of the duty alone completes the offense.

## The Radiation and Nuclear Energy Cluster (Articles 172-174)

Article 172 alone contains four gradations of severity within a single provision. Its first paragraph punishes exposing another person to radiation with the specific purpose of damaging that person's health, where the act is capable of achieving that purpose, with imprisonment of three to fifteen years; actual harm to health is not required, and because the offense turns on a specific intent combined with a merely capable act, whether the offender's purpose is ultimately achieved has no bearing on liability. The second paragraph treats the same conduct as an aggravated offense when directed at an indeterminate number of people, raising the minimum sentence to five years. The third paragraph, which does not require the specific purpose found in the first paragraph, punishes emitting radiation or interfering with nuclear fission in a manner capable of causing serious harm to another's life, health or property with imprisonment of two to five years — a genuine concrete-danger offense. The fourth paragraph addresses the same outcome arising from a breach of the duty of care expected during the operation of a laboratory or facility, i.e., from negligence, and reduces the penalty to six months to three years. Article 173 punishes releasing atomic energy in a way that causes an explosion and thereby seriously endangers another's life, health or property, with a minimum sentence of five years; because it requires that the explosion actually occur and that danger actually result, it is — unlike Article 172(1) — a genuine result-based offense. Its second paragraph separately punishes the negligent version of the same conduct with imprisonment of two to five years, echoing the intent/negligence split already found in Article 172. Article 174 targets a different risk category altogether — permit-dependent hazardous substances: a person who manufactures, imports, exports, transports, stores, sells, purchases or processes explosive, corrosive, caustic, poisonous, nuclear, radioactive, chemical or biological substances without the required authorization from the competent authorities faces imprisonment of four to eight years plus a judicial fine of up to five thousand day-units; the same penalty applies to equivalent conduct involving equipment and materials intended for manufacturing, processing or using such substances. Where the act is committed within the activity of a criminal organization, the sentence is increased by half; conversely, purchasing, accepting or possessing an insignificant type or quantity of explosive material is subject to a separate, reduced sentencing regime of up to one year. Unlike Articles 172 and 173, Article 174 requires neither concrete danger nor any resulting harm — the offense is complete the moment the unauthorized activity itself takes place, making it a pure administrative-prohibition offense.

## Breach of a Supervisory Duty: Mentally Ill Individuals and Animals (Articles 175 and 177)

Articles 175 and 177 are structurally parallel. Both punish a person who neglects the duty of control they actually exercise over something in their care — a mentally ill individual or an animal — where that neglect is carried out in a manner capable of endangering the life, health or (in Article 175 only) property of others. Both are offenses that only a person who actually carries the relevant care or supervisory duty can commit; a stranger with no such role cannot be held liable as a principal. The penalty under each provision is up to six months' imprisonment or a judicial fine. What is debated in Turkish legal scholarship is the mental element these offenses require: because the conduct is itself framed as "neglecting" a duty, it can be committed knowingly, yet in its outward appearance it closely resembles a negligence offense. For that reason, some commentators classify these as offenses of their own peculiar kind — a form of built-in negligence — and argue that the Penal Code's general negligence framework under Article 22 does not additionally apply to them.

## Activity-Based Safety Duties: Construction/Demolition and Warning Signs (Articles 176 and 178)

Article 176 punishes failing to take the safety measures required to protect human life or physical integrity during construction or demolition work, with imprisonment of three months to one year or a judicial fine. Article 178 punishes failing to place the warning signs or barriers needed to prevent danger arising from work carried out, or objects left, in places open to public passage — or removing or relocating signs or barriers that have already been placed — with imprisonment of two to six months or a judicial fine. Neither provision contains language requiring a concrete danger in its text; both adopt a drafting technique under which the breach of the duty is sufficient by itself. In this respect they sit closer to an abstract-danger structure than Articles 175 and 177, and they operate as normative (blanket) provisions that draw their content from administrative and technical safety regulations: the prosecution does not need to separately prove that a concrete danger actually resulted, only that the applicable safety duty was breached.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence

Because Article 173(1) is a genuine result-based offense, attempt is generally possible for it. Whether attempt is possible for Article 172(1) and (2) is debated in scholarship, since the legislature has already fixed the completion point of the offense at the moment the act becomes capable of achieving its purpose, leaving little room between attempt and completion. For the negligence-based paragraphs (Article 172(4) and Article 173(2)), and for Articles 175 through 178, which are built around neglect rather than an intentional act, attempt is excluded by the general rule on attempt in Article 35 of the Penal Code. As for participation, because Articles 175 and 177 can only be committed by a person who carries the relevant duty, only such a person can be held liable as principal, while inciting or assisting the offense remains possible for anyone under the ordinary rules on participation. On concurrence, where the danger created under any of these provisions escalates into an actual death or injury, the prevailing view is that the offender must additionally answer for intentional or negligent homicide or injury, given that a different legal interest is at stake; a minority view holds instead that the danger offense is absorbed into the resulting harm offense. None of these offenses require a complaint from the victim — because they concern public order, they are investigated and prosecuted ex officio. Jurisdiction generally lies with the criminal court of first instance, though paragraphs carrying sentences above ten years, such as Article 172(1)-(2) and Article 173(1), can fall within the jurisdiction of the heavy penal court.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Article 172 require that the victim's health actually be damaged?**
No, not under its first paragraph. The offense is complete once the offender exposes another person to radiation with the purpose of damaging their health and in a manner capable of achieving that purpose — whether the purpose is actually realized does not affect liability.

**Is a company operating a facility automatically liable if radiation is released accidentally?**
Liability under Article 172(4) requires a breach of the duty of care expected in operating a laboratory or facility, and it requires that the resulting radiation release be capable of causing serious harm to another's life, health or property. A release that occurs despite adequate care, or one incapable of causing serious harm, does not meet the threshold.

**Can a business be prosecuted under Article 174 even if no accident or injury occurs?**
Yes. Article 174 penalizes unauthorized manufacture, import, export, transport, storage, sale, purchase or processing of hazardous substances as a stand-alone permit violation. Neither a concrete danger nor any resulting harm needs to be shown — the absence of the required authorization is itself sufficient.

**If a supervised animal injures someone, does that always mean the caretaker is liable under Article 177?**
Not automatically, but Turkish courts have treated failing to adequately restrain an animal that then escapes and causes harm as satisfying Article 177, and this can apply in parallel with a separate negligent-injury charge if the victim also pursues that route. The two offenses protect different interests and can both apply to the same incident.
