TTK Art. 1189 is the good-faith safety valve on the otherwise unforgiving one-year preclusive period — a short provision that matters most in how cargo claim negotiations should be handled before a lawsuit is even filed.

## The Rule: Stalling Forfeits the Defense

If the party a claim is addressed to stalls the claimant in a way that causes it to miss the filing deadline, that party can no longer invoke the expired preclusive period as a defense. This is a specific, self-contained application of the general good-faith principle (TMK Art. 2) — and applies directly in cargo disputes without needing to invoke the general civil code provision separately. Conduct that can qualify as stalling includes evasive or indefinite responses to a claim, repeated document requests that drag out talks, creating the impression a claim will be honored so the claimant sees no need to sue, or settlement negotiations kept open without genuine intent to resolve them.

## Practical Stakes for Both Sides

For claimants, this means negotiating in good faith with a carrier doesn't automatically waive the filing-deadline protection — if the carrier's conduct during those talks later qualifies as stalling, it forfeits the defense. But this is not a guarantee: the claimant must actually prove the causal link between the carrier's conduct and the missed deadline: a bare claim that "negotiations happened" is not enough. For carriers, the safer course is to reserve rights explicitly in claims-handling correspondence, or clearly communicate that a claim is rejected or disputed — running lengthy negotiations without ever reserving the time-bar defense carries real litigation risk.

## The Clock Restarts From Discovery

Once stalling is established, the filing period restarts from when the claimant becomes aware of the situation — either that they were being stalled, or that the counterparty never actually intended to satisfy the claim. Because this date is rarely fixed by a single document, disputes under this article tend to turn heavily on the parties' correspondence during negotiations, making it important for claimants to keep organized records of that exchange.

## What the Case Law Shows

Reported decisions directly applying Art. 1189 are scarce — largely because stalling is hard to prove — but the one line of cases available shows courts holding claimants to a strict evidentiary standard, rejecting the defense where correspondence didn't support it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### May the carrier always raise the defence of the preclusive period?

No. Under TTK Article 1189/1 <b>where the addressee of a claim for damages puts off the injured party in a way that causes them to miss the period for bringing an action, that party may not rely on the objection that the preclusive period has expired</b>. This is a concrete application of the rule of good faith (Article 2 of the Turkish Civil Code) to preclusive periods: a person who has delayed the other party by their own conduct may not profit from that delay.

### Does the period start again where there has been such delaying conduct?

Yes. Under TTK Article 1189/2, in that case <b>the period for bringing an action runs from the date the injured party learns of the situation</b>. The one-year period in Article 1188 is therefore recalculated not from the date of delivery but from the date on which the injured party realised they were being put off. This gives them a reasonable period in which they can in fact bring an action.

### Who proves that they were put off?

The burden of proof lies on the <b>injured party</b>, who seeks to defeat the objection based on the period. In practice this is established through correspondence, e-mails, statements containing a promise of payment, notices that a surveyor's examination is still under way, or records of settlement talks. For that reason, keeping all correspondence with the other side is of practical importance in maritime carriage disputes.
