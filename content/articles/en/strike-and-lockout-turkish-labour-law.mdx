Strikes and lockouts are the last-resort pressure tools available to employees and employers when a collective bargaining dispute cannot be resolved through mediation. Turkish law treats the right to strike as a constitutional guarantee under Article 54 of the Constitution, but it is Law No. 6356 on Trade Unions and Collective Labour Agreements (STISK) that spells out, in considerable detail, when a work stoppage or a mass exclusion of employees is lawful and what happens when it is not. For employers operating in Turkey — particularly foreign investors unfamiliar with the mechanics of Turkish collective labour relations — understanding this framework matters because the line between a lawful strike and an unlawful one determines whether the employer can terminate employment contracts for just cause or, conversely, whether the employer itself faces liability toward the workforce.

## What Counts as a Strike or a Lockout

Article 58 of Law No. 6356 defines a strike as employees collectively ceasing to work — either by mutual agreement among themselves or by following a decision taken by a labour organisation — with the purpose of stopping activity at the workplace entirely or disrupting it significantly. The mirror concept, set out in Article 59, is the lockout: a collective exclusion of employees from work, decided and carried out by the employer (or the employer's representative), in a way that brings the workplace's activity to a complete halt.

Both definitions share a structural logic. Each article first states the general definition, then identifies the subset of that action that qualifies as "lawful", and finally clarifies that anything falling short of the lawful conditions is treated as unlawful. Two elements sit at the centre of both definitions: the collective character of the action, and its purpose. A strike is not an individual employee's refusal to work — it is a coordinated cessation of labour by the workforce as a group. A lockout, similarly, is not an isolated dismissal but a collective exclusion carried out with the same organisational logic in mind.

## When a Strike or Lockout Is Lawful

A strike qualifies as lawful only when three conditions are met together: the dispute must have arisen during the negotiation of a collective labour agreement; its purpose must be to protect or improve the economic and social position and working conditions of employees; and the statutory procedure must have been followed in full — meaning the collective bargaining and authorisation process concluded without a result, mediation failed to resolve the dispute, a strike decision was properly taken and announced, and, where required, a strike ballot was held. If any one of these elements is missing, the action is unlawful. Turkish legal doctrine and practice extend this unlawful classification to strikes with a political purpose, general strikes, solidarity strikes, workplace occupations, and deliberate work slowdowns aimed at reducing output — none of these fit within the narrow statutory definition of a lawful strike.

A lockout follows a different logic: it can only become lawful as a reaction to an already-existing lawful strike decision taken by the employees' union. In other words, a lockout is a dependent tool — its lawfulness is conditioned on the union having properly resolved to strike in the first place, rather than on an independent set of criteria the employer must satisfy on its own initiative.

## Workplaces Where Strikes and Lockouts Are Absolutely Prohibited

Article 62 of Law No. 6356 places certain workplaces entirely outside the reach of strike or lockout action, regardless of whether the statutory procedure has otherwise been followed. This absolute prohibition covers life- and property-rescue operations; funeral and cemetery services; the production, refining and distribution of urban water supply networks, electricity, natural gas and petroleum, together with petrochemical operations that begin from naphtha or natural gas; workplaces operated directly by the Ministry of National Defence, the Gendarmerie General Command and the Coast Guard Command; and firefighting and hospital services run by public bodies. In these categories, a strike or lockout cannot become lawful under any circumstances, even if every procedural step under the law is otherwise satisfied.

Beyond this fixed list, the President may temporarily prohibit strikes and lockouts at specific workplaces located in areas affected by a natural disaster that significantly disrupts ordinary life, for as long as that disruption continues; once the prohibition is lifted, the parties may resume strike or lockout action within sixty days, subject to six working days' prior notice to the other side. A separate, narrower prohibition applies to sea, air, rail and land transport vehicles that have not yet completed a journey already begun to a destination within Turkey — action cannot be taken until the vehicle reaches its domestic destination.

The Turkish Court of Cassation's General Assembly of Civil Chambers has confirmed that Law No. 6356 narrowed this list of absolute prohibitions compared to the earlier Law No. 2822 that it replaced, and — importantly — that whether a given workplace actually falls within the prohibited categories is a legal question ultimately reserved to the courts, not to the High Board of Arbitration.

## Employees Who Must Keep Working During a Lawful Strike or Lockout

A separate mechanism under Article 65 does not remove an entire workplace from the scope of strike and lockout action, but instead keeps a defined subset of employees at work even while a lawful strike or lockout is under way. The purpose is narrow: to maintain, throughout the action, a number of employees sufficient to preserve workplace safety, prevent damage to machinery, fixtures, raw materials, semi-finished and finished goods, or protect animals and plants — provided none of this work relates to production or sale of goods, and provided there is a genuine technical necessity for continuity.

The employer must post a written notice — within six working days of the start of collective bargaining — identifying the number, qualifications and substitutes of the employees who will be excluded from the strike or lockout, and must serve a copy on the employees' union. The union may challenge this notice before the courts within six working days of service; absent a challenge, the notice becomes final, and where a challenge is filed the court decides definitively within six working days. If the required determination is never made through this procedure, either the employer or the union may ask the competent authority to determine the number of employees required to keep working, even after the statutory deadline has passed; the authority's own determination can likewise be challenged before the courts within six working days. Union and branch officers who are themselves parties to the collective bargaining are exempt from this mandatory work obligation, preserving the union's ability to maintain representation on the ground during the action itself.

## Legal Consequences of an Unlawful Strike or Lockout

Article 70 sets out the consequences for both sides once a strike or lockout is found to be unlawful. On the employee side, the employer may terminate for just cause the employment contract not only of an employee who actually took part in an unlawful strike, but also of one who participated in the decision to strike or who encouraged others to start or continue the action — a termination ground specific to Law No. 6356, separate from the general just-cause grounds under Article 25/II of the Labour Law No. 4857. Any losses the employer suffers because of the unlawful strike are recoverable from the labour organisation that decided on the strike; where the unlawful strike was not the product of any union decision at all, the employees who took part in it bear that liability directly.

The same article works symmetrically for an unlawful lockout: the excluded employees may terminate their own employment contracts for just cause, and the employer must pay them everything they would have been entitled to under their employment contracts for the lockout period — without any work having been performed in exchange — and compensate them for any further losses they suffered as a result.

## How Turkish Courts Assess an Unlawful Strike Claim

Decisions of the 9th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation show that classifying a work stoppage as an "unlawful strike" requires careful case-by-case scrutiny rather than a mechanical label. Before an employee can be terminated on this ground, the employer must actually prove that the individual took part in the action — mere allegation or assumption is not enough. Turkish case law also draws a meaningful line between a genuine unlawful strike and employees collectively withholding their labour because their wages were not paid within the statutory deadline: where wages are late under Labour Law No. 4857, an employee's refusal to work is not treated as an unlawful strike even if several employees act together. Courts assessing dismissals grounded in an alleged unlawful strike also apply principles of proportionality and equal treatment, expecting a consistent approach across employees who took part in the same incident rather than a selective one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is every collective work stoppage automatically an unlawful strike?**
No. Turkish courts distinguish a genuine unlawful strike from situations such as employees collectively refusing to work because their wages were not paid within the legal deadline — the latter does not, on its own, amount to an unlawful strike even though it involves a group of employees acting together.

**Can an employer dismiss every employee involved in an unlawful strike the same way?**
Not automatically. The employer must be able to show actual participation in — or encouragement of — the unlawful action for each individual, and courts expect consistent, non-discriminatory treatment among employees involved in the same incident, applying principles of proportionality and equal treatment.

**Are strikes and lockouts ever completely prohibited, regardless of procedure?**
Yes. Article 62 of Law No. 6356 lists categories of workplaces — such as life- and property-rescue services, hospitals, firefighting, and the production and distribution of water, electricity, natural gas and petroleum — where strikes and lockouts cannot become lawful under any circumstances.

**Do all employees stop working during a lawful strike or lockout?**
Not necessarily. Under Article 65, the employer must identify a limited number of employees who continue to work throughout the action to preserve workplace safety and prevent damage to equipment, materials or goods, subject to challenge and confirmation through a defined notice and court-review procedure.
