Reconciliation between a victim and a suspect or defendant is one of the few mechanisms in Turkish criminal procedure that can end a case without a conviction and without the state ever exercising its power to punish. Articles 253 through 255 of the Turkish Code of Criminal Procedure (Law No. 5271, "CPC") set out not just which offences qualify for this restorative process, but a detailed, technical procedure for how the reconciliation offer must be made, negotiated, and enforced. In practice, a large share of disputes over reconciliation do not concern whether the parties actually reached an agreement — they concern whether the offer that preceded it was delivered the way the law requires. This article looks closely at that procedural layer: how a case is routed to reconciliation, what a properly made offer looks like, what happens once performance is agreed, and what recourse exists if the other side does not follow through.

## The Three Categories of Offences Covered by Article 253

Article 253 does not use a single test for which offences qualify; it lays out three separate categories. The first covers any offence whose investigation and prosecution depend on the victim filing a complaint, regardless of what that offence is. The second is a fixed list of Penal Code offences that qualify for reconciliation whether or not they require a complaint, including intentional and negligent bodily injury, threats, violation of the immunity of the home, violation of the freedom to work, theft, breach of trust, fraud, acquiring or accepting the proceeds of crime, and abduction of a child. The third category applies specifically to children in conflict with the law: where the victim is a natural person or a private-law legal entity, offences carrying an upper penalty of no more than three years of imprisonment or a judicial fine also qualify — a deliberately wider scope reflecting the restorative philosophy that underlies the juvenile justice system. For offences created by statutes other than the Penal Code, Article 253/2 adds a further requirement: unless the offence is complaint-dependent, reconciliation is only available if that specific statute expressly provides for it. Courts do not extend the mechanism to other special-law offences by analogy.

Article 253/3 then carves out firm exceptions. Offences against sexual inviolability, the offence of persistent stalking, and the offence of defamation are excluded from reconciliation even though defamation and some sexual offences would otherwise be complaint-dependent. The legislature's reasoning is that the imbalance of power often present between victim and offender in these categories makes a negotiated settlement risky for the victim. The same provision adds a rule aimed at closing a loophole: if an offence that would otherwise qualify for reconciliation is committed against the same victim together with a different, non-qualifying offence, reconciliation is unavailable for either offence. An offender cannot bundle a covered offence with an excluded one to access the mechanism.

## Delivering a Valid Reconciliation Offer: Why Procedure Decides Outcomes

Once the public prosecutor determines that the offence under investigation qualifies for reconciliation and that there is sufficient suspicion to justify prosecution, the file is transferred to the mediation office, which assigns a mediator drawn from law-faculty graduates. The mediator's role is strictly neutral — managing the negotiation, not proposing or imposing a particular resolution. If either the suspect, the victim, or the person harmed by the offence is a minor, the offer must be directed to their legal representative rather than to the minor directly.

How the offer is delivered matters more than it might appear. The preferred method is a face-to-face communication; where that is not practical, the law permits service with explanation, judicial assistance between courts, or audio-visual systems such as SEGBİS. What the law does not treat as sufficient is a bare telephone call — Turkish appellate practice has repeatedly held that a phone call alone does not amount to a properly made reconciliation offer, because it cannot reliably confirm that the party understood both the proposal and the legal consequences of accepting or rejecting it. A recipient who does not respond within seven days of being properly notified is deemed to have rejected the offer. If a party cannot be reached at their address of record, the file proceeds without reconciliation being attempted at all, and where there is more than one victim, every one of them must accept before reconciliation can be treated as achieved.

A second procedural point that recurs in practice concerns who receives the offer. The right to accept or reject reconciliation is treated as a personal right belonging to the party themselves, not an ordinary matter that any attorney of record can decide. An offer made only to a party's lawyer is not automatically valid unless that lawyer's power of attorney contains an explicit, specific authorization to reconcile on the client's behalf. This is a frequent point of failure: a settlement negotiated between attorneys can later be found procedurally defective if the underlying power of attorney did not specifically cover reconciliation.

Once negotiations begin, they are confidential. The mediator must ordinarily conclude the process within thirty days, a period the mediation office can extend twice by twenty days each. If the parties reach an agreement, the mediator prepares a detailed report bearing their signatures. Nothing said during the negotiation itself may later be used as evidence in the investigation or any prosecution — a protection that is essential to letting both sides negotiate candidly.

## What Happens at the Prosecution Stage If Reconciliation Was Missed Earlier

Reconciliation is meant to be resolved during the investigation stage, before an indictment is filed. But if the point was overlooked when the case was filed, or if it only becomes apparent at trial that the charged offence qualifies, Article 254 allows the court to send the file to the mediation office at that later stage, applying the same procedure set out in Article 253. The consequence of a successful reconciliation then depends on how the agreed act is to be performed. If the defendant performs the agreed act in a single, immediate act, the court issues a decision that the case has lapsed. If performance is deferred to a later date, paid in instalments, or is of a continuing nature, the court instead issues a decision suspending proceedings, during which the statute of limitations does not run. If the defendant later fails to perform as agreed, the trial resumes from where it left off — a safeguard against using reconciliation merely to buy time.

## Multiple Offenders: Reconciliation Is Personal, Not Collective

Where an offence was committed by more than one person, whether or not they acted in concert, Article 255 makes clear that only the person who actually reconciles benefits from it. Reconciliation is evaluated separately for each suspect or defendant; one offender's agreement with the victim has no automatic effect on a co-offender who was not part of that agreement. In practice, this means the mediator must extend a separate offer to each individual suspect — a blanket approach, or an offer addressed only to one of several co-offenders, does not bind or benefit the others.

## Consequences at the Investigation Stage and What Happens If a Party Does Not Perform

At the investigation stage, the same distinction between immediate and deferred performance governs the outcome. If the suspect performs the agreed act immediately, the prosecutor issues a decision of non-prosecution. If performance is deferred, paid in instalments, or continuing, the prosecutor issues a decision postponing the filing of the public case — without needing to satisfy the conditions normally required for that kind of postponement — and the statute of limitations is suspended for the duration. Successful reconciliation also bars a separate compensation lawsuit over the same offence, except for damage that could not be identified at the time or that only emerged afterward; any compensation case already filed is treated as withdrawn.

If a party fails to perform their side of the bargain, the written reconciliation report is not merely a broken promise — it is treated as a document having the legal force of a court judgment for enforcement purposes, meaning it can be taken directly to enforcement proceedings without the need for a fresh lawsuit. This gives the victim a genuine enforcement mechanism rather than leaving them to start over. If the reconciliation attempt fails outright, the law prohibits any second attempt in the same file, which keeps the process from being used to stall proceedings indefinitely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can my lawyer accept or reject a reconciliation offer on my behalf without asking me first?**
Only if the power of attorney you gave your lawyer specifically authorizes reconciliation. Because the decision to reconcile is treated as a personal right, Turkish courts have found offers made solely to a general-authority attorney to be procedurally invalid, meaning the reconciliation attempt has to be redone properly.

**Is a phone call from the mediator enough to count as a valid offer?**
No. Turkish appellate case law treats a bare telephone call as insufficient. The offer should be made face-to-face where possible, or through formal service, judicial assistance, or an audio-visual system, so that the party's understanding of the offer and its consequences can be verified.

**If there are several co-defendants, does one of them reconciling help the others?**
No. Under Article 255, reconciliation is assessed individually for each suspect or defendant. A mediator must make a separate offer to each one, and an agreement reached with one offender has no legal effect for co-offenders who did not separately reconcile.

**What happens if the other party agrees to pay compensation as part of reconciliation but then does not pay?**
The signed reconciliation report can be enforced directly, without a new lawsuit, because the law treats it as having the same legal force as a court judgment for enforcement purposes.
