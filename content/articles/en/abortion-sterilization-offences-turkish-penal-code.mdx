The Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") treats interference with pregnancy and with a person's reproductive capacity as a distinct family of offences against the person, set out in Articles 99 through 101. These provisions sit alongside — and are read together with — the administrative and medical framework of the Population Planning Law (Law No. 2827), which sets the conditions under which a pregnancy may lawfully be terminated or a person may lawfully be sterilized. Where a procedure stays within that administrative framework, no offence arises; where it departs from it — most often because the pregnancy has passed a fixed time limit, because consent is missing, or because the person performing the procedure lacks the required medical authorization — the criminal provisions apply. This article explains how Turkish law structures these offences for a foreign audience, including residents, medical professionals and international counsel who need to understand the framework without wading through the statutory text itself.

## What the Law Protects

The offences in this group protect several distinct interests at once. First, they protect the interest of the fetus in continuing to develop to term. Because Turkish law does not treat a fetus as a person capable of being a victim of homicide, the legislature created these separate offence categories specifically to fill that gap and criminalize interference with an ongoing pregnancy.

Second, the provisions protect the pregnant woman's own bodily integrity and reproductive autonomy. Terminating a pregnancy without her consent is an assault on both the fetus and on her right to decide whether to continue the pregnancy.

Third, sterilization offences protect a person's bodily integrity and reproductive capacity directly, regardless of the person's sex.

Fourth, running through all of these provisions is a concern for medical safety: the law separately penalizes performing an abortion or sterilization procedure without the medical authorization to do so, reinforcing that these are procedures only qualified practitioners should carry out.

## Causing an Abortion (Article 99)

Article 99 targets a person other than the pregnant woman herself who terminates her pregnancy. Anyone can commit this offence, including a physician or other health professional; whether or not the person performing the procedure held the necessary medical authorization does not change whether the offence exists, but it materially affects the applicable penalty.

Two variables determine how the offence is classified and punished. The first is whether the woman consented. Terminating a pregnancy without her consent is the most serious form of the offence and carries a prison sentence in the range of five to ten years. The second variable is the length of the pregnancy. Even where the woman has consented, terminating a pregnancy of more than ten weeks' duration — absent a genuine medical necessity — is punished less severely than the non-consensual form, with a sentence in the range of two to four years' imprisonment. In that scenario, the law also separately penalizes the woman herself, who faces up to one year's imprisonment or a judicial fine for consenting to the termination of a pregnancy beyond the ten-week mark.

Where the non-consensual act causes physical or psychological harm to the woman, the sentence rises sharply — to a range of six to twelve years — and if it causes her death, the range becomes fifteen to twenty years. Parallel, somewhat lower increases apply to the consent-based, past-ten-week variant: three to six years where harm results, and four to eight years where death results.

A further aggravating rule applies regardless of the pregnancy's stage: if the person who performs the termination is not authorized to do so medically, and the pregnancy has not yet reached ten weeks, the penalty is two to four years' imprisonment; for the other variants of the offence, being performed by an unauthorized person increases the otherwise-applicable sentence by half.

The offence requires that the fetus's presence in the womb actually come to an end — it is a result crime, not merely an attempt to interfere with the pregnancy — and it can be carried out by any means: medication, a physical procedure, or direct physical force against the woman.

## Self-Induced Termination (Article 100)

Article 100 covers a narrower and structurally different situation: a pregnant woman who terminates her own pregnancy voluntarily. Because only the pregnant woman herself can be the person who physically commits this act, it is what Turkish criminal doctrine calls a "proper" or self-contained offence — no one else can be its principal offender, although third parties can still be liable as instigators or accomplices under general participation rules.

Critically, the woman's own act is only punishable once the pregnancy has exceeded ten weeks. A woman who terminates her own pregnancy before the ten-week mark commits no offence under Turkish law at all. Where the ten-week threshold has been crossed, the applicable penalty mirrors the lighter, consent-based track under Article 99 — up to one year's imprisonment or a judicial fine.

## Sterilization Offences (Article 101)

Article 101 addresses procedures that eliminate a person's capacity to reproduce. Anyone can be the offender, and the offence is punishable regardless of the victim's sex — the statute expressly protects both men and women against unlawful sterilization.

The baseline offence is sterilizing a person without consent, punished by three to six years' imprisonment. As with abortion offences, medical authorization is a decisive factor: performing sterilization without the required authorization to do so increases the sentence for the non-consensual form by one-third. Where the person carrying out the sterilization lacks authorization but the affected person did in fact consent, the applicable sentence is one to three years' imprisonment — a materially lower range than the non-consensual offence, since consent remains relevant even though it does not eliminate liability once an unauthorized practitioner is involved.

The mental element for sterilization requires that the person performing the procedure act knowing that the intervention will eliminate the victim's reproductive capacity.

## Fault, Attempt, Participation and Concurrence

All three offences require intent (kast); the acting person must know that a pregnancy exists, or that the procedure will eliminate reproductive capacity, and must want that result to occur. Where consent-based liability turns on the pregnancy having passed ten weeks, a genuine mistake about the length of the pregnancy is treated as negating the required intent for the more serious variant, since Turkish law does not punish these acts when committed only negligently — only the aggravated forms tied to a resulting physical, psychological or fatal harm allow for a lower fault standard as to that particular consequence.

Because Articles 99 and 101 describe an actual result — the pregnancy ending, or reproductive capacity being eliminated — both are capable of being attempted: if the procedure is begun but the pregnancy does not in fact end, or the sterilization does not in fact take effect, attempt provisions apply. Article 100, by contrast, can only be attempted by the pregnant woman herself, given its self-contained nature.

Participation in the causing-abortion and sterilization offences is possible in every form recognized by Turkish law — principal, instigator, or accomplice. Because self-induced termination under Article 100 can only be committed by the pregnant woman, a third party who physically performs the procedure with her consent is charged directly under Article 99 rather than as a participant in her Article 100 offence; other third parties who merely encourage or assist her own act are liable only as instigators or accomplices to that act.

Where a non-consensual termination under Article 99 causes bodily or psychological harm or death, that consequence is already built into the offence's own aggravated brackets, so the ordinary assault or homicide provisions do not apply separately; the enhanced penalty ranges within Article 99 absorb those results. If, however, an independent offence is committed in the course of the same events, ordinary rules on concurrence of offences (gerçek içtima) apply.

## The Non-Punishment Rule for Pregnancy Resulting From a Crime

Turkish law carves out a specific exception from criminal liability where a woman becomes pregnant as a result of being the victim of a crime. In that situation, a person who terminates the pregnancy is not punished, provided that the pregnancy does not exceed twenty weeks and the woman consents to the termination. This exception is conditional, not automatic: the termination must also be carried out by specialist physicians in a hospital setting. All of these conditions — the twenty-week ceiling, the woman's consent, qualified physicians, and a hospital environment — must be satisfied together; if any one of them is missing, the exception from punishment does not apply and the ordinary rules under Article 99 govern instead.

## Procedural Notes

These offences are not subject to a complaint requirement — prosecutors investigate and prosecute them on their own initiative, without needing a formal complaint from a victim. They also fall outside the categories of offences eligible for mediation under Turkish criminal procedure. Jurisdiction generally lies with the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi), but where the applicable maximum sentence exceeds ten years — notably the aggravated brackets for non-consensual termination causing harm or death, and cases where the sentence is increased by half for an unauthorized practitioner — jurisdiction shifts to the heavy penal court (ağır ceza mahkemesi). The applicable limitation period for prosecution is likewise calculated from the maximum sentence set out for the specific paragraph engaged. In practice, establishing the length of the pregnancy, whether a genuine medical necessity existed, and whether valid consent was given are the central factual questions, and they typically require forensic medical examination to resolve.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it a criminal offence for a woman to terminate her own pregnancy in Turkey?**
Only if the pregnancy has exceeded ten weeks. A woman who terminates her own pregnancy before that point commits no offence under Article 100. Past the ten-week mark, she faces up to one year's imprisonment or a judicial fine, and the same threshold triggers liability for a woman who consents to a third party terminating her pregnancy under Article 99.

**Does it matter whether the person performing an abortion or sterilization is a licensed physician?**
Yes, significantly. Lacking the medical authorization to perform the procedure is treated as a separate aggravating factor throughout Articles 99 and 101, on top of whatever penalty already applies based on consent and the stage of pregnancy. The law is structured to ensure these procedures are carried out only by properly authorized practitioners.

**Can a woman who becomes pregnant through a crime terminate that pregnancy without criminal consequences?**
Yes, but only under cumulative conditions: the pregnancy must not exceed twenty weeks, the woman must consent, and the termination must be performed by specialist physicians in a hospital. If any of these conditions is not met, the exception does not apply.

**Are these offences prosecuted only if the victim files a complaint?**
No. Causing an abortion, self-induced termination, and sterilization offences are all prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative; no complaint from the woman or affected person is required, and these offences cannot be resolved through mediation.
