Foreign nationals facing a criminal investigation in Turkey often assume the only two outcomes are freedom or jail. In practice, Turkish criminal procedure offers a middle path: judicial control, a set of court-imposed supervisory conditions that a suspect or defendant must follow while an investigation or trial proceeds, instead of being held in pretrial detention. For a foreign suspect, this measure typically appears in the form of a ban on leaving the country, a duty to sign in periodically at a police station, or both. Understanding how judicial control works — who can order it, what conditions it may include, how long it can last, and what happens if it is breached — matters enormously to anyone caught up in a Turkish criminal case, since it can mean the difference between staying at liberty and being remanded in custody.

## What Judicial Control Is and How It Relates to Detention

Judicial control is regulated in Article 109 of the Criminal Procedure Code (CMK) and is not a freestanding measure that a prosecutor or judge can reach for whenever they like. It is legally tied to pretrial detention: a court may only order judicial control where the grounds for detention set out in Article 100 of the same code — concrete facts suggesting a flight risk, or a danger that evidence will be tampered with — are actually present in the case. In other words, judicial control is the more proportionate alternative a judge chooses instead of detention once the legal threshold for detention has already been met; it is not an independent, lesser sanction that can be imposed when no detention ground exists at all. Turkish appellate practice has consistently reinforced this link, holding that judicial control serves the same underlying purpose as detention — preventing a suspect or defendant from fleeing, hiding, or tampering with evidence — and that ordering it where no detention ground exists is itself unlawful. The law does carve out one broader application, however: even in cases where detention is expressly prohibited by statute, judicial control may still be applied, giving it a somewhat wider practical reach than detention itself.

## The Conditions a Court Can Impose

Article 109(3) lists a range of conditions a court may combine, tailored to the individual case rather than applied as a fixed package. They include a ban on leaving the country; a duty to report periodically to a place and within intervals set by the judge; compliance with supervisory check-ins with a designated authority or person, including in connection with work or continuing education; a ban on driving vehicles (with surrender of the driving licence against a receipt); mandatory treatment or examination for drug, alcohol or substance dependency, including inpatient treatment where necessary; depositing a financial security set by the judge at the prosecutor's request; a ban on possessing or carrying firearms, with any owned weapons surrendered into judicial custody; posting a security to protect a victim's rights, in an amount and on a payment schedule set by the judge; a guarantee that family obligations and any court-ordered maintenance payments will be met; and restrictions on leaving one's home, leaving a designated place of residence, or entering specified locations or areas. In practice, the two conditions used most often are the travel ban and periodic reporting to the police. Turkish courts have made clear that none of these conditions — including a travel ban — may be imposed unless the underlying requirements of Article 109 are actually satisfied and the order follows proper procedure; a travel ban tacked onto a conviction as an afterthought, without a genuine judicial-control decision behind it, is unlawful. Each condition is meant to be justified on its own footing and tested against the principle of proportionality, not applied automatically as a matter of routine.

## Who Decides, and for How Long

During the investigation stage, a judicial control order is issued by a magistrate judge (sulh ceza hâkimi) at the prosecutor's request, and it can be entered at any point in the investigation; once the case moves to trial, the competent trial court has the same authority. Article 110 also allows the same body to revisit its own order later — adding new conditions, lifting or modifying existing ones, or temporarily excusing the suspect from complying with some of them — which reflects the fact that judicial control is meant to flex with how the case develops rather than remain frozen at the moment it was first ordered. A 2021 amendment (Law No. 7331) introduced outer time limits that did not previously exist: judicial control may run for up to two years in cases outside the jurisdiction of the heavy penal courts (extendable by one more year where necessary), and up to three years in cases within the heavy penal courts' jurisdiction (extendable up to four years for certain offences); for minors, these periods are halved. The amendment was designed specifically to stop judicial control from being left in place indefinitely.

## Non-Compliance and the Route Back to Detention

The relationship between judicial control and detention is not one-directional. Under Article 112, if a suspect or defendant deliberately fails to comply with the conditions imposed — for example, missing the reporting schedule or breaching a travel ban — the competent court may order immediate detention regardless of the maximum prison sentence the underlying charge would otherwise carry. This provision exists precisely to keep judicial control meaningful; without it, the conditions would carry no real consequence. At the same time, Article 111(2) guarantees a right to challenge any judicial control decision — whether it imposes, modifies, or lifts a condition — and a suspect or defendant may specifically ask the court to remove or ease the conditions under Article 110(2). The court must decide on such a request within five days of hearing the prosecutor's opinion. Any financial security posted as one of the conditions is separately addressed under Article 115: it is returned once the person has fulfilled the relevant obligations, and it is likewise returned, in whole or in relevant part, where the case ends in a decision not to prosecute or an acquittal.

## Judicial Control and Compensation Claims

A judicial control measure that runs for years — particularly a travel ban that disrupts a person's business or family life abroad — can cause real hardship. Turkish appellate case law, however, has consistently held that the situations giving rise to a compensation claim under Article 141 of the Criminal Procedure Code are set out as a closed, exhaustive list, and judicial control is not one of them. As a result, simply having been placed under judicial control does not, on its own, entitle a person to compensation, even where the measure lasted for years. Courts have treated this as a natural consequence of judicial control being considered a lighter measure than detention, though this outcome has drawn criticism in cases involving multi-year travel bans where the affected person has no ordinary avenue for redress. That said, Turkish courts have recognised two narrower paths to compensation in practice: general liability under Article 141(3) can apply where a judicial control measure, though not compensable in itself, was applied for so long and so intensively that it effectively amounted to detention in substance, and it can also apply where a judicial control order was formally lifted but continued to be enforced afterward due to an administrative failure to notify the relevant authorities. Because judicial control shares its legal grounding with detention, careful scrutiny of whether the underlying detention conditions genuinely existed is critical whenever a judicial control order is challenged.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can judicial control be imposed even if there is no ground for detention?**
No. Judicial control under Article 109 requires that the grounds for detention listed in Article 100 of the Criminal Procedure Code actually exist in the case. If no detention ground is present, judicial control cannot lawfully be ordered either.

**What happens if someone breaches their judicial control conditions, such as a travel ban?**
Under Article 112, deliberate non-compliance allows the competent court to order the person's detention immediately, regardless of the maximum sentence the charge carries. This is the main enforcement mechanism behind judicial control.

**Is a person entitled to compensation just for having been under judicial control for a long time?**
Generally no. Turkish courts treat the list of compensable measures under Article 141(1) of the Criminal Procedure Code as exhaustive, and judicial control is not included. Compensation has only been awarded in narrower situations, such as where a measure was so prolonged and intensive that it effectively became equivalent to detention, or where a lifted order was still being enforced due to an administrative failure.

**Can the conditions of judicial control be changed after they are first ordered?**
Yes. Under Article 110, the judge or court that ordered judicial control can later add, modify, ease, or lift conditions, and the affected person can apply for such changes. Any judicial control decision can also be formally challenged, with the court required to rule within five days of hearing the prosecutor's opinion.
