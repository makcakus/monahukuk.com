*Article 9 – (1) The mediator performs their duty with care, impartially and in person.*

*(2) A person appointed as mediator is obliged to inform the parties where there are significant circumstances and conditions requiring doubt as to their impartiality. Notwithstanding that disclosure, if the parties jointly so request, the mediator may take up the duty or continue the duty already undertaken.*

*(3) The mediator is obliged to observe equality between the parties.*

*(4) In an action brought in relation to the dispute in which they acted in that capacity, the mediator may not subsequently act as counsel for one of the parties.*

The mediator does not decide; yet they largely determine what information the parties will share, which offer will reach the table and along which axis the negotiation will run. That influence places the mediator at the centre of the impartiality obligation despite having no power of decision. Article 9 separates this obligation into four distinct duties, and a breach of each produces a different consequence.

## Performance in Person: Mediation Cannot Be Delegated

The word "in person" in Article 9/1 shows that mediation is a duty strictly attached to the individual. The appointed mediator cannot delegate the activity to a trainee, a clerk or another mediator; they conduct the meetings themselves and draw up the final minutes themselves. This is the natural consequence of the definition of "natural person" in Article 2/a — just as a law firm or a company cannot be a mediator, the mediator's duty cannot be assigned to an organisation.

A breach of the personal-performance principle directly affects the validity of the minutes. A meeting conducted by a person who has not been appointed does not count as mediation within the meaning of Article 2/b; the document drawn up at its end cannot benefit from the protection afforded by Article 18.

## The Content of the Duty of Care

"Care" is a standard left undefined by the Law, and its content is derived from the other provisions: inviting the parties to the first meeting as soon as possible (Art. 15/1), concluding the process within the three-week period in Article 18/A/9, informing the parties under Article 11, retaining the documents for five years under Article 17/4 and sending a copy of the final minutes to the Directorate General within one month.

The most debated aspect of the duty of care is the mediator's duty to **review the content of the settlement document**. In recording the matters agreed by the parties, the mediator must ensure that the scope does not remain uncertain, since under Article 18/5 no action may be brought again concerning the matters agreed upon. A settlement document with an ambiguous scope becomes the subject of a separate action about which claims were covered.

## Doubt as to Impartiality: The Duty to Disclose and Waiver

Article 9/2 imposes on the mediator a duty to **disclose** where there are "significant circumstances and conditions" requiring doubt as to their impartiality. The Law does not enumerate these situations; in practice, kinship with one of the parties, a prior mandate or consultancy relationship, partnership, an ongoing commercial relationship and having worked in the same office as the other side's lawyer are assessed within this scope.

The distinctive feature of the rule is that the outcome is not disqualification but **disclosure and consent**. Unlike the regime of judicial recusal, doubt as to the mediator's impartiality does not automatically remove them from the role: notwithstanding the disclosure, if the parties **jointly** so request, the mediator may take up or continue the duty. The word "jointly" is critical; the consent of one party is not sufficient.

Behind this flexibility lies the voluntary nature of mediation. The parties may find it more productive to work with a mediator familiar with the subject matter than with a complete stranger. But where no disclosure has been made, consent is not valid either; the later emergence of an undisclosed relationship becomes the basis for a request to annul the settlement document on the ground of defective consent.

## The Effect of Having Acted as Mediator on Later Proceedings

Article 9/4 prohibits the mediator from subsequently acting as counsel for one of the parties in an action brought in relation to the same dispute. The prohibition is absolute; it cannot be overcome by the parties' consent. The rationale is clear — the mediator is acquainted with the confidential information of both sides, their real bargaining limits and the weak points of the file.

The same idea has found a place among the grounds for recusal of a judge in administrative-justice legislation: having acted as mediator or as a conciliator in the dispute is treated as a ground for recusal. The 8th Chamber of the Council of State expressly referred to this rule in one of its decisions. On the same logic, appointing the mediator subsequently as an expert or arbitrator is likewise unacceptable.

A point requiring attention in practice is whether the prohibition extends to **the lawyer's firm**. Although the Law covers only the mediator personally, another lawyer from the same office taking on the file must be separately assessed under the Attorneyship Law and professional rules.

## Consequences of a Breach of Impartiality

A breach of impartiality may produce three distinct consequences. **Invalidity of the settlement document**: the 9th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation has held that, in a case where the mediator acted against one of the parties, the text drawn up did not qualify as a valid mediation settlement document and had to be assessed under the conditions in Article 420 of the Turkish Code of Obligations or the provisions on performance. **Removal from the registry**: under Article 21/2, a mediator found not to have fulfilled their obligations is given a written warning and, if the warning is not heeded, their defence is taken and removal from the registry may be requested from the Board. **Liability under professional rules**: the Turkish Mediators' Ethical Rules laid down by the Mediation Board contain detailed criteria on impartiality and independence; the Council of State has regarded the making of these rules as falling within the Board's statutory competence.

## Case Law of the Court of Cassation and the Council of State

The following decisions show how the mediator's duties of impartiality and care are reviewed.

**1. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2025/5789, Decision No. 2025/7613, dated 09.10.2025**

The Chamber held that a document drawn up in a case where the mediator acted in a manner adverse to a party did not qualify as a valid mediation settlement document, and stated that this document had to be assessed under the conditions in Article 420 of the Turkish Code of Obligations No. 6098 or the provisions on performance.

**2. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2024/8846, Decision No. 2024/11005, dated 09.09.2024**

The decision establishes that a mediator's complete failure to explain to the other side that they were the mediator and what the legal consequences of mediation are is incompatible with the mediator's independent and impartial position. The absence of any document showing that the parties had been invited, and the fact that the minutes had the character of a release, were also taken into account together in this assessment.

**3. Council of State, Council of Administrative Litigation Chambers, Case No. 2023/1830, Decision No. 2024/1165, dated 23.05.2024**

The Council rejected the claim that the Mediation Board had not been given competence to lay down the Turkish Mediators' Ethical Rules, stating that under Articles 30 and 32 of Law No. 6325 the Department is charged with ensuring that mediation services are carried out in an orderly and efficient manner, while the Board is charged with determining the professional rules of mediation.

**4. Council of State, 8th Chamber, Case No. 2023/3436, Decision No. 2023/3217, dated 16.06.2023**

In enumerating the grounds for recusal of a judge, the decision also refers to having acted as mediator or conciliator in the dispute as one of those grounds. The rule reflects the general approach that the office of mediator prevents later assumption of a judicial or representative role in the same dispute.

**5. Council of State, 10th Chamber, Case No. 2018/62, Decision No. 2022/6437, dated 22.12.2022**

The Chamber accepted that, although Law No. 6325 and the Regulation contain general provisions on the fundamental principles, rights and obligations of mediators, determining ethical rules for mediators is a necessity in order to meet an important need.
