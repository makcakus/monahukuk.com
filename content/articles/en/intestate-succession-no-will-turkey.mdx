Most people know they should write a will, but many never get around to it. In Turkey, dying without a will — known in legal terms as *vasiyetsiz vefat* — does not leave the estate in limbo. The Turkish Civil Code (Türk Medeni Kanunu — TMK, Law No. 4721) steps in with a detailed set of rules that determines exactly who inherits, in what order, and in what proportions. For foreign nationals who own property, hold bank accounts, or run a business in Turkey, understanding these rules is essential — because Turkish inheritance law applies to your Turkish assets regardless of your nationality.

## The Order of Legal Heirs

Turkish law ranks heirs in a strict sequence based on blood ties, governed by Articles 495 to 501 of the Civil Code.

**First group — descendants (TMK Article 495):** The deceased's primary heirs are their own children. Children inherit in equal shares. If a child has already died but left their own children, those grandchildren step into the parent's place and share that parent's portion — the principle known as representation (*halefiyet*). This chain of representation extends downward through every generation.

**Second group — parents and their descendants (TMK Article 496):** If the deceased left no living descendants at all, the estate passes to the parents — again in equal shares. If a parent has already died, that parent's descendants (siblings, nieces, nephews) step in by representation. If one side of the family has no living members, the entire estate goes to the other side.

**Third group — grandparents and their descendants (TMK Article 497):** Where neither descendants nor parents survive, the estate reaches the grandparents, again with representation applying at every level. Turkish law keeps searching outward through the family tree until a relative is found. Only when no relatives of any degree exist does the estate fall to the Turkish State under Article 501 of the Civil Code.

## The Surviving Spouse's Share

A surviving spouse does not fit neatly into the blood-heir sequence — their share is instead determined by which blood relatives happen to survive. Article 499 of the Turkish Civil Code sets out the four scenarios precisely:

- **With descendants:** the surviving spouse receives **one-quarter** of the estate.
- **With parents or their descendants:** the spouse receives **half** the estate.
- **With grandparents or their descendants:** the spouse receives **three-quarters**.
- **With no blood relatives at all:** the entire estate passes to the surviving spouse.

In practice, the most common outcome is a division between the surviving spouse and the children — the spouse receiving one-quarter and the children sharing the remaining three-quarters in equal parts.

## Reserved Shares — Limits on Freedom of Disposition

Even if a person does leave a will, the Civil Code protects certain heirs from being entirely cut out. Article 506 of the TMK sets the following minimum reserved shares (*saklı pay*):

- Descendants: **half** of their statutory inheritance share.
- Each parent: **one-quarter** of their statutory share.
- The surviving spouse: the **full** statutory share in all cases.

Any testamentary gift that reduces a protected heir below their reserved share can be challenged through a reduction action (*tenkis davası*) in the Turkish courts. A will that ignores these limits is not void — but the affected heir can seek judicial correction of the excessive portion.

## Foreign Nationals and Cross-Border Complications

The rules above apply directly to any property located in Turkey, regardless of the owner's nationality. Turkish courts apply Turkish law to real estate (*tapu*) and rights held in Turkey. For movable assets — bank accounts, vehicles, company shares — the applicable law may depend on international private law principles, particularly the deceased's last domicile. However, for Turkish real estate there is no exception: Turkish law governs.

If the deceased held assets in multiple countries, each country's courts will generally handle the assets in their own jurisdiction. A foreign national who owns an apartment in Antalya and a house abroad may face two separate inheritance procedures running simultaneously in two different legal systems. Planning ahead — particularly through a notarised will — is by far the simplest way to avoid this complexity.

## Obtaining the Inheritance Certificate (Veraset İlamı)

Before heirs can access bank accounts, transfer title deeds, or deal with any Turkish institution, they must obtain a formal inheritance certificate from a Turkish court or notary. This document — the *veraset ilamı* — lists all legal heirs and their shares. Without it, no Turkish institution will process transfers.

For foreign heirs living abroad, gathering the required documents (birth certificates, death certificates, apostilles, translations) and navigating the Turkish system can be time-consuming. Working with a Turkish lawyer in Antalya from the start typically reduces both the timeline and the number of costly missteps. If no one claims the estate — or if heirs are unknown — the court may appoint an estate administrator (*tereke kayyımı*) to protect the assets until the situation is resolved.

## Why Writing a Will Still Matters

Even when Turkish law gives a clear answer on who inherits, it may not give the answer the deceased would have wanted. The legal rules are rigid: a partner in a long-term relationship who was never legally married has no inheritance right at all under Article 499 — only a legally registered spouse is entitled. A stepchild receives nothing. A close friend who cared for the deceased receives nothing unless named in a will.

Beyond personal preferences, a will also allows the deceased to appoint an executor, consolidate assets, and reduce the administrative burden on surviving family. It cannot override the reserved shares under Article 506 — but within those limits, it gives considerable freedom to direct where the estate goes. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Does my foreign will automatically apply to my Turkish property?
A Turkish court will generally recognise a foreign will for movable assets, but for Turkish real estate the will must satisfy certain formal requirements and may need to go through a recognition process. See our article on [foreign will recognition in Turkey](/en/articles/foreign-will-recognition-turkey) for the details.

### Q: Can an unmarried partner inherit under Turkish intestate rules?
No. Article 499 of the Turkish Civil Code gives inheritance rights only to a legally registered spouse — not to a cohabiting partner, regardless of the length of the relationship. If you want your partner to inherit, you must name them explicitly in a valid will, subject to the reserved share limits under Article 506.

### Q: What happens if heirs cannot agree on how to divide the estate?
Any heir can ask a Turkish court to conduct a formal distribution (*paylaşma davası*). The court will assess the assets, settle debts, and divide what remains according to each heir's statutory share. This process can take considerable time and is far easier to avoid with clear advance planning.

### Q: How long does the intestate inheritance process take in Turkey?
It varies widely. Straightforward cases — a small estate, all heirs in Turkey, no disputes — can be resolved in a few months. Cases involving foreign heirs, multiple properties, or disputes can take considerably longer. An early application for the [inheritance certificate](/en/articles/certificate-of-inheritance-foreign-heirs) from a Turkish notary is typically the fastest first step.

### Q: Does the Turkish State really inherit if there are no relatives?
Yes — under Article 501 of the Civil Code, if no heirs of any degree survive and there is no surviving spouse, the entire estate vests in the Turkish State. This is rare in practice but does happen, particularly with elderly foreigners who have lost contact with their families over time.
