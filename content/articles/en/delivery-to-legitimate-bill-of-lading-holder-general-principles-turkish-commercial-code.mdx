With the bill of lading defined and its required content settled, TTK Art. 1230-1231 turn to the most practical question of all: who is actually entitled to receive the cargo?

## The Legitimate Holder Rule

Under Art. 1230(1), the legitimate holder of the bill of lading is entitled to receive the cargo — what matters is not whether the presenter is the original shipper, but whether they hold the document legitimately. Turkish courts treat this strictly: an agent that released cargo to a third party without the bill of lading being surrendered was found to have breached the standard of care expected of a diligent merchant, exposing it to liability for the resulting loss. What counts as "legitimate holder" depends on the bill's form: for a straight bill, only the named party qualifies; for an order bill, an unbroken chain of endorsements establishes legitimacy, much like bill-of-exchange endorsement; for a bearer bill, physical possession alone suffices. Where multiple originals were issued, surrendering just one is enough to claim delivery (Art. 1230(2)) — the originals are redundant copies of the same right, not independent claims.

## When Multiple Legitimate Holders Show Up at Once

That redundancy creates a risk: what if endorsements go wrong and several people show up simultaneously, each holding a seemingly valid original? Art. 1231 resolves this by taking the captain out of the adjudicating role entirely — the captain must reject all claimants and deposit the cargo in a public warehouse or other safe location, notifying every claimant of the reason. Determining who actually has the superior claim is a legal, often judicial, question that a captain on deck cannot reasonably be expected to resolve; wrongly delivering to the wrong holder exposes the carrier to liability toward the true rights-holder. The captain may also have an official record drawn up documenting the reasoning, with resulting costs covered by the carrier's lien.

## Practical Takeaway

For carriers and their agents, the rule is unforgiving: deliver without proper surrender of the bill, and liability follows almost automatically, regardless of good intentions. For anyone dealing in bills of lading — banks, buyers, insurers — knowing exactly which form (straight, order, bearer) governs a given transaction is essential to confirming who can actually claim the goods.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who may take delivery of the cargo?

Under Article 1230/1 TTK <b>the lawful holder of the bill of lading is entitled to take delivery of the goods</b>. Who that is depends on the type of bill: on a bill to a named person, the person named; on an order bill, the person who proves entitlement through a regular and unbroken chain of endorsements; on a bearer bill, whoever possesses it. Under Article 1230/2, where the bill was issued in several originals, <b>the goods are delivered to the lawful holder of a single original</b>.

### What happens if several people claim the cargo at the same time?

Under Article 1231/1 TTK, where <b>several lawful holders of the bill of lading apply at the same time, the master must refuse them all</b> and <b>deposit the goods in a public warehouse or another safe place</b>, and must <b>notify</b> those holders of this, stating the reasons for acting in that way. The master may not attempt to decide entitlement; the dispute is resolved between the holders.

### Why is a bill of lading issued in several originals?

In commercial practice the originals are sent by different routes as a safeguard against loss of the document. Under Article 1228/4 TTK <b>all originals of the bill must contain the same text and each must state the number of originals issued</b>. On delivery, however, Article 1230/2 makes presentation of a single original sufficient; under Article 1236 the goods are delivered against return of that original endorsed to acknowledge receipt.
