Intentional injury is one of the most frequently prosecuted offenses in Turkey, ranging from a minor push that leaves a bruise to an attack that permanently disables the victim. It sits at the core of the Turkish Penal Code's chapter on offenses against bodily integrity, and its structure is unusual in one important respect: whether a case can even proceed often depends on whether the victim files a formal complaint. For foreign nationals and international counsel encountering a Turkish criminal file involving a physical altercation, understanding this complaint mechanism — and how it interacts with the offense's aggravated forms — is essential to predicting how a case will unfold.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Article 86 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) protects a person's physical integrity and health. The protection is broader than it first appears: the statute treats an attack on a victim's mental or perceptual faculties as equivalent to physical harm, so an act that impairs a person's ability to perceive or process their surroundings can qualify just as much as an act that causes physical pain. Because bodily integrity is considered a personal, non-transferable interest, a victim's consent to being injured does not, as a general rule, remove the criminal character of the act — the main exceptions being lawful medical procedures and comparable situations recognized by law.

## Who Can Commit the Offense and Who Can Be a Victim

Intentional injury is a general offense: it can be committed by anyone, and no special status is required of the perpetrator. That said, certain relationships between perpetrator and victim — such as a parent-child, spousal, former-spousal or sibling relationship — turn an ordinary injury into an aggravated one, as does an abuse of official authority by a public official. These circumstances affect the severity of the sentence rather than the basic definition of the crime. On the victim side, any living person can be a victim regardless of age, sex or health condition, though the law singles out victims who cannot defend themselves physically or mentally — young children are frequently treated as falling into this category in practice — for harsher punishment of the offender. Where a minor injury is inflicted on a woman, the law also sets a higher statutory minimum sentence than would otherwise apply.

## The Physical Act and Its Required Outcome

The conduct element of intentional injury is not tied to any specific method — striking, pushing, cutting or burning can all satisfy it, provided the act produces one of three alternative outcomes: physical pain, impairment of health, or impairment of perceptual faculties. Only one of these three needs to occur for the offense to be complete; there is no requirement that all three be present simultaneously. Because the offense requires a concrete outcome rather than merely an action, a causal link between the conduct and the resulting harm must be established. In practice, a forensic medical report is what determines the severity of the injury, and in particular whether the harm is minor enough to be resolved with ordinary medical treatment — a distinction that separates the standard form of the offense from its minor variant, discussed below.

## Intent and the Line Between Injury and Attempted Homicide

Intentional injury, as its name indicates, can only be committed with intent — the perpetrator must act knowing and willing that the victim's body will be harmed. Turkish law recognizes both direct intent and a form of contingent intent where the perpetrator foresees the harmful outcome as a likely consequence and proceeds regardless. No particular motive is required for the offense to exist. The most contested question in practice is usually not whether the perpetrator acted intentionally, but toward what result the intent was directed: was the perpetrator's aim limited to injuring the victim, or did the conduct in fact aim at killing them? Courts weigh factors such as the type of weapon or object used, which part of the body was targeted, and the number and force of the blows to decide whether a case is properly charged as intentional injury or as attempted intentional homicide.

## The Minor Form and the Complaint Requirement

This is where Article 86 diverges most sharply from many other violent offenses. Where the effect of the injury on the victim is minor enough to be resolved through ordinary medical treatment, the offense is only prosecuted if the victim files a complaint, and the resulting sentence is lighter than for the standard form of the offense. If the victim never complains, or withdraws the complaint before a final judgment, the case cannot proceed on this ground alone. Where this minor form is committed against a woman, the statutory minimum sentence is raised, reflecting a deliberate legislative choice to treat violence against women more seriously even within the complaint-dependent category. Both the standard form of intentional injury and its minor, complaint-dependent form are also eligible for mediation between victim and offender — a distinct procedural track that can resolve the matter without a full trial — except where one of the aggravating circumstances discussed below is present.

## Aggravated Forms and Injury With Serious Consequences

Article 86(3) lists a set of circumstances that increase the sentence by half regardless of whether the victim complains: the injury is committed against an ascendant, descendant, spouse, former spouse or sibling; against a victim unable to defend themselves physically or mentally; because of a public duty the victim performs; through a public official's abuse of authority; with a weapon; or, most severely, out of a savage motive, which increases the sentence not by half but by a full multiple. None of these aggravated categories depends on a complaint — prosecution proceeds automatically once the facts are established.

A separate and more serious layer is added by Article 87, which addresses injury that results in a serious consequence for the victim — for example lasting impairment of a sense or organ function, permanent speech difficulty, a visible scar on the face, a life-threatening condition, premature birth caused to a pregnant victim, permanent loss of an organ's function, an incurable illness or vegetative state, loss of the ability to speak or to have children, a lasting change in facial appearance, miscarriage, or a bone fracture or dislocation. Depending on which consequence occurred, the sentence otherwise applicable under Article 86 is increased by a fixed multiple, by a proportion tied to how the fracture or dislocation affects the victim's vital functions, or — where the injury results in death — by a substantially higher fixed range of imprisonment set out directly in the statute. Holding the perpetrator liable for these aggravated consequences requires that the consequence be at least attributable to the perpetrator's negligence; liability for the aggravated outcome cannot be extended beyond that standard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a victim stop a prosecution for intentional injury by withdrawing a complaint?**
This depends on which form of the offense is at issue. Withdrawal is only effective for the minor, complaint-dependent form of the offense under Article 86(2). Once one of the aggravating circumstances under Article 86(3) applies, or the injury falls under Article 87's serious-consequence categories, the case proceeds regardless of the victim's wishes and a complaint is not required in the first place.

**Does the type of weapon used affect the outcome of the case?**
Yes, in two distinct ways. Use of a weapon is itself one of the statutory grounds that increases the sentence by half under Article 86(3). Separately, the nature of the weapon and how it was used are also examined by courts when determining whether the perpetrator's intent was limited to causing injury or extended to causing death, which can change the charge entirely.

**Is a forensic medical report always required in an injury case?**
In practice, yes. The forensic report is what establishes whether the injury is minor enough to fall under the complaint-dependent form of Article 86(2), or serious enough to fall under one of the aggravated categories of Article 86(3) or the serious-consequence categories of Article 87. This classification materially affects both the applicable sentence and whether the case can be resolved through mediation.

**Can a person be held liable for intentional injury if they only intended a fight, not a serious outcome?**
Liability for the base act still requires intent to cause the harmful result. However, where the injury unexpectedly develops into one of the serious consequences addressed by Article 87 — such as permanent disability or death — the perpetrator can be held liable for that aggravated outcome as long as they were at least negligent with respect to it, even without direct intent to cause that specific consequence.
