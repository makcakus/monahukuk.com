Anyone caught up in a Turkish criminal investigation — whether as a tourist questioned after an incident, a foreign resident facing a police interview, or a family member trying to understand what is happening to a relative in custody — quickly encounters an unfamiliar vocabulary: "suspect," "defendant," and "defense counsel" are not interchangeable terms but distinct legal statuses, each carrying its own set of rights. Turkish criminal procedure, primarily under Law No. 5271 (the Code of Criminal Procedure, commonly abbreviated CMK), builds its entire defense framework around the idea that a person facing the state's investigative and prosecutorial machinery should never have to face it alone. This article explains how that framework works in practice — who qualifies for a lawyer, when having one becomes mandatory rather than optional, and what happens when the rules are not followed.

## Suspect, Defendant and Defense Counsel: Three Distinct Roles

Turkish law draws a clear line between the investigation phase and the trial phase, and the terminology tracks that line. A person is a "suspect" for as long as an investigation into a suspected offense is ongoing but no formal indictment has yet been filed with a court. Once an indictment is accepted and the case moves to trial, the same person becomes a "defendant" — a status that continues until the final judgment becomes legally binding. The lawyer representing either a suspect or a defendant is referred to under Turkish law simply as "defense counsel," regardless of whether that lawyer was personally hired or appointed by the bar association. This unified terminology is a deliberate choice: an earlier procedural code distinguished between a bar-appointed lawyer and a privately retained one, treating them somewhat differently. The current Code eliminated that distinction so that the strength and scope of the right to counsel would not depend on how the lawyer came to represent the person.

## Choosing or Being Assigned Defense Counsel

A suspect or defendant may benefit from the assistance of one or more defense lawyers at every stage of both the investigation and the trial — this is not limited to the courtroom phase but extends to interviews, interrogation, searches, seizures, and any other investigative measure. If the person has a legal guardian, that guardian may also select a lawyer on their behalf. To keep police interviews manageable in practice, Turkish law caps the number of lawyers who may be physically present during an investigative interview at three. Separately, the right of a lawyer to consult with the suspect or defendant, to be present during questioning, and to provide legal assistance throughout that process cannot be obstructed or restricted under any circumstances — Turkish appellate courts treat interference with this right as a restriction of the defense itself, which is grounds for overturning a conviction.

There are two routes by which a person obtains defense counsel: they (or their legal guardian) may personally select and retain a lawyer, or, where the conditions described below are met, the local bar association appoints one. Under settled appellate practice, a privately retained lawyer does not need to produce a notarized power of attorney simply to act as defense counsel in the criminal proceeding — it is enough that the lawyer's appointment is properly communicated to the relevant authority. This flexibility exists precisely so that the right to a defense is not delayed by paperwork. That said, a formal power of attorney is still required for certain specific steps, such as requesting that the defendant be excused from personally attending a hearing, or withdrawing an appeal.

## When a Lawyer Is Mandatory, Not Optional

As a general rule, having a defense lawyer in a Turkish criminal proceeding is the suspect's or defendant's own choice. But Turkish law carves out important exceptions where a lawyer must be appointed regardless of whether the person asks for one. If a suspect or defendant without a lawyer states that they are unable to select one themselves, a lawyer is appointed at their request. Beyond that, appointment becomes compulsory — no request needed — in two situations: first, where the person is a minor, or has a disability or hearing/speech impairment severe enough to impede their own defense; and second, where the offense charged carries a minimum sentence exceeding five years' imprisonment. In both scenarios, the bar association appoints counsel on its own initiative. This reflects a considered policy choice: where a person's practical capacity to defend themselves is limited, or where the charge is serious enough that an inadequate defense could have consequences that are difficult to reverse, the state takes on a proactive duty to ensure legal representation exists.

Failures to observe the mandatory-counsel rule are a recurring basis for Turkish appellate courts to overturn convictions, particularly in cases involving pre-trial detention for serious offenses. One recurring scenario worth understanding: if the minimum sentence for an offense is later increased — through a legislative amendment enacted after the alleged offense but before the defendant's statement is taken — courts assess whether mandatory counsel applied by looking at the law as it stood on the date the statement was actually given, not the law in force when the offense was allegedly committed. Taking a defendant's statement without providing mandatory counsel where it was legally required amounts to an unlawful restriction of the defense.

## Confidential Access to Counsel and Its Narrow Exception

A cornerstone of the right to a defense is that a suspect or defendant may consult privately with their lawyer at any time, without needing to produce a power of attorney, in a setting where the conversation cannot be overheard by others, and correspondence between them is not subject to any form of monitoring. This confidentiality exists because no genuine defense strategy can be built if the client cannot speak freely with their own lawyer. Turkish law allows only one narrow exception: for certain serious offenses connected to organized criminal activity, and offenses falling within counter-terrorism legislation, a judge may — on the request of the public prosecutor — restrict a detained suspect's access to their lawyer for a period of up to twenty-four hours, during which no statement may be taken. The fact that this exception is confined to a specific, limited category of offenses and capped at twenty-four hours underscores that it is meant to be exceptional rather than routine.

## Procedural Safeguards During Questioning: The Right to Remain Silent

Turkish law sets out a detailed list of procedural safeguards that must be observed whenever a suspect or defendant is questioned by police, prosecutors, or a court: confirming the person's identity, explaining the offense they are suspected of, informing them of their right to choose a lawyer and that one can be appointed by the bar association if they cannot afford one, notifying a relative of the arrest, and — perhaps most importantly — advising them that they are under no legal obligation to make any statement about the accusation, in other words, that they have the right to remain silent. Whether a statement taken without these warnings having been properly given can lawfully be used as the basis for a conviction is something Turkish appellate courts consistently scrutinize; questioning a defendant without first informing them of the right to choose a lawyer is treated as a restriction of the right to a defense.

Exercising the right to remain silent can never be treated as an implicit admission of guilt or as behavior corroborating the accusation — drawing an inference of guilt from a person's silence violates both that right and the presumption of innocence. The most concrete safeguard in this area addresses the reliability of the statement itself: any statement by a suspect or defendant must be the product of their own free will, and statements obtained through mistreatment, torture, forced medication, exhaustion, deception, or physical or psychological coercion cannot be used as evidence even if the person appeared to consent at the time. On top of that, a statement taken by police in the absence of defense counsel cannot form the basis of a conviction unless the suspect or defendant later confirms it before a judge or the court. This rule exists to prevent the earliest and most vulnerable stage of a criminal case — the first statement given, often shortly after arrest — from single-handedly determining the outcome of a trial without the protection a lawyer's presence provides. Turkish appellate practice consistently holds that where the only evidence against a defendant is an uncorroborated confession taken by police without a lawyer present, and that confession is never confirmed at a later stage, the correct outcome is acquittal, not conviction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the right to a lawyer only apply once a case reaches trial?**
No. Under Turkish law, the right to defense counsel applies from the earliest stages of a criminal investigation — including police interviews, searches, and seizures — not just at the courtroom trial stage. The terminology shifts (a person is called a "suspect" during the investigation and a "defendant" after an indictment is filed), but the right to legal assistance is continuous.

**Can a foreign national appoint a Turkish lawyer without a notarized power of attorney?**
For most purposes, yes. A privately retained lawyer can act as defense counsel in a criminal case as soon as the appointment is properly communicated to the relevant authority, without needing a notarized document. A formal power of attorney is only required for specific procedural steps, such as excusing the defendant from a hearing or withdrawing an appeal.

**What happens if the police take a statement without a lawyer present?**
A statement taken by police without defense counsel present cannot, by itself, be used as the basis for a conviction unless the suspect or defendant later confirms that statement before a judge or the court. If that confirmation never happens and no other evidence exists, Turkish courts have held that a conviction based solely on the unconfirmed statement is unlawful.

**When is legal representation mandatory rather than a matter of personal choice?**
It becomes mandatory, regardless of whether the person requests it, when the suspect or defendant is a minor, has a disability or hearing/speech impairment that impedes their own defense, or faces a charge carrying a minimum sentence of more than five years' imprisonment. In these situations the bar association must appoint a lawyer on its own initiative.
