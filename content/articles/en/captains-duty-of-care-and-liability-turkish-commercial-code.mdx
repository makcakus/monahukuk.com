TTK Art. 1088-1089 open the Code's chapter on the ship's captain, setting the foundational standard of care and liability that every later, more specific captain's duty builds on.

## The "Prudent Captain" Standard

Art. 1088 requires the captain to act like a "prudent captain" in all matters — not just a defined list of duties, but everything connected with the ship and voyage, with particular emphasis on performing contracts the captain is responsible for executing. This is the maritime-specific version of the objective care standards found throughout Turkish commercial law (the "prudent merchant," the "prudent shipowner"): what matters is not the individual captain's personal skill or good intentions, but what an objectively careful, competent captain would have done in the same situation. A captain cannot defend a claim by saying "I did my best" if that best fell short of the objective professional standard.

## Personal Liability — Even When Following Orders

Art. 1089 makes the captain personally liable, toward everyone connected with the ship and cargo — including passengers — for damage caused by fault in failing to perform the captain's duties. Two linked rules give this real force. First, following the shipowner's orders does *not* excuse the captain (Art. 1089(2)) — closing off an obvious escape hatch that would otherwise let a captain hide behind "the owner told me to." Second, and cumulatively, a shipowner who knowingly gave an order likely to cause harm is *also* liable (Art. 1089(3)) — so the captain's and the shipowner's liability stack rather than substitute for each other. Neither "I was just following orders" nor "the captain acted on their own judgment" works as a complete defense. As with the shipowner's own liability, the captain's right to limit liability under international conventions Turkey has ratified is preserved.

## Practical Takeaway

Turkish courts apply the "prudent captain" standard rigorously in practice, not as an abstract slogan — one case examined whether a specific docking maneuver, given the vessel's engine power and prevailing conditions, could have been performed safely even accounting for a mechanical failure, and found the captain liable for not doing so. In a fatal sinking case, courts have split fault percentages between shipowner, captain, and even passengers based on each party's specific contribution.
