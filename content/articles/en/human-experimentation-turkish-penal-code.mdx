Foreign research institutions, pharmaceutical sponsors and clinicians working with Turkish hospitals or universities sometimes assume that clinical-trial compliance is purely a regulatory matter, handled through ethics committee approvals and administrative permits. Under Turkish law it is more than that: Article 90 of the Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) makes scientific experimentation on a human being a standalone criminal offense, sitting in the part of the Code devoted to crimes against bodily inviolability. The provision does not ban research on human subjects outright, but it conditions its lawfulness on a closed list of consent and safety requirements — and treats any experiment that fails even one of them as a crime, independent of whether the subject was actually harmed.

## What Interest the Offense Protects

The primary interest behind Article 90 is human dignity — the idea that a person may not be reduced to a mere instrument for generating scientific knowledge, even where no physical harm results. Alongside dignity, the provision protects the subject's bodily integrity, physical and mental health, and the underlying right to decide what happens to one's own body. It also has an institutional dimension: by tying human experimentation to a verifiable authorization and consent framework, the law protects the credibility of the research enterprise itself, not only the individual test subject. Because the interest protected is dignity and self-determination rather than physical safety alone, the offense is treated as complete the moment an unlawful experiment is carried out — a resulting injury is not an element of the crime, only an aggravating consequence.

## Who Can Commit the Offense and Who Is Protected

The basic offense under the first paragraph of Article 90 has no special-actor requirement: any person who conducts a scientific experiment on a human being can be held liable, whether or not they are a physician or a professional researcher. The picture changes for the separate offense addressed further below — treatment-oriented experimentation on a patient — where the statute's own conditions (treatment in a hospital setting, administered by a specialist physician) mean that in practice only a medical professional can lawfully invoke the defense, and only a person acting in that capacity typically faces this specific charge. Because modern research is usually carried out through institutions rather than lone individuals, the statute also addresses corporate involvement directly: where the offense is committed in the course of a legal entity's activities, the law provides for security measures against that entity, separate from the criminal liability of the natural persons involved.

The victim is simply the natural person on whom the experiment is performed. Two categories of victim receive distinct treatment within the article: children, who benefit from additional safeguards layered on top of the general consent regime, and patients, who are the specific subject of the treatment-oriented provision discussed below.

## Experiment Versus Treatment-Oriented Attempt: The Core Distinction

Article 90 actually defines two different offenses, and distinguishing them is the key to understanding the whole provision. The first and more serious offense is scientific *experimentation*: an intervention undertaken to generate knowledge whose outcome is not yet known, and which is not intended to benefit the subject directly. This is completed the moment the experiment is carried out, regardless of outcome.

The second, separately punished and more leniently treated offense is a treatment-oriented *attempt* (sometimes translated as a therapeutic trial) carried out on a patient without the patient's consent. Unlike an experiment, a treatment-oriented attempt is undertaken specifically because recognized medical methods have already proven ineffective for that patient, and the physician resorts to an unproven approach in the patient's own interest. Turkish courts applying this article have repeatedly stressed that the two are not interchangeable: a procedure performed on a healthy person for research purposes is an experiment, while a procedure performed on a patient who cannot be helped by known methods, undertaken to help that specific patient, is a treatment-oriented attempt — and the applicable consent regime, and the penalty, differ accordingly.

The offense in both forms can only be committed intentionally. The person carrying out the experiment must know and want to be performing a scientific experiment on a human being; because of how such interventions are structured, this direct intent is generally self-evident from the act itself. There is no criminal liability for a merely negligent version of this offense — a badly performed but non-experimental medical procedure falls instead under the ordinary rules on negligent bodily injury, not Article 90. Pursuing a scientific purpose does not excuse the actor; on the contrary, the scientific character of the intervention is precisely what makes it fall within this offense rather than an ordinary assault.

## The Seven Conditions for Lawful Consent-Based Experimentation

A consent-based scientific experiment on a human subject escapes criminal liability only if every one of the following conditions is met at once:

1. The required authorization has been obtained in advance from the competent board or authority.
2. The experiment has first been carried out in a non-human environment or on a sufficient number of animals.
3. The scientific data produced by that non-human or animal-based testing shows that testing on humans is genuinely necessary to reach the intended objective.
4. The experiment is not expected to leave any foreseeable, permanent harmful effect on the subject's health.
5. No method inconsistent with human dignity, causing pain beyond what is tolerable, is used on the subject during the experiment.
6. The objective pursued by the experiment outweighs the burden it places on the subject and the risk to that person's health.
7. The subject's consent is based on adequate information about the nature and consequences of the experiment, is given in writing, and is not conditioned on receiving any benefit in exchange.

These seven conditions are cumulative: the absence of even one of them makes the intervention unlawful and the basic offense is made out. At the center of the regime sits the concept of informed consent in its strict sense — a subject must actually understand the risks and likely consequences before agreeing, must record that agreement in writing, and must not have been paid or otherwise incentivized to participate. The ban on tying consent to any benefit exists specifically to prevent economically vulnerable individuals from being recruited as test subjects through payment. The written-form requirement is not just an evidentiary convenience; it is meant to force the subject to pause and genuinely weigh the decision before signing.

## Additional Protections for Children and for Treatment-Oriented Attempts

Where the subject is a child, the statute layers three further requirements on top of the general seven-condition regime: the scientific data must show that testing specifically on children (not just on adults) is necessary to reach the research objective; both the child's own consent (where the child is capable of expressing it) and the written consent of the parents or legal guardian must be obtained; and the approving board must include a specialist in pediatric medicine and diseases. These extra safeguards reflect the elevated protection Turkish law affords to minors as research subjects, on top of — never instead of — the general consent conditions.

For the separate treatment-oriented attempt on a patient, the statute requires that the intervention rely on scientifically grounded methods, that consent be informed and written, and that the treatment be administered by a specialist physician in a hospital setting. Where a treatment-oriented intervention proceeds without the patient's consent at all, it constitutes the lesser offense under the fourth paragraph of Article 90, carrying a lighter sentence than unlawful experimentation under the first paragraph.

## Consequences, Aggravation and Procedural Notes

The basic offense of unlawful human experimentation carries a prison sentence of one to three years; the law provides no option of a fine in lieu of imprisonment. Treatment-oriented attempts performed on a patient without consent carry a lighter sentence of up to one year. Where the unlawful experiment results in the subject's injury or death, the applicable provisions on intentional injury or intentional homicide apply instead — the experiment does not absorb these more serious consequences into a single lesser charge. Legal entities involved in the commission of the offense are subject to their own dedicated security measures. Because the offense is prosecuted ex officio rather than on complaint, and does not fall within the categories of offenses eligible for mediation, a criminal investigation can proceed regardless of whether the victim files a formal complaint. Given its penalty range, the case is tried before a first-instance criminal court (asliye ceza mahkemesi), and the general eight-year statute of limitations applies. Reported case law on this specific offense remains relatively sparse in Turkish practice; most disputes touching on human experimentation are resolved through administrative oversight of research institutions or civil compensation claims, which means the practical interpretation of Article 90 is still shaped largely by legal commentary and bioethical principles rather than a dense body of criminal precedent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Article 90 ban all scientific experimentation on humans in Turkey?**
No. It permits consent-based experimentation, but only where all seven statutory conditions are satisfied — prior authorization, prior non-human or animal testing, a demonstrated necessity to test on humans, no foreseeable permanent harm, no methods incompatible with human dignity, an objective that outweighs the risk, and informed written consent free of any inducement.

**What is the difference between a "scientific experiment" and a "treatment-oriented attempt" under this article?**
An experiment is performed to generate knowledge and is not aimed at benefiting the subject directly; it is typically performed on a person who is not being treated for an existing condition. A treatment-oriented attempt is performed on a patient specifically because recognized medical methods have failed, with the goal of helping that patient, and carries a distinct and lighter penalty when performed without consent.

**Can a research subject validly consent by signing a form without being told the risks?**
No. Consent is only valid if it follows adequate information about the experiment's nature and likely consequences, is recorded in writing, and is not linked to any payment or other benefit. A signature obtained without genuine, informed disclosure does not satisfy the statutory consent requirement.

**Are there extra requirements when the research subject is a child?**
Yes. In addition to the general consent conditions, the data must show the experiment is specifically necessary on children, both the child's own consent (if capable of giving it) and the written consent of the parents or guardian are required, and the approving board must include a pediatric specialist.
