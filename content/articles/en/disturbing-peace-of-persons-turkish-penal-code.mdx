A phone that rings several times over the course of a day, an upstairs neighbor dragging furniture at midnight, or a former acquaintance who keeps showing up at the door — none of these, viewed in isolation, are unusual or unlawful. Article 123 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") nonetheless makes room for a criminal offense built entirely out of ordinary, often lawful conduct — calling someone, making noise — once that conduct is combined with a very specific state of mind. Foreign nationals and businesses dealing with Turkish criminal complaints frequently underestimate how narrowly this offense is drawn, and how much weight Turkish courts place on the caller's purpose rather than the number of calls.

## What the Offense Protects and Who Can Be a Victim

Article 123 does not protect a person's body, property, or reputation — those interests are covered by other provisions. What it protects is something closer to a person's peace of mind and their ability to go about daily life without being deliberately harassed. Because that interest is inherently personal and psychological, only a natural person can be a victim of this offense; a company or other legal entity cannot bring a complaint under this article, since it has no "peace of mind" to disturb. The victim must also be a specific, identifiable individual — conduct that is simply a general nuisance to the public at large, without being aimed at any particular person, falls outside Article 123 and is instead handled, if at all, as an administrative or misdemeanor matter. No special status is required of the person accused: anyone can commit this offense, and the disturbing conduct can be directed at anyone.

## The Conduct the Law Targets

The statute identifies three ways the offense can be committed: repeatedly telephoning someone, making noise, or engaging in any other unlawful act carried out with the same purpose. The first two categories are concrete; the third is a deliberately open-ended catch-all that lets the provision keep pace with newer forms of harassment — persistent text messages, voice notes, social media notifications, or repeatedly ringing someone's doorbell can all, in principle, fall within it. That flexibility has a limit, however: whatever the method, the conduct itself must be unlawful in nature. An ordinary, tolerable contact that a person is otherwise entitled to make cannot be swept into the offense merely because the recipient found it unwelcome.

## Persistence: More Than a Head Count

The first pillar of the offense is persistence. A single phone call, one night of noise, or one unwelcome visit to someone's door does not satisfy this element, no matter how unpleasant it was. At the same time, persistence is not simply a matter of counting repetitions. Courts look at how the conduct is spread out over time, the hours chosen, whether it continued after the other person made clear they wanted no further contact, and whether the person responsible showed any willingness to stop. A short burst of calls concentrated in the middle of the night and a steady pattern of contact spread across several months can both satisfy the same persistence standard despite looking very different on paper. What does not qualify is a handful of scattered, unconnected contacts with no discernible pattern tying them together.

## The Narrow Intent Requirement That Makes or Breaks the Case

The second — and usually decisive — pillar is intent. This offense can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent version of it. Turkish law goes further than requiring ordinary intent, however: the person must have acted for the sole purpose of disturbing the other person's peace of mind. This narrow purpose functions as a gatekeeper for the entire offense. However persistent the conduct was, if the underlying goal was something other than simply harassing the victim, Article 123 does not apply.

This distinction surfaces most often in debt-collection scenarios. A creditor who calls a debtor repeatedly to recover a sum of money is, as a rule, not acting for the sole purpose of disturbing that person, even if the calls are unwelcome — the calls serve a separate, legitimate goal. The same reasoning applies to a parent trying to reach a child, someone following up on a work matter, or a person insisting that a service actually be delivered. That legitimate purpose is not an unlimited shield, though: if the manner of contact becomes completely detached from what the stated purpose actually requires and turns into conduct aimed only at wearing the other person down, the true purpose is treated as having shifted. The real question in any investigation, then, is not how many times contact was made, but why it was made. Notably, the offense does not require separate proof that the victim's peace was actually disturbed in fact — persistent conduct carried out with this specific purpose is sufficient in itself.

## Where Article 123 Ends and Related Offenses Begin

A related offense, stalking (TCK Art. 123/A), was added to the Penal Code in 2022 and punishes physically following a person or repeatedly attempting to contact them through communication tools, information systems, or third parties, where that conduct creates a serious sense of unease in the victim or fear for their own safety or that of people close to them; the penalty range is six months to two years. The key difference between the two offenses is the outcome required: Article 123 is satisfied by mere harassment, while Article 123/A requires that a serious sense of unease or genuine safety concern actually resulted. Article 123/A also carries an increased penalty of one to three years where the victim is a child or a former or separating spouse, where the conduct forces the victim to change or give up their school, job, or home, or where the person responsible is already subject to a restraining or no-contact order. Once conduct reaches that level of severity, Article 123/A displaces Article 123 rather than applying alongside it.

Persistent contact can also shade into other offenses depending on what is actually said. Where the words used during the contact amount to a threat, insult, or sexual harassment, the more specific offense applies instead of Article 123, and that offense is treated as absorbing the disturbance-of-peace charge — a person is not separately convicted of both disturbing the peace and, for example, insult, for the same course of conduct.

## Attempt, Participation, and How Repeated Acts Are Counted

Because persistence itself requires the conduct to reach a certain intensity before the offense is complete, an isolated act that falls short of that threshold is not an attempt — it is, as a rule, simply not a crime at all; attempt is only meaningfully discussed in unusual situations where the course of conduct can be broken into distinguishable stages. There is nothing unusual about how participation rules apply: the offense can be committed jointly, through instigation, or through assistance, like most other crimes. On the question of how repeated acts are counted, persistence already builds multiple instances of the conduct into the definition of the offense itself, so repeated acts against the same victim are treated as a single crime rather than as a chain of separate offenses. Where the same course of conduct disturbs more than one victim, however, a separate offense arises for each person affected.

## Complaint, Mediation, and Which Court Hears the Case

Prosecution depends on a complaint from the victim; the offense is not investigated on the authorities' own initiative. The complaint must be filed within six months of the victim learning who committed the act and what happened — after that window closes, no investigation can proceed. If the victim withdraws the complaint during trial and the accused agrees to that withdrawal, the case is dismissed. The prescribed penalty is imprisonment of three months to one year. Because the offense is complaint-dependent, the file is referred to mediation at the start of the investigation, and if the parties reach an agreement, a decision is issued that no prosecution will proceed. If mediation fails, the case is heard before the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi). Given the modest penalty range, conversion of a short prison sentence into an alternative sanction, and the option to defer the announcement of the verdict, both come up frequently in practice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a debtor's complaint about repeated collection calls automatically qualify under Article 123?**
Not usually. Where the caller's real purpose is to recover a debt rather than simply to disturb the other person, the calls generally fall outside Article 123, even if the debtor experiences them as harassing. The analysis changes if the calls stop tracking any real collection purpose and become directed purely at wearing the debtor down.

**How is "persistence" actually measured in practice?**
There is no fixed number of calls or incidents. Courts weigh frequency together with how the conduct is spread across time, the hours chosen, whether contact continued after a clear refusal, and whether the person responsible showed any willingness to stop. A cluster of contacts in a short period and a steady pattern spread over months can each satisfy the requirement.

**What is the difference between this offense and the stalking offense under Article 123/A?**
Article 123 only requires persistent, unlawful contact carried out for the sole purpose of disturbing the other person; it does not require proof that the victim's peace was actually disturbed. Article 123/A requires an actual outcome — a serious sense of unease or a genuine safety concern for the victim or those close to them — and carries a higher penalty, particularly where the victim is a child, a former or separating spouse, or where the conduct forces the victim to change residence, school, or job.

**If the calls or messages contain threats or insults, is the offender charged under both provisions?**
No. Where the content of the contact rises to the level of a specific offense such as threat, insult, or sexual harassment, that offense applies on its own and is treated as absorbing the disturbance-of-peace charge; the same conduct is not punished twice.
