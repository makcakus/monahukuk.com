Turkish criminal law treats sexual offenses against children as a distinct and considerably more serious category than sexual offenses against adults. Article 103 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), found in the chapter on offenses against sexual inviolability, sets out a layered scheme in which the applicable penalty range depends on the victim's age, whether the act rose above a fleeting or momentary level, and whether it involved penetration. For international counsel, foreign parents, or foreign nationals facing proceedings in Turkey, understanding this structure is essential, because the same underlying conduct can fall into penalty brackets that differ by years, or even decades, depending on how the facts are classified.

## The Interest the Law Protects

The core interest protected by Article 103 is the child's sexual inviolability, but the provision reaches further than that single concept. It also protects the child's physical and psychological integrity and the child's right to complete sexual development according to their own natural maturation process. For adults, Turkish law measures sexual offenses against the adult's sexual autonomy — essentially, whether the adult freely consented. For children, that standard is replaced by a developmental one: below a defined age threshold, purported consent by the child carries no legal weight at all. A defendant cannot argue that a child of that age agreed to the conduct, because the law treats such agreement as incapable of curing the offense.

## Who Can Be Held Liable, and Who Is Protected

Any person can commit this offense; it is not restricted to offenders holding a particular status, role, or relationship to the victim. The offender's sex, marital status, or relationship with the victim does not affect whether the offense has occurred, though certain relationships and roles (discussed below) do trigger enhanced penalties. Even a child can be the offender under Article 103, though this has a specific procedural consequence explained further below.

The victim, by contrast, must always be a child, and the law separates children into two groups with different evidentiary requirements:

- **Children under fourteen, and children who have reached fourteen but lack the capacity to understand the legal meaning and consequences of the act:** any sexual conduct directed at a child in this group qualifies as abuse, regardless of whether force, threat, or deception was used.
- **All other children (generally, those who have reached fourteen and have the relevant capacity to understand):** sexual conduct against a child in this group only falls under Article 103 if it was carried out through force, threat, deception, or some other factor that overrode the child's will.

A further distinction applies to children under twelve: this does not create a separate offense, but it raises the minimum sentence within each penalty bracket described below.

## How the Law Grades the Conduct

Article 103 does not enumerate specific acts. Instead, it uses a broad description covering any sexual conduct directed at a child's body, and then divides that conduct into three tiers of severity:

- **Conduct at the level of a fleeting or momentary act ("sarkıntılık"):** sudden, brief, non-continuous sexual conduct. The penalty range is three to eight years' imprisonment; if the victim is under twelve, the minimum cannot be less than five years.
- **Sexual abuse in its basic form:** sexual conduct that goes beyond a fleeting act and shows continuity or intensity. The penalty range is eight to fifteen years' imprisonment; if the victim is under twelve, the minimum cannot be less than ten years.
- **Abuse involving penetration of the body by an organ or any other object:** the most serious form of the offense. The penalty cannot be less than sixteen years' imprisonment; if the victim is under twelve, the minimum cannot be less than eighteen years.

The offense is complete once the described conduct occurs; Turkish law does not require any additional harmful outcome for the basic offense to exist. However, a separate, further-aggravated form applies where the victim is left in a vegetative state or dies as a result: in that case, the law imposes aggravated life imprisonment.

## Fault Requirement and the Constitutional Court's 2016 Ruling

The offense can only be committed intentionally; negligent conduct does not fall under Article 103. The offender must be aware both that the victim is a child and that the conduct in question is sexual in nature. A genuine mistake about the victim's age is assessed under the Penal Code's general rules on error, and the prevailing view in Turkish legal scholarship does not require proof that the offender acted for a specific purpose such as sexual gratification — general intent is sufficient, and the sexual character of the conduct is assessed objectively rather than by reference to the offender's subjective motive.

Article 103 in its current form is also the product of a significant constitutional correction. In a decision dated 26 May 2016, Turkey's Constitutional Court annulled part of the article's first paragraph, finding that applying a single, undifferentiated penalty range to a very wide age band of children — children whose maturity and vulnerability could differ substantially — raised proportionality concerns. Following that ruling, the legislature restructured the provision, introducing the separate fleeting-act ("sarkıntılık") category and the age-twelve threshold for minimum sentences described above. Any analysis of Article 103 needs to account for both the scope of that annulment and the subsequent legislative amendments that followed it.

## Relationship to Consensual Sexual Relations With a Minor (Article 104)

Article 104 of the Penal Code addresses a related but distinct scenario: sexual relations with a minor who has reached the age of fifteen, carried out without force, threat, or deception. Because Article 103 requires one of those coercive elements once the child has reached that age and has the relevant capacity, consensual conduct with an older minor instead falls under Article 104, which carries a considerably lower penalty range of two to five years' imprisonment and is, as a rule, prosecuted only upon complaint by the victim or their legal representative. That lighter regime does not apply, however, where the offender is someone legally barred from marrying the victim (such as certain relatives) or where the offender has assumed a caregiving, guardianship, or foster-family role toward the child — in those situations, the complaint requirement falls away and the penalty rises sharply to a range of ten to fifteen years' imprisonment.

## Aggravating Factors

Article 103's third paragraph raises the penalty by half where the offense is committed under any of the following circumstances: by more than one person acting together; by exploiting an environment where people are compelled to live in shared, communal conditions; by a relative by blood or marriage up to the third degree, or by a stepfather, stepmother, stepsibling, or adoptive parent; by a guardian, teacher, instructor, caregiver, foster family member, or healthcare provider, or by anyone else with a duty of protection, care, or supervision over the child; or through abuse of the influence conferred by a public office or an employment relationship.

A separate aggravating rule in the fourth paragraph applies specifically to force and weapons: the penalty is likewise increased by half where abuse of a younger child (the group requiring no coercive element) is carried out through force or threat, or where abuse of an older child is carried out through the use of a weapon. Where the force or violence used to commit the abuse causes the more serious consequences associated with intentional injury, the provisions on intentional injury apply in addition.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does it matter whether the child appeared to consent?**
For children below the law's protective age threshold — under fourteen, or fourteen and above without the capacity to understand the act's legal meaning — no, apparent consent does not affect criminal liability. For older children with the relevant capacity, the offense under Article 103 requires force, threat, deception, or another factor overriding the child's will; absent any of those elements, the conduct may instead fall under the separate, less severe offense in Article 104.

**Is a fleeting or one-time touch treated the same as sustained abuse?**
No. Turkish case law distinguishes conduct that is sudden, brief, and non-continuous — punished as a fleeting act with a three-to-eight-year range — from conduct that shows continuity or greater intensity, which is punished as basic abuse with an eight-to-fifteen-year range. Whether particular conduct crosses that line is a fact-specific determination the courts make case by case.

**Can this offense be prosecuted without a complaint from the victim?**
As a general rule, yes — prosecution proceeds automatically, without requiring a complaint. The single exception concerns a fleeting-level act committed by an offender who is also a child; in that scenario, prosecution requires a complaint from the victim, the victim's parent, or the victim's guardian. Mediation is not available for offenses against sexual inviolability, even where prosecution otherwise depends on a complaint.

**Which court hears these cases?**
Jurisdiction follows the maximum penalty available. Basic abuse and abuse involving penetration are tried before the Heavy Penal Court (ağır ceza mahkemesi), while fleeting-level acts, where the maximum penalty does not exceed ten years, fall to the Court of First Instance for Criminal Matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi).
