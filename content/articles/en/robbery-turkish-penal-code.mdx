Foreign residents, tourists and business owners in Turkey sometimes encounter the term "yağma" in a criminal case file and assume it means simple theft. It does not. Robbery, defined in Article 148 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), is a substantially more serious offense that sits at the intersection of crimes against property and crimes against the person: it requires that the offender use force or threat to compel a victim to hand over property or to stop resisting its seizure. Because the law treats this as an attack on two protected interests at once — ownership and physical or psychological integrity — the penalties are far heavier than those for theft, threat or coercion taken separately, and the procedural consequences, from mandatory prosecution to the trial court's jurisdiction, differ sharply as well.

## A Crime That Attacks Two Interests at Once

Although robbery is classified among Turkey's property offenses, its legal structure is that of a composite crime: the legislature deliberately merged what would otherwise be two separate offenses — theft, and either coercion or threat — into a single, more severely punished offense. The offender's conduct simultaneously violates the victim's ownership or possession of property and the victim's bodily integrity, sexual inviolability, or freedom of will. Article 148(1) captures this dual structure directly: it punishes anyone who, by threatening a person or a close relative with an attack on life, physical or sexual integrity, or with serious harm to their assets, or by using outright force, compels that person to hand over property or to refrain from resisting its seizure. This is why the sentencing range for robbery — six to ten years' imprisonment for the basic form — sits well above what theft or threat would draw on their own; the law is not simply adding two penalties together but recognizing a distinct and more dangerous type of wrongdoing.

## The Coercion Must Be Aimed at Getting the Property, Here and Now

What separates robbery from a coincidental combination of theft and threat is timing and purpose: the force or threat must be applied at the moment of the taking and directed specifically at overcoming the victim's resistance to handing over the property. The offender's goal throughout is to reach the asset itself, not to achieve some unrelated end. Article 148(2) extends this concept beyond the physical handover of an object. It also covers situations where the victim is forced to issue a document that could place themselves or someone else under a debt obligation, to hand over — or not resist the taking of — a document proving an existing debt has been settled, to sign a paper that could later be turned into such a debt instrument, or to destroy — or not resist the destruction of — an existing debt instrument. In other words, robbery is not limited to the physical transfer of movable goods; it also reaches acts that alter a victim's legal and financial position through coerced declarations of will.

## The Legal Presumption of Force: Incapacitating the Victim

Article 148(3) broadens the concept of "force" in an important way: putting the victim into a state where they cannot perceive events or defend themselves, by any means, is itself treated as force for purposes of the robbery offense. If an offender administers a drug or an intoxicating or stupefying substance, gets the victim drunk, or otherwise neutralizes the victim's will through comparable means, the force element of the crime is satisfied even without any classic physical struggle. This provision makes clear that the physical component of robbery cannot be reduced to raw violence — any method that removes the victim's capacity to resist is legally sufficient.

## The Mental Element: Intent to Obtain an Unlawful Benefit

Robbery can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent version of the offense. The offender must knowingly apply force or threat and must be aware that this is what compels the victim to hand over the property or stop resisting its seizure. As with theft, the offender's purpose of obtaining the property unlawfully for themselves or for someone else is an inseparable part of the mental element. Where that purpose is absent — where the offender's aim was something other than acquiring the property — the conduct may fall under a different offense rather than robbery. Robbery is therefore completed not merely by the presence of force or threat, but by the offender's deliberate use of those means to secure an unlawful benefit at the victim's expense.

## Distinguishing Robbery from Theft, Threat and Extortion

The dividing line between robbery and its neighboring offenses comes down to the timing of the coercion and what it is aimed at achieving. In theft, the property is taken without the victim's knowledge or consent and without any resort to force or threat; in robbery, the handover is itself obtained through that force or threat. The line with extortion (blackmail) is finer: in extortion, the offender leverages the threat of a future disclosure or accusation to obtain a benefit, whereas in robbery the threat is immediate and directly aimed at securing instant delivery of the property. As for the relationship with simple threat, Turkish courts pay close attention to the purpose behind the coercion — a threat made to collect a genuine debt is treated very differently from one made to avoid repaying one, and this distinction determines whether the more lenient provision discussed below applies. The consequences go beyond sentence length: the applicable offense also determines whether mediation is available and which court has jurisdiction over the case.

## Aggravated Forms Under Article 149

Article 149 lists a series of circumstances that raise the penalty for robbery to ten to fifteen years' imprisonment: committing the act with a weapon; disguising oneself to avoid recognition; acting together with more than one person; ambushing the victim on a road, or committing the act inside a residence, workplace or their annexes; targeting a victim who cannot defend themselves physically or mentally; drawing on the intimidating power of an actual or perceived criminal organization; acting to benefit a criminal organization; or committing the offense at night. What these circumstances have in common is that they each significantly reduce the victim's realistic ability to resist compared with an ordinary robbery. Article 149(2) separately confirms that where the aggravated consequences of intentional injury occur during a robbery, the provisions on intentional injury apply in addition — the two offenses are punished cumulatively rather than one absorbing the other.

## The Mitigating Provision and Reduction for Minor Value

Article 150 carves out an important exception. Under Article 150(1), where a person uses threat or force to collect a debt genuinely owed to them under an existing legal relationship, only the provisions on threat or intentional injury apply — not the much harsher robbery provisions. This reflects the idea that someone forcibly collecting a real, legally valid claim is not pursuing an unlawful gain in the same sense as a robber, even though the method used is unlawful. For this exception to apply, the underlying claim must be genuine and legally grounded; coercion based on a purely fictitious or unsupported claim still amounts to robbery. Article 150(2) provides a separate, discretionary reduction: where the property taken is of minor value, the sentence may be reduced by between one-third and one-half, at the trial court's discretion based on the specifics of the case.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence with Other Offenses

Because its execution can be broken down into distinct stages, robbery is capable of being attempted: if the offender begins using force or threat but fails to actually obtain the property, the offense remains at the attempt stage. If the offender voluntarily abandons the use of force or threat before seizing the property, the general rules on voluntary abandonment may apply to whatever conduct occurred up to that point. Where more than one person commits the act together, this can trigger both the general rules on participation and the aggravated form under Article 149(1)(c). On the question of concurrence, if the aggravated consequences of intentional injury occur during the robbery, Article 149(2) requires that the injury provisions be applied separately; if the outcome is even more severe — such as the victim's death — the offense is treated as being in genuine concurrence with intentional homicide.

## Penalties and Procedure

Basic robbery carries a sentence of six to ten years' imprisonment, while the aggravated forms under Article 149 carry ten to fifteen years. Because robbery is considered a serious offense affecting public order and personal safety, not just private property interests, its investigation and prosecution proceed automatically rather than depending on a victim's complaint, and the offense falls outside the scope of mediation — a victim's withdrawal of a complaint, or even a settlement between the parties, does not stop the prosecution from continuing. Given the severity of the potential sentence, robbery cases are tried before the Heavy Penal Court (Ağır Ceza Mahkemesi).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the main difference between robbery and theft under Turkish law?**
Theft involves taking property without the victim's knowledge or consent and without force or threat. Robbery requires that the property be handed over, or its taking not resisted, specifically because of force or threat applied at that moment.

**Can drugging or intoxicating someone count as "force" for robbery purposes?**
Yes. Article 148(3) treats putting a victim into a state where they cannot perceive events or defend themselves — through drugs, alcohol or similar means — as force, even without any physical struggle.

**If I use threats to collect a debt someone genuinely owes me, is that robbery?**
Not necessarily. Article 150(1) provides that force or threat used to collect a real, legally grounded debt is punished under the threat or intentional injury provisions rather than as robbery. This only applies where the underlying claim is genuine; a fabricated claim does not qualify.

**Can a robbery charge be resolved through mediation or withdrawn by the victim's complaint?**
No. Robbery is prosecuted automatically and is excluded from mediation. Neither the victim dropping the complaint nor a private settlement between the parties will stop the criminal proceedings.
