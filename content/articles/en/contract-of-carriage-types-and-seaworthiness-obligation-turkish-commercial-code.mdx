TTK Art. 1138-1141 open the Code's chapter on contracts of carriage by sea — arguably the most litigated part of Turkish maritime law.

## Two Contract Types

Under Art. 1138, a carrier undertakes carriage by sea in one of two structures: a voyage charter, where the carrier allocates the whole ship, part of it, or a specific space to the shipper; or a general cargo contract, where the carrier undertakes to carry specifically identified goods without any space being allocated — closer to modern less-than-container-load carriage. The distinction matters because loading time, demurrage, and termination rules operate differently for each. Art. 1139 lets either party demand a written voyage charter party at its own cost, and Art. 1140 clarifies that chartering the "whole ship" excludes cabins unless the shipper consents to their use for cargo.

## Seaworthiness: The Chapter's Linchpin

Art. 1141 is the central provision of the entire chapter: in *every* contract of carriage, the carrier must keep the ship seaworthy, voyage-worthy, and cargoworthy. The carrier is liable to cargo interests for damage from any failure in this regard — unless it proves that, despite exercising a diligent carrier's care, the defect could not have been discovered before the voyage began. This closely tracks the international "due diligence" standard familiar from the Hague-Visby Rules, and Turkish courts read the carrier's escape route narrowly: a mandatory safety device (like a bridge alarm system) not working at the time of an accident defeats the seaworthiness defense even if the failure is later characterized as a "technical" crew error, because the device's operability is itself part of seaworthiness.

## Practical Takeaway

For cargo owners and insurers, whether a loss traces to *initial* unseaworthiness (unlimited liability, including lost profit) or to an in-voyage technical fault (typically limited liability) is usually the decisive, threshold question in a Turkish cargo damage case — and Turkish courts have extended "seaworthiness" even to defects in carrier-supplied containers, treating a faulty container gasket as unseaworthiness of the ship itself.
