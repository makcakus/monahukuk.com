Foreign investors and expatriates involved in Turkish criminal proceedings sometimes encounter a charge that has no direct equivalent in many other legal systems: founding, leading, or joining an "organization established for the purpose of committing crime" under Article 220 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK). Unlike ordinary complicity rules, which punish people for the crimes they actually help commit, Article 220 punishes the act of organizing itself — before any underlying offense has necessarily taken place. Because the threshold for what counts as a punishable "organization" is defined by a specific set of cumulative criteria rather than a vague standard, understanding those criteria is essential for anyone facing, or advising on, such a charge in Turkey.

## What Article 220 Protects and Why It Exists

Article 220 does not protect a particular victim's rights; it protects public order and public safety as collective interests. The legislature's premise is that once three or more people organize themselves around a shared criminal purpose, in a structure with internal ranking and staying power, that structure is itself dangerous to society — independently of whether any planned offense is ever carried out. For this reason, Turkish criminal doctrine generally classifies the offense of founding or leading an organization as an abstract-danger crime: prosecutors do not need to prove that the organization actually committed a crime, only that it was organized in a way genuinely capable of carrying out its criminal aims.

Article 220 is also a residual, general-purpose provision. Where the Penal Code already contains a specialized organizational offense for a particular category of crime — for example, organizations formed to commit genocide or crimes against humanity, or armed terrorist organizations under separate provisions — that specialized provision takes precedence, and Article 220 steps aside. Article 220 applies only where no such dedicated organizational offense exists for the underlying criminal purpose.

## The Three Cumulative Criteria for "Organization" Status

Not every group of people who cooperate in crime qualifies as an "organization" for purposes of Article 220. Turkish law and settled case law require three elements to be present together:

1. **A minimum headcount.** The law fixes a numerical floor: an organization within the meaning of Article 220 cannot exist with fewer than three participants. Two people acting together, however serious their shared purpose, fall outside Article 220 entirely — their conduct is assessed instead under the Penal Code's general rules on complicity.
2. **Hierarchy and continuity.** There must be some structure of authority among participants — even a loose chain of command — together with a division of roles and a bond that persists over time. A single, spontaneous joint act does not create an organization; continuity is what separates organized crime from ordinary complicity in a one-off offense.
3. **Operational capacity.** The group's structure, membership, and access to tools or resources must actually be sufficient to carry out the crimes it is organized around. This is assessed case by case: intent alone, or a loose association without the practical means to act, does not satisfy this requirement. Turkish courts have made clear that repeated contact or communication between individuals, standing alone, is not enough to establish this capacity — it must be backed by concrete indicators of an ability to execute the group's aims.

If any one of these three elements is missing, Article 220 cannot be applied, and the conduct falls back into the ordinary framework of complicity in the underlying offense.

## Who Can Be Liable, and for What Conduct

Article 220 does not require any special personal status — anyone can be a perpetrator. On the victim side, Turkish doctrine treats the offense as one where society at large, as the bearer of public order, is exposed to the risk of harm; no specific individual is named as the victim.

The provision distinguishes between several forms of culpable conduct, punished at different levels:

- **Founding** the organization — creating its initial structure.
- **Leading** it — directing its activity or giving instructions, whether or not the same person was a founder.
- **Membership** — voluntarily joining and taking a place within the organization's hierarchy, which carries a separate and lower penalty range than founding or leading.

Founding and leading are punished within the same range; ordinary membership is treated as a distinct, less severe form of conduct. Any one of these forms of participation is sufficient on its own — someone who is both a founder and a leader is not punished more severely simply for holding both roles.

## Intent, Aggravating Circumstances and Related Offenses

The offense requires direct intent. The person must know that the structure they are creating, leading, or joining is organized around the aim of committing offenses recognized as crimes under Turkish law, and must want that purpose to be pursued. There is no such thing as negligently founding or negligently joining a criminal organization — the mental element is what separates Article 220 from a merely negligent or coincidental association between people.

Several circumstances increase the applicable penalty or extend liability:

- **Armed organizations.** If the organization is armed, the penalties otherwise applicable are increased by half, provided the weapons belong to the organization and are suited to carrying out its criminal purposes.
- **Use of children.** Where children are used as instruments in offenses committed through the organization's activity, the leadership faces a further increase — from half again up to double the otherwise applicable penalty — reflecting a later legislative amendment targeting the exploitation of minors within organized structures.
- **Cumulative liability for underlying offenses.** Where a crime is actually committed in the course of the organization's activity, the person responsible is punished separately both for the organizational offense and for that underlying crime — a straightforward application of Turkey's general rule against merging distinct offenses into one. Organizational leaders bear an even broader responsibility: they can be held personally liable, as if they were the direct perpetrator, for every offense committed within the organization's activity, even without personally taking part in the physical act.
- **Assistance without hierarchical integration.** A person who knowingly and willingly assists the organization without being formally part of its chain of command is nonetheless punished as if a member, though the penalty may be reduced by up to one-third depending on the nature of the assistance rendered.
- **Propaganda.** Separately, glorifying, legitimizing, or encouraging an organization's violent or coercive methods is itself criminalized, with a heavier penalty where the propaganda is disseminated through the press or media.

## Attempt, Complicity and Voluntary Withdrawal

Because the offense already criminalizes what amounts to preparatory conduct, the classic doctrine of attempt has little practical room to operate here — the organization is considered fully formed, and the offense complete, once it is actually established. On complicity, the legislature has effectively absorbed part of the general complicity framework into Article 220 itself, by making membership and assistance independent, separately punishable forms of conduct rather than leaving them to be assessed solely under general participation rules.

Turkish law also provides a distinct, staged regime of active repentance specific to organizational offenses, set out separately from Article 220 itself:

- Founders or leaders who disband the organization, or whose information enables its disbandment, before any investigation begins and before any offense is committed toward the organization's aims, face no penalty at all for the organizational offense.
- A member who voluntarily leaves the organization without having taken part in any offense committed in its activity, and who reports the departure to the relevant authorities, likewise faces no penalty.
- A member captured without having taken part in any such offense who, out of genuine remorse, provides information capable of leading to the organization's disbandment or the capture of its members also escapes punishment.
- Anyone — founder, leader, member, a person who commits crimes on the organization's behalf without being a member, or a knowing assistant — who voluntarily surrenders and provides information about the organization's structure and activities avoids punishment for the founding, leading or membership offense itself; if the same information is given only after capture, the penalty is instead reduced by between one-third and three-quarters.
- Anyone benefiting from this regime is placed under a period of judicial supervision, ordinarily one year, extendable up to three, and the benefit cannot be granted more than once to the same person.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many people are needed before a group can be charged as a criminal organization under Turkish law?**
At least three. A structure involving only two people, regardless of its purpose, cannot be prosecuted as an organization under Article 220; ordinary complicity rules apply instead.

**Does the organization actually have to commit a crime for the founding or leadership offense to apply?**
No. Because this is treated as an abstract-danger offense, what matters is that the organization's structure, membership and resources make it capable of carrying out its criminal purpose — not that it has already done so. If a crime is committed in the organization's activity, that crime is punished separately and in addition.

**What is the practical difference between being an "organization" and simple complicity in a crime committed by several people?**
The dividing line is hierarchy and continuity. A structure with an internal chain of command, division of roles, and an ongoing (not one-off) criminal purpose is an organization; a single coordinated act among a group of people, without that lasting structure, is assessed under ordinary complicity provisions instead.

**Can someone avoid punishment by cooperating with the authorities after being investigated for this offense?**
Yes, within limits. Turkish law provides a graduated system of active repentance: disbanding the organization or providing decisive information before any investigation begins can eliminate liability entirely, while cooperation given only after capture typically results in a substantial sentence reduction rather than full immunity from punishment.
