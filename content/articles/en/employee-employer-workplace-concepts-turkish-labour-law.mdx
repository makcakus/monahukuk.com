Every protection Turkish labour law offers ultimately depends on how the law classifies the parties involved: is this person an "employee", is the counterparty an "employer", and where does the "workplace" begin and end? Article 2 of Labour Code No. 4857 answers exactly these questions, and it does so with more precision than a casual reading suggests. These are not abstract definitions reserved for academic discussion — they determine who can bring a claim for severance pay or reinstatement, which entity bears joint liability when work is outsourced, and which court has jurisdiction over a given dispute. For foreign investors and HR teams structuring operations in Turkey, particularly where subcontracting or outsourced services are part of the business model, understanding these categories is a precondition to managing legal risk correctly.

## Who Counts as an Employee and an Employer

Article 2 defines an employee as a natural person who works under an employment contract. Three consequences follow from this narrow wording. First, only a natural person can be an employee — a company or other legal entity can never hold employee status, no matter how it is described in a commercial contract. Second, the relationship must rest on an actual employment contract, not simply an arrangement that resembles one. Third, and most importantly in practice, the work must be performed in a position of dependence: the person carries out tasks under the employer's instructions, within a schedule and organisational structure the employer sets. This element of subordination is what distinguishes an employee from an independent contractor or freelance professional working under a service or agency-type arrangement — a distinction that becomes critical whenever a business tries to structure what is functionally an employment relationship as a commercial services contract to avoid labour obligations.

The employer, by contrast, is defined broadly: any natural or legal person, or any entity or organisation without legal personality, that engages an employee. This sweeps in public bodies, associations, foundations, and ordinary partnerships, not only commercial companies. The bond between the two is what the law calls the "employment relationship."

Article 2 also carves out a distinct category: the **employer's representative** — anyone who acts on the employer's behalf and takes part in managing the work, the workplace or the business. Crucially, the employer itself remains directly liable for how the representative deals with employees; the representative's actions and omissions toward staff are treated as the employer's own. At the same time, holding this representative status does not strip away the rights an employer's representative may separately hold as an employee. A branch manager or HR director, for instance, exercises the employer's authority in one capacity while remaining, in respect of their own employment contract, an employee entitled to the same statutory protections as anyone else on the payroll.

## What Constitutes a Workplace

The law defines a workplace not as a physical address but as the organisational unit in which the employer brings together tangible and intangible elements with employees for the purpose of producing goods or services. This functional definition matters because it deliberately reaches beyond the factory floor or office building. Article 2 extends the concept to cover **auxiliary premises** — locations that are functionally connected to the core production activity and organised under the same management — as well as ancillary facilities and equipment such as rest areas, nursing rooms, dining and sleeping facilities, washing and medical-care areas, vocational training spaces and courtyards.

The workplace, its auxiliary premises, and its ancillary facilities and equipment together form a single, integrated business organisation. This principle of organisational unity is not a theoretical nicety: it drives the outcome in workplace transfer disputes, trade union authorisation at a given workplace, and mass layoff procedures, among other institutions. Correctly identifying the boundaries of a workplace also matters for determining which court has territorial jurisdiction, since procedural rules in labour litigation are frequently tied to the location where the work was actually performed.

## The Main Employer-Subcontractor Relationship

Paragraph six of Article 2 addresses a structure that has become widespread in modern business: subcontracting. A main employer-subcontractor relationship arises when one employer takes on, from another employer, an auxiliary task connected to that employer's production of goods or services at its workplace, or takes on part of the core work itself where doing so requires specialised expertise for operational or technological reasons — and then deploys its own employees exclusively on that assigned work, at that same workplace.

Two conditions must be satisfied together for such an arrangement to be valid: the work handed to the subcontractor must either be auxiliary in nature, or, if it is part of the core work, its transfer must be justified by genuine operational or technological necessity requiring specialisation. Outside these narrow exceptions, the core work cannot lawfully be split off and handed to subcontractors. Where a valid relationship exists, the main employer becomes jointly liable, alongside the subcontractor, for the subcontractor's obligations toward its employees at that workplace — obligations arising from the Labour Code itself, from the individual employment contract, or from any collective bargaining agreement the subcontractor is party to. This joint liability exists precisely to make sure that employees of a subcontractor are not left without a solvent party to answer for their statutory and contractual rights.

## The Prohibition on Sham Subcontracting (Muvazaa)

Because outsourcing arrangements can easily be misused to strip employees of protections, Article 2 imposes an explicit prohibition on sham, or collusive, subcontracting arrangements (known in Turkish practice as *muvazaa*). Two specific abuses are barred: an employer cannot restrict its employees' existing rights by having them formally rehired by a subcontractor to continue performing the same work, and a subcontractor relationship cannot be established with respect to someone who was previously employed directly at that same workplace. Where either of these patterns is found, or more generally wherever the relationship is found to rest on a collusive arrangement, the subcontractor's employees are deemed, retroactively from the very start of their employment, to be employees of the main employer.

The Court of Cassation's 9th Civil Chamber has developed a consistent line of case law on how this is assessed. For a main employer-subcontractor relationship to be treated as genuine, there must first be two truly independent employers — the subcontractor must have its own organisation, its own expertise, and must bear its own entrepreneurial risk. Where the work is instead performed under the main employer's direct supervision and instructions, using the main employer's own tools and equipment, this is treated as a strong indicator that the arrangement is collusive. Once collusion is established, the affected employee can bring a reinstatement claim directly against the main employer, and seniority is calculated from the original start date as if the employee had always worked for the main employer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a company or other legal entity be an "employee" under Turkish law?**
No. Article 2 restricts employee status to natural persons who work under an employment contract. A corporate service provider, however the underlying arrangement is labelled, cannot hold employee status.

**Does calling someone an "independent contractor" prevent them from being reclassified as an employee?**
Not by itself. Courts look at whether the person actually works under the employer's instructions, schedule and organisational structure. If that dependence is present in practice, the contractual label given to the relationship will not prevent a court from treating the person as an employee.

**When is outsourcing part of the core business lawful, and when is it a sham?**
Outsourcing part of the core work is lawful only where genuine operational or technological reasons require specialised expertise, and where the subcontractor operates with its own organisation and bears its own business risk. If the subcontractor's employees work under the main employer's direct supervision with the main employer's equipment, or if employees are simply shifted onto a subcontractor's payroll to continue the same work, the arrangement risks being treated as collusive.

**What happens if a subcontractor relationship is found to be collusive?**
The subcontractor's employees are deemed to have been employees of the main employer from the very beginning of the relationship. They may bring claims, including reinstatement claims, directly against the main employer, and their seniority is calculated as if they had always been employed there.
