Criminal proceedings in Turkey are conducted in the name of the public, prosecuted by a public prosecutor rather than by the injured party. That structure often confuses foreign nationals who are victims of a crime in Turkey, or whose family member has been harmed, because it differs sharply from adversarial systems where a victim's lawyer can directly drive a private prosecution. The Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (Ceza Muhakemesi Kanunu, "CMK") nonetheless gives the person harmed by an offense a defined role: first as a victim or complainant who must be notified and heard, and later — if a formal request is made — as an "intervening party" (katılan) who becomes an active party alongside the prosecutor. Understanding the difference between these statuses, and the deadlines attached to each, is essential for anyone navigating a Turkish criminal case as a complainant.

## Victim, Complainant and Intervening Party Are Not the Same Thing

Turkish criminal procedure distinguishes three related but legally separate positions. The "victim" (mağdur) is the person directly harmed by the conduct that the law criminalizes. The "complainant" (şikâyetçi) is someone who has formally expressed the will to see the offense prosecuted, which matters chiefly in offenses that can only be prosecuted upon a private complaint — and while the victim and the complainant are frequently the same individual, that is not always the case. Neither status, on its own, makes a person a party to the case: victims and complainants must be summoned and heard, but the prosecution is carried out by the public prosecutor regardless of their involvement. Becoming an actual party to the proceedings — with the ability to request evidence, question witnesses, and later appeal the judgment — requires a separate, affirmative step: applying to "intervene" in the public case (kamu davasına katılma). This distinction between being informed of a case and being a party to it is the single most important concept for a foreign complainant to grasp early on.

## Notification of the Victim and Complainant

Under CMK Article 233, the public prosecutor, the presiding judge, or the judge handling the case must summon the victim and the complainant by formal notice so that they can be heard. Once the case moves into the trial phase, a copy of the indictment is attached to that summons. Where the file contains contact details such as a phone number, fax number, or email address, the law also allows the essential content of the indictment and the trial date to be communicated through those channels — a practical safeguard meant to ensure the victim is not simply left out of a process that concerns them, particularly relevant for foreign victims who may be abroad or unfamiliar with Turkish postal procedures. Because Article 233 also applies the procedural rules used for witnesses to how victims and complainants are summoned and, if necessary, compelled to appear, the same mechanics used to bring a witness to court can be used to bring in a victim or complainant. This does not turn the victim into a witness for evidentiary purposes; it merely borrows the same administrative procedure for getting them before the court.

## Rights During the Investigation and Trial Stages

CMK Article 234 sets out a structured list of entitlements for victims and complainants, split between the investigation stage and the trial stage. During the investigation, they may request that evidence be gathered, ask the prosecutor for copies of documents (subject to the confidentiality and purpose of the investigation), and — for a defined set of serious offenses, such as sexual assault, sexual abuse of children, persistent stalking, or intentional injury, torture or ill-treatment committed against a woman, as well as any offense carrying a minimum sentence above five years — request that the bar association assign them a lawyer free of charge if they do not already have one. They may also object, through the procedure the law prescribes, to a prosecutor's decision not to prosecute. During the trial stage, their rights include being informed of hearing dates, applying to intervene in the case, obtaining copies of the case record, requesting that witnesses be summoned, the same right to a bar-appointed lawyer in the serious-offense categories described above, and — importantly, only once they have actually become an intervening party — the right to appeal decisions that conclude the case. Where the victim is a minor, is deaf or mute, or is otherwise unable to adequately express themselves, and has no lawyer of their own, the law requires a lawyer to be assigned automatically, without any request being necessary; this converts what is otherwise a right that must be actively claimed into an automatic safeguard for particularly vulnerable individuals. Authorities are also obliged to explain these rights to the victim or complainant and record that explanation in the file — an omission that can become grounds for challenging the proceedings later.

## Applying to Intervene in the Public Case

CMK Article 237 identifies three categories of people who may apply to intervene in a criminal case: the victim, natural or legal persons who suffered harm from the offense, and persons who bear financial liability in connection with it. It is worth stressing that "victim" and "person harmed by the offense" do not always overlap — in offenses aimed more broadly at society at large, a specific individual may not qualify as directly harmed, in which case that person also has no right to intervene. A request to intervene may be filed at any stage of the trial before the first-instance court, right up until judgment is rendered, but it cannot be raised for the first time at the appellate stage; the only exception is where a request was already made and either rejected or left undecided at first instance, and the appeal explicitly raises that earlier request. The court is required to actually rule on any intervention request rather than let it go unaddressed, since silence on the point can undermine both the efficient conduct of the trial and the applicant's later right to appeal. Once an intervention request is accepted, the applicant acquires the status of "intervening party" (katılan) and becomes a party to the case alongside the public prosecutor, with a distinct set of procedural powers. Evidence gathered earlier, during the investigation phase, remains open to review and objection by the intervening party and their lawyer even after intervention is granted.

## What Changes Once a Person Becomes an Intervening Party

Acquiring intervening-party status transforms a passive victim into an active participant in the trial. An intervening party may request that evidence be collected, put questions to witnesses, make statements during hearings and — critically, only because they have intervened — appeal the judgment, whether through an ordinary appeal or a further appeal to the Court of Cassation. This last power matters enormously in practice: a victim who never applied to intervene, or whose application was not granted, cannot challenge an acquittal or a judgment they consider legally deficient. For this reason, filing a timely and procedurally correct intervention request effectively determines whether a victim retains any voice once the trial concludes. A decision rejecting an intervention request can itself be challenged, and the court must give reasons for that decision in the case file.

CMK Article 239 adds a further, separate right once intervening-party status has been obtained: in the same category of serious offenses referenced above, the intervening party may request a bar-appointed lawyer free of charge, independent of the similar right available at the investigation and trial stages under Article 234. Where the victim or the person harmed is a child, is deaf or mute, or suffers from a mental illness that prevents self-defense, this lawyer is assigned automatically, without any request being necessary. The lawyer's role extends well beyond appearing in court: reviewing the file, requesting evidence, attending hearings, and pursuing an appeal can all be exercised by the lawyer on the client's behalf. In practice, this raises a genuinely contested question for child victims — Turkish courts have held that the decision to intervene belongs to the child's legal representative, not to a lawyer acting for the child directly, and that a lawyer cannot independently trigger intervention on the child's behalf without that representative's consent.

## When Intervening-Party Status Comes to an End

Intervening-party status is not permanent. CMK Article 243 recognizes two ways it ends: the intervening party's own decision to withdraw, or their death. A withdrawal ends that person's status as a party but has no effect on the underlying prosecution, which continues in the prosecutor's hands regardless. Death produces the same result for the deceased, but the law gives the heirs an option: they may apply to intervene in their own right, in order to pursue the rights the deceased held. This matters particularly in homicide cases, where it recognizes that the harm caused by certain offenses extends to the victim's family, not only to the individual who died. Ending intervening-party status does not retroactively undo procedural steps already taken while the person held that status — it only ends their standing as a party going forward.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Am I automatically a party to a criminal case in Turkey if I am the victim?**
No. Being a victim or complainant only entitles you to be notified and heard. To become an actual party with the right to request evidence, question witnesses, or later appeal, you must file a separate application to intervene in the case before judgment is rendered.

**Can I request a free lawyer if I cannot afford one?**
Yes, in specific circumstances. For a defined list of serious offenses — including sexual offenses, persistent stalking, and violence against women — a victim or intervening party without their own lawyer may request that the bar association assign one free of charge. For particularly vulnerable victims (minors, or those unable to express themselves), this lawyer is assigned automatically, without a request.

**What happens to my case if I withdraw as an intervening party or pass away?**
Your withdrawal ends only your own status as a party; the public prosecution itself continues unaffected. If you pass away, your heirs may apply to intervene in your place to pursue the rights you held, which is particularly relevant in cases involving a family member's death.

**If I never applied to intervene, can I still appeal an acquittal I disagree with?**
No. The right to appeal a judgment belongs only to someone who has actually been granted intervening-party status. A victim or complainant who never applied, or whose application was rejected, has no standing to challenge the outcome.
