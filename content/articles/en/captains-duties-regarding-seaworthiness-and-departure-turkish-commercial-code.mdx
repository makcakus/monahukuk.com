TTK Art. 1090-1095 spell out the concrete, preventive duties that flow from the captain's general duty of care — the checks and choices a captain must make before and while a ship gets underway.

## Checking the Ship Before Sailing

Under Art. 1090, before departure the captain must confirm the ship is seaworthy and voyage-worthy, and that the ship's, crew's, and cargo's documents are actually on board — not just technical readiness, but administrative compliance too. Art. 1091 extends this to loading: the captain must ensure the ship is not overloaded, carries adequate ballast, and has holds fit to receive the cargo — and critically, this duty survives even when stevedoring is contracted out to third parties. A FIOS clause (shifting loading/stowage cost to the charterer) does not relieve the captain of the oversight role; Turkish courts have confirmed this repeatedly in cargo damage litigation.

## Compliance Abroad and Departure Discipline

Art. 1092 makes the captain personally liable for damages arising from failure to observe a foreign port's police, tax, and customs rules — and separately liable for knowingly loading war-contraband goods. Art. 1093 requires departing at the first suitable opportunity once ready; even personal incapacity (illness) does not excuse delay — the captain must either get instructions from the shipowner or appoint a substitute, and is only liable for that substitute's acts if the selection itself was negligent.

## Staying Aboard and the Limits of Consultation

Art. 1094 requires the captain (or the first mate) to remain aboard during loading/unloading and, more strictly, whenever the ship is in an unsafe anchorage or at sea. Art. 1095 confirms that consulting ship's officers in an emergency is good practice but never shifts responsibility: the captain is not bound by their advice and remains solely accountable for whatever measures are taken.

## Practical Takeaway

For shipowners and charterers dealing with Turkish-flagged vessels, these provisions confirm that operational discipline — documentation, loading oversight, departure timing, on-board presence — sits squarely on the captain as a matter of personal law, independent of any contractual allocation of stevedoring or logistics tasks to third parties.

One Turkish Court of Cassation decision applying the loading-oversight rule held that a FIOS clause does not discharge the captain's duty to supervise stowage for the ship's stability and safety.
