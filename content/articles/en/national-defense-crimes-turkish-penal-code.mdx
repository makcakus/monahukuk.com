Foreign counsel encountering a Turkish criminal file that references "crimes against national defense" are usually dealing with one of nine distinct offences grouped together in Articles 317 through 325 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"). These provisions sit within the Code's chapter on crimes against the nation and the state, and their unifying purpose is to protect the integrity of military command, the proper functioning of compulsory military service, and the state's capacity to defend itself, particularly once war or general mobilization has been declared. Several of these articles only become operative in wartime or during a declared mobilization, which explains why reported case law on them is sparse outside a handful of peacetime-applicable provisions. This article walks through each offence, the interest it protects, and the procedural features that distinguish this group of crimes from ordinary criminal charges.

## The Structure of the Chapter and What It Protects

The chapter divides naturally into two groups. Four articles — usurping military command, discouraging the public from military service, inciting soldiers to disobedience, and enlisting in or arranging enlistment into a foreign armed force — can be committed in peacetime and therefore generate the bulk of any practical case law. A further four articles are conditioned on an actual state of war or a declared mobilization: disobeying orders in wartime, failing to perform wartime duties, spreading false news during war, and neglecting mobilization-related duties. Because Turkey has not been in a formally declared state of war or general mobilization in the modern period, these four provisions have almost no reported judicial application — a gap that reflects the narrow scope of the rules themselves rather than any uncertainty in how they are interpreted. A ninth article, addressing acceptance of a title or honor from an enemy state, stands somewhat apart from both groups. Across the chapter, the shared protected interest is the state's national defense capacity and the military chain of command that sustains it; in the provisions on discouraging military service and inciting disobedience, public confidence in the institution of military service and internal military discipline are also protected. Most of these offences are classified as abstract endangerment crimes against state security — liability does not depend on proof that any concrete harm actually materialized, only that the conduct was capable of endangering the defense order the law protects.

## Usurping Military Command (Article 317)

This offence penalizes a person who takes actual control of a military command — over a unit, fleet, warship, air squadron, fortress, military base or installation, or a port or city under military administration — without having been legally authorized or officially appointed to that role. The same penalty applies to someone who did hold a command lawfully but refuses to relinquish it when ordered to do so by the competent authority. What distinguishes this offence from the general rules on usurping a public function is that it targets the military chain of command specifically, rather than public office generally: the victim of the offence is not an individual but the state's military command structure itself. The offence requires intent — the person must know they lack authority and act deliberately regardless. Notably, the law treats the unauthorized seizure of command and the unlawful refusal to give it up as equally serious, both carrying the heaviest sanction the chapter provides, aggravated life imprisonment.

## Discouraging the Public from Military Service (Article 318)

Anyone can commit this offence; the conduct consists of statements or actions, made through publication or of a propagandizing character, that are capable of discouraging the public from performing military service. The hardest line to draw under this article is between legitimate criticism of the military service system — which remains within the bounds of freedom of expression — and propaganda genuinely aimed at deterring people from serving. Courts are expected to weigh the speaker's intent, the context in which the statement was made, its intended audience, and its capacity to spread, since identical wording can be assessed differently depending on the channel through which it was expressed. This is the most heavily litigated point in the surrounding academic and judicial discussion. The basic offence carries a sentence of six months to two years' imprisonment, increased by one half when committed through the press or broadcast media.

## Inciting Soldiers to Disobedience (Article 319)

Here the protected interest is military discipline itself. The conduct consists of inciting soldiers to disobey their superiors or to resist orders. Completion of the offence does not require that any soldier actually follow through and disobey — the incitement itself is enough, which again marks this out as an abstract endangerment offence. No special status is required of the offender; civilians can commit this crime just as soldiers can. The base penalty is one to three years' imprisonment, rising to two to five years if committed publicly, and doubled if committed in wartime.

## Enlisting in, or Arranging Enlistment into, Foreign Armed Forces (Article 320)

This article punishes two related but distinct forms of conduct: arranging for other people to join the armed forces of a foreign state without authorization, and a person enlisting themselves in such a force. The underlying concern is preventing a citizen's defense obligations toward Turkey from being diverted to serve a foreign power. The offence therefore appears in two forms — the person who recruits others, and the person who enlists on their own account — and the law treats these with different severity. What matters for liability is simply the absence of official authorization; whether the foreign state in question is friendly or hostile to Turkey does not, by itself, change the analysis. A person who recruits others into foreign service or arms citizens for that purpose faces three to six years' imprisonment, while a person who personally accepts such service faces one to three years; if any of those enlisted or armed are of military-service age, the sentence is increased by one third.

## Wartime-Specific Duty Violations (Articles 321-324)

The four remaining provisions — disobeying orders in wartime (Article 321), failing to fulfil wartime duties (Article 322), spreading false news during war (Article 323), and neglecting mobilization-related duties (Article 324) — all share the common feature that an actual state of war or declared mobilization is a precondition for liability. The offender is typically someone holding a defined duty or role — a soldier, civil defense official, or competent authority — making these largely offences that can only be committed by a person in that specific position. Article 323 in particular addresses the risk that spreading false or exaggerated news creates for public order during wartime and its capacity to weaken the population's resolve; the interest protected there extends beyond military discipline to the integrity of public information during a national emergency. Because application of these four articles depends on Turkey formally being in a state of war or mobilization, reported case law addressing them in peacetime is effectively nonexistent — a consequence of how narrowly they are drafted, not a sign of legal uncertainty.

## Accepting a Title or Honor from an Enemy State (Article 325)

This offence sanctions a Turkish citizen who accepts a title, decoration, or similar honor from an enemy state in a manner contrary to the interests of the state. What makes this offence distinct is that it targets a breach of the loyalty a citizen owes by virtue of citizenship itself; the offence is complete once the honor has actually been accepted, and no further showing of resulting harm is required.

## Common Features, Concurrence and Procedure

Every offence in this chapter requires intent; none of them can be committed negligently. Attempt is possible wherever the conduct can be broken down into distinct executory steps — this is particularly relevant for the gradual, staged conduct typical of Articles 317 and 320. General rules on participation apply, though the line between the principal offender and an inciter requires careful attention in Articles 318 and 319, given that incitement and propaganda are themselves the core conduct being punished. Where the same act also satisfies the elements of another offence — misuse of public office or espionage, for example — whether the more serious offence absorbs the conduct or the rules on concurrent offences apply instead depends on the specifics of the case. Prosecution proceeds ex officio; none of these offences require a private complaint to be pursued. The offender's status is central to determining which court has jurisdiction, and where the conduct occurred in connection with a military duty, the applicable procedural framework is examined separately. Because sentencing varies according to each article's paragraph structure and any aggravating circumstances, the actual penalty in a given case must be worked out directly from the statutory text.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a civilian be prosecuted under these provisions, or do they only apply to military personnel?**
Most of the offences in this chapter, including discouraging military service (Article 318) and inciting disobedience (Article 319), can be committed by anyone, civilian or military. Some of the wartime-specific provisions (Articles 321-324), however, presuppose that the offender holds a specific duty or role, which in practice limits who can be charged.

**Does criticizing Turkey's military service system amount to a crime?**
Not on its own. Article 318 targets propaganda genuinely capable of discouraging the public from performing military service, not criticism that remains within the bounds of legitimate expression. Courts examine the intent behind the statement, its context, its audience, and its capacity to spread before classifying it one way or the other.

**Do the wartime-specific articles (321-324) currently have any practical application in Turkey?**
Their application is conditioned on an actual, formally declared state of war or general mobilization. In the absence of such a declaration, these provisions remain dormant, which is why reported case law addressing them is essentially nonexistent — not because of any ambiguity in the rules, but because their triggering condition has not arisen.

**Is it relevant whether the foreign state involved under Article 320 is friendly or hostile to Turkey?**
No. What matters for that offence is the absence of official authorization for the enlistment, not the diplomatic relationship between Turkey and the foreign state in question.
