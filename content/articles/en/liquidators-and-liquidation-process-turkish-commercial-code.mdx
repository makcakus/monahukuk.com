Once a Turkish joint-stock company dissolves, someone must wind it down. TTK Art. 536-544 govern who that is and how the process runs.

## Appointment and Removal of Liquidators

By default, the board itself acts as liquidator unless the articles or the general assembly appoint dedicated liquidators, who may be shareholders or third parties (Art. 536). Courts appoint the liquidator where dissolution was ordered by a court. At least one liquidator with signing authority must be a Turkish citizen resident in Turkey. The general assembly can remove and replace liquidators at any time without cause; a court can only do so on a shareholder's request and only for just cause (Art. 537) — courts have found that conduct compromising a liquidator's neutrality (such as taking sides in a shareholder dispute) qualifies, but liability for pre-appointment conduct does not.

## Selling Company Assets and Third-Party Protection

Liquidators may sell company assets by private negotiation by default; selling a significant portion of assets in bulk requires a qualified-majority general assembly resolution (Art. 538). Transactions liquidators make with third parties outside the liquidation purpose still bind the company unless the third party's bad faith is proven — mere registration of the liquidation is not, by itself, sufficient proof of that bad faith (Art. 539).

## Creditor Protection Is Central to the Process

Liquidators must first draw up an inventory and balance sheet reflecting the company's position at the start of liquidation (Art. 540). Known creditors get notice by registered letter; unknown creditors are called through three weekly announcements in the Trade Registry Gazette and the company website (Art. 541). If the company's debts exceed its assets, liquidators must immediately notify the commercial court, which can convert the process into bankruptcy (Art. 542) — Turkish courts have treated selective payment to one creditor, made without first verifying solvency, as grounds for removing a liquidator.

## Distribution and Record-Keeping

Remaining assets, after debts are paid, go to shareholders proportional to their paid-in capital — but not before three months have passed since the third creditor notice, absent court permission (Art. 543). Company books must then be retained under the general ten-year rule (Art. 544).
