Foreign residents, expats and international counsel dealing with a Turkish criminal file sometimes encounter two little-known offenses tucked into the "Crimes Against Public Health" chapter of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"): violating a quarantine measure under Article 195, and burying a body outside a designated cemetery under Article 196. Neither offense requires proof that anyone was actually harmed. Both are built around a breach of administrative rules — a quarantine order issued by a public health authority, or a burial-location rule set by cemetery regulations — and both can result in a prison sentence even where the defendant acted without any intent to endanger others. Understanding how narrowly each provision is framed, and what the prosecution must actually prove, is essential for anyone facing an investigation or indictment under these articles.

## The Shared Logic: Protecting Public Order, Not a Specific Victim

Articles 195 and 196 do not protect an individual victim in the way that, say, an assault or theft provision does. Instead, they protect a collective interest: the public's protection from contagious disease, and society's expectation that the dead will be treated with the dignity administrative regulations require. Because of this, prosecutions under these articles proceed automatically (ex officio) rather than depending on a private complaint, and the "victim" is best understood as the community rather than any single person. A second shared feature is that both provisions are what Turkish doctrine calls framework norms: the criminal liability itself is anchored to a separate administrative act or regulation — a quarantine decision under public health law, or a rule about where burials may lawfully take place. If that underlying administrative measure does not exist, was never properly issued, or was not lawfully communicated, the criminal charge has no foundation to stand on.

## Article 195: Breaching a Quarantine Measure

Article 195 of the TCK punishes a person who fails to comply with measures ordered by a competent authority to place under quarantine the location where someone infected with, or who has died from, a contagious disease is found. The prescribed penalty ranges from two months to one year of imprisonment. Three elements must combine for the offense to exist: there must be a place where a person infected with (or deceased from) a contagious disease is located; that place must actually have been placed under quarantine by a competent authority; and the defendant must have failed to comply with that specific measure. Notably, the law does not require that the disease actually spread to anyone else or that any concrete harm resulted — the offense is complete the moment the quarantine measure is breached. For this reason, Turkish doctrine classifies Article 195 as an abstract-danger offense: whether the disease genuinely spread is irrelevant: violating the order is enough on its own.

Anyone present within the quarantined area who is aware of the measure can be a perpetrator of this offense — no special status or relationship to the disease is required. Because the "place" being quarantined is fixed by an administrative decision under Turkey's general public health legislation (Law No. 1593) and related regulations, the offense's applicability turns entirely on whether that quarantine decision genuinely exists, was tied to a specific location rather than a specific person, and was properly communicated to the defendant. If no such formal decision was made, or if it was never announced through the required channels, the basis for criminal liability disappears. As for intent, the article contains no provision for negligence, so it can only be committed intentionally: the defendant must have known that the place was under quarantine and have chosen to disregard that measure anyway. Knowing exactly which disease triggered the quarantine is not required — awareness that a binding restriction existed is sufficient.

## Article 196: Burial Outside a Designated Cemetery

Article 196 addresses a different concern. It punishes a person who buries, or has someone else bury, a body somewhere other than a place designated for burial, with a penalty of up to six months' imprisonment. The value being protected is not purely hygienic — unlike Article 195, this provision is equally about the community's respect for the dead and about preventing unregulated, unsupervised interment. The offense turns solely on where the burial took place: the location must have been an area other than a cemetery or another officially designated burial site. Whether a burial permit was obtained or whether the death was properly reported to civil registry authorities is a separate question; even a body that was reported through all the correct channels still triggers this offense if it is interred outside a cemetery. The reverse is also true: a burial carried out within cemetery grounds, even without proper prior notification, falls outside Article 196 and may instead be assessed under separate provisions (such as civil registry violations).

The law also broadens who can be held liable: both the person who physically carries out the burial and the person who arranges for someone else to do so are treated as principal offenders on equal footing — effectively folding an instigation-like role directly into primary liability, rather than leaving it to general complicity rules. Neither an unclear identity of the deceased nor a suspicious cause of death is required for the offense; what matters is simply that the burial site fell outside the legally designated location. As with Article 195, the offense requires intent: the defendant must have known that the burial was taking place outside the designated area and acted deliberately. A genuine and reasonable mistake about a location's cemetery status could negate intent, but carrying out the burial in line with religious rites or family tradition does not excuse choosing a non-designated site — the only test the law applies is whether the burial location itself was outside official allocation.

## Attempt, Complicity and Concurrence

Both offenses are conduct-based rather than result-based, so while an attempt is theoretically conceivable if the underlying act is interrupted midway, the practical scope for attempt is narrow given how these acts unfold. On complicity, because Article 196 already folds the person who "has a body buried" into primary liability, general complicity rules become largely secondary for that specific role, though they continue to apply to anyone who merely assists. Where a burial outside a designated location is accompanied by a separate wrongful act — for example, falsifying a death certificate or making false statements to civil registry authorities — those acts are prosecuted separately, under forgery of official documents or the relevant offense, in addition to Article 196; where the irregularity is limited to the burial location itself, only Article 196 applies.

## Sentencing and Procedure

Article 195 carries a sentence of two months to one year of imprisonment; Article 196 carries a sentence of up to six months. Given these ranges, both offenses are, in principle, eligible for conversion into a judicial fine, suspension of the sentence, or a deferral of the announcement of the verdict, depending on the circumstances of the case. Jurisdiction lies with the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi). Because no exception is made in the law, both offenses are investigated and prosecuted ex officio; in practice, the underlying administrative measure or burial irregularity is typically first identified and reported by the competent administrative body — a health directorate, cemetery administration, or law enforcement — before making its way to the prosecutor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I be convicted under Article 195 just for being told to self-isolate, without an official quarantine decision covering my location?**
No. Turkish appellate practice has repeatedly emphasized that Article 195 requires a quarantine measure tied to a specific place, issued by a competent authority — not merely an instruction directed at an individual. Being asked to isolate as a suspected or confirmed contagious-disease case, without a documented quarantine decision covering the location, does not satisfy the elements of the offense.

**Does it matter whether anyone actually caught the disease because I ignored a quarantine order?**
No. Article 195 is treated as an abstract-danger offense — the breach of the quarantine measure itself completes the offense, regardless of whether transmission or any concrete harm actually occurred.

**If I arranged a burial with a proper permit and registered the death, but the burial site was not an official cemetery, am I still liable under Article 196?**
Yes. Article 196 focuses exclusively on the location of the burial. A properly permitted and registered burial that nonetheless takes place outside a designated cemetery or burial ground still constitutes the offense.

**Can someone be prosecuted under Article 196 for arranging a burial through another person rather than digging the grave themselves?**
Yes. The provision treats the person who arranges for a burial to be carried out by someone else as a principal offender, on the same footing as the person who physically performs the burial.
