Articles 195 through 210 of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK) bring corporate groups — holding structures — within a legal framework. Rather than simply recognizing a parent company's practical control over its subsidiaries, the Code builds a distinctive liability and equalization system to prevent that control from harming minority shareholders and creditors of the controlled company.

## When Group Rules Apply

A company is "controlling" and another "controlled" where it holds a majority of voting rights, can appoint a majority of the management body, or otherwise holds control through a contract — with at least one party having its registered office in Turkey (Art. 195). Notably, the controlling party need not be a company at all — an individual "undertaking" can qualify, and is then itself treated as a merchant. Turkish courts have required, under related registry regulations, that an individual control at least three companies before qualifying as a controlling "undertaking" for these purposes.

## Disclosure Thresholds

Crossing ownership thresholds of 5%, 10%, 20%, 25%, 33%, 50%, 67%, or 100% of a company's capital triggers a mandatory notification, registration, and publication within ten days (Art. 198). Failure to comply freezes all rights attached to the relevant shares, including voting rights, until the disclosure is made — a substantial enforcement mechanism.

## Liability for Abuse of Control

The centerpiece of this chapter is Art. 202: a controlling company may not exercise its control in a way that causes loss to the controlled company — unless that loss is actually offset within the same fiscal year, or the controlled company is given an equivalent claim to be settled by year-end. Where neither happens, every shareholder of the controlled company may claim damages directly from the controlling company and the responsible directors, and creditors may claim the loss be paid to the company even before insolvency. A "business judgment" defense is available where the controlling company shows an independent, careful director of an unaffiliated company could reasonably have made the same decision.

## Full Ownership and the Minority Squeeze-Out

Where a company owns 100% of a subsidiary, it may issue binding instructions to the subsidiary's management even where these cause loss, provided this follows a defined group policy — subject to a floor: instructions cannot be given that clearly exceed the subsidiary's ability to pay or threaten its survival (Art. 203-204). Separately, where a controlling company holds at least 90% of a subsidiary and the minority is disruptive, acting in bad faith, or behaving recklessly, the controlling company may buy out the minority's shares (Art. 208) — but Turkish courts have confirmed this squeeze-out right runs one way only: it gives no reciprocal right for minority shareholders to force a buy-out of their own shares.

Foreign investors structuring a Turkish holding group should build the Art. 202 equalization requirement into intra-group agreements from the outset, since undocumented cross-subsidization between group companies is a recurring source of minority shareholder litigation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When is a company regarded as a 'controlling company'?

Under TTK Article 195/1, a commercial company is regarded as the controlling company, and the other as the dependent company, where it holds directly or indirectly <b>the majority of the voting rights</b> of another commercial company, where it <b>can procure the election of enough members to form a decision-making majority on the management body</b>, where together with its own voting rights it commands a majority of the votes on the basis of an agreement, or where it <b>can keep the other company under its control by virtue of an agreement or otherwise</b>. In addition, under Article 195/2, holding the majority of a company's capital also gives rise to a presumption of control.

### May the controlling company cause the dependent company a loss?

No. Under TTK Article 202/1(a) <b>the controlling company may not exercise its control in a manner that causes the dependent company a loss.</b> If a loss-causing transaction has been carried out, the controlling company must either actually offset it within that financial year or grant a claim specifying how and when it will do so. If no equalisation is made, <b>every shareholder</b> of the dependent company may claim compensation from the controlling company and from the directors who caused the loss; creditors may also bring this action.

### Can the controlling company squeeze out the minority's shares?

Under certain conditions, yes. Under TTK Article 208/1, where the controlling company holds directly or indirectly <b>at least ninety per cent of the shares and voting rights</b> of a capital company and <b>the minority obstructs the company's operations, acts contrary to the rule of good faith, creates appreciable difficulty or behaves recklessly</b>, it may purchase the minority's shares at <b>their stock exchange value, if any, or otherwise at the value determined in the manner laid down in Article 202/2</b>. The condition is not merely the percentage but the minority's qualified obstructive conduct.
