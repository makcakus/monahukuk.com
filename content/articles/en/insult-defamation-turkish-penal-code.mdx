Few offenses under Turkish criminal law sit closer to the everyday use of language than insult. The same sentence can be protected, if sharp, criticism in one context and a punishable attack on someone's dignity in another. Foreign nationals, journalists and businesspeople who become involved in a Turkish criminal file over an insult complaint are often surprised to learn that the real dispute is rarely about whether particular words were said — that is usually undisputed — but about which legal category those words fall into. Article 125 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), together with the provisions that follow it, sets out a fairly detailed framework for making that distinction.

## The Interest Protected: Honor and Reputation

Article 125 protects a person's honor, dignity and reputation. Turkish legal doctrine treats this as having two sides: the internal sense of self-worth a person holds, and the external reputation that others attribute to them in society. The provision is drafted broadly enough to cover both, which is why the offense can be committed either in the victim's presence or behind their back. Because this interest is tied so closely to the individual, insults directed at a deceased person are dealt with separately, under a distinct provision (Article 130), rather than under the general insult offense.

## Two Ways of Committing the Offense: Factual Imputation and Abuse

Article 125 criminalizes two different kinds of conduct under the same penalty. The first is attributing to someone a concrete act or fact capable of demeaning their honor, dignity or reputation — a specific, checkable claim that can, in principle, be shown to be true or false and that can be tied to a particular time and place. The second is abuse in the narrower sense: a direct value judgment or insult with no underlying factual claim attached to it. This distinction is not a mere technicality — it has real consequences for how a case unfolds. Only where a concrete fact has been imputed can the accused later invoke the defense that the claim was true (discussed below); an abusive remark carries no factual content and therefore leaves no room for that defense.

The offense can also be committed through a message — spoken, written, or in the form of an image or recording — addressed directly to the victim. This covers text messages, emails, and direct messages sent through social media, and it is punished the same as an insult delivered face to face.

### In the Victim's Presence Versus Behind Their Back

An insult said to someone's face is punishable without any further condition. An insult made about someone in their absence is treated differently: it is only punishable if the statement was communicated to at least three other people. Turkish law calls this requirement "ihtilat" — those three people must actually have perceived the statement, not merely have had the opportunity to hear it. If this threshold is not met, words said about someone behind their back, however harsh, do not amount to a criminal insult.

## Does the Victim Have to Be Named?

A common defense strategy is to argue that the statement never mentioned the complainant by name. Turkish law does not accept this at face value. Article 126 provides that failing to name the victim explicitly, or wrapping the imputation in vague or indirect language, does not matter if it is nonetheless clear that the statement was directed at that particular person. The relevant test is whether the target of the statement can reasonably be identified by others who read or hear it — not whether a name was used.

## Where Criticism Ends and Insult Begins

The real tension in this area of law runs between freedom of expression and the right to reputation. As a general rule, even harsh, uncomfortable or wounding criticism remains protected. What is not protected is language that has severed its connection to the subject under discussion and serves only to demean the person. Courts weigh the context in which the words were used, the roles of the parties, whether the exchange took place in a public or matter-of-public-interest setting, and whether the statement was anchored to any underlying fact. Politicians and other public officials are expected to tolerate a higher degree of criticism than private individuals — but that higher threshold never converts genuinely abusive language into lawful speech.

## Intent Requirement

Insult is only punishable when committed intentionally; there is no such thing as a negligent insult under Turkish law. It is enough that the person knew the words used were capable of demeaning the victim's honor and chose to use them anyway — the law does not generally require proof of a specific motive beyond that. One qualified form of the offense is an exception: where the insult is committed because the victim expressed, changed, or tried to spread their religious, political, social or philosophical beliefs, or acted in accordance with the requirements of their religion, that causal link — the "because of" — becomes a distinct element the prosecution must establish.

## Aggravated Forms and Publicity

Several circumstances raise the penalty. Where the insult targets a public official because of their duties; targets a person because they expressed, changed or tried to spread their religious, political, social or philosophical convictions, or acted according to the requirements of their religion; or invokes values considered sacred under a person's religion, the minimum sentence cannot be less than one year. Separately, where the insult is committed publicly, the sentence is increased by one-sixth. Publicity here means that the statement was made in a setting where an indeterminate number of people could hear or see it — a public social media post is the standard example. Where an insult targets a public body that operates collectively, because of its members' duties, the offense is treated as having been committed against each member of that body individually, and the rules on connected/repeated offenses (a single course of conduct treated as one aggravated offense rather than multiple separate ones) apply.

## When No Punishment Is Imposed: Proof of the Fact and Immunity for Statements in Proceedings

Turkish law provides two significant routes by which an insult that would otherwise be punishable escapes punishment.

The first, under Article 127, concerns proof. If the concrete fact imputed to the victim — and which itself amounts to an offense — turns out to be true, no punishment is imposed on the person who made the statement. Where a final conviction already exists against the person the statement was about, the imputed fact is deemed proven without further inquiry. In every other case, a request to prove the truth of the imputed fact will only be granted if establishing whether it is true serves a public interest, or if the complainant consents to the proof being examined. A second, easily overlooked limit applies here: a person can still be convicted of insult if they use an already-proven fact as a vehicle to demean someone — proof of the underlying fact does not license adding an abusive commentary on top of it.

The second route, under Article 128, is a form of immunity for statements made in the course of legal proceedings. Concrete claims or negative assessments about a person, made in a written or oral application, claim or defense submitted to a judicial authority or an administrative body, do not attract punishment — but only if those claims and assessments rest on real, concrete facts and are actually connected to the underlying dispute. Simply including a remark in a court petition does not protect it if it has no genuine bearing on the matter being litigated.

## Provocation and Mutual Insult

Where an insult is committed in response to an unjust act by the victim, the court may reduce the sentence by up to one-third, or may decide not to impose any punishment at all. Where the insult is a response to an intentional physical assault, no punishment is imposed at all. Where the insult is mutual — both sides insulted each other — the court may, depending on the circumstances, reduce the sentence for one or both parties by up to one-third, or again decide not to punish either or both. In practice, this makes it essential to establish precisely which party's words started the exchange, since that sequencing can determine whether either side is punished at all.

## Insults Against a Deceased Person's Memory

A person who insults the memory of someone who has died, provided the statement reaches at least three other people, is punishable, and the penalty is increased by one-sixth if the insult was made publicly. A related provision separately punishes taking a corpse or human remains, or committing degrading acts against them.

## Attempt, Participation and Multiple Offenses

Insult is generally treated as complete the moment it is uttered, without requiring any further result. Where the insult is spoken directly to the victim's face, the act cannot meaningfully be broken into stages, so an attempted version of the offense is not recognized. Where it is committed through a letter or a message that is intercepted before reaching the addressee, however, an attempt can be recognized. All forms of participation (aiding, inciting, acting jointly) are possible. Repeated insults against the same person over time are treated as a single connected offense, while insults directed separately at different people are counted as that many separate offenses.

## Penalty, Complaint and Procedure

The basic penalty is imprisonment from three months to two years, or a judicial fine — the court chooses between these two options. Except where the insult is committed against a public official because of their duties, prosecution depends entirely on a complaint filed by the victim. That complaint must be filed within six months of learning both of the act and of the identity of the person responsible. If the victim died before filing a complaint, or if the offense was committed against the memory of someone already deceased, the victim's spouse, siblings, and relatives up to the second degree (ascendants and descendants) may file the complaint instead. Because the offense is complaint-dependent, it falls within the scope of mediation under Turkish criminal procedure — except, again, for insults against a public official committed because of their duties, which are excluded from mediation. Insult cases are tried before the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it a defense that the accused never mentioned the complainant's name?**
Not by itself. Article 126 makes clear that if the statement's target can reasonably be identified from the surrounding context, the absence of an explicit name does not prevent a conviction.

**If the imputed fact turns out to be true, is the accused automatically cleared?**
Not automatically, but it is a strong defense under Article 127. If the imputed fact is proven true — or a final conviction already exists on it — no punishment follows. However, using a proven fact merely as a pretext to demean the person is still punishable in its own right, and outside of a final prior conviction, the request to prove the fact must still be granted by the court, which happens only where there is a public interest in establishing the truth or the complainant consents.

**Does harsh criticism of a public official count as insult?**
Generally not, as long as the criticism stays connected to the official's conduct or the matter under discussion. Public officials and politicians are expected to tolerate a higher level of criticism than private individuals, but language that abandons any connection to the subject and simply demeans the person can still be punished, regardless of who the target is.

**How long does a victim have to file a complaint, and can anyone else file it?**
Six months from learning both the act and the identity of the offender, since prosecution depends on the victim's complaint (except for insults against public officials because of their duties). If the victim dies before complaining, or the insult targeted the memory of someone already deceased, the spouse, siblings, and relatives up to the second degree may file the complaint in their place.
