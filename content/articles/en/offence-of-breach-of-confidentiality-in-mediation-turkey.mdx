*Article 33 – (1) A person who, by acting contrary to the obligation set out in Article 4 of this Law, causes damage to a legally protected interest of another person shall be punished with imprisonment of up to six months.*

*(2) The investigation and prosecution of these offences depend on complaint.*

Article 33 is the sole criminal provision of Law No. 6325, and its subject is confidentiality. The legislature's choice is telling: while a mediator's breach of impartiality, failure to fulfil the duty to inform, or violation of the advertising ban attract administrative and civil rather than criminal sanctions, a breach of confidentiality is made a criminal offence outright. The reason is that confidentiality is the only guarantee that makes mediation function — in a system where what is said at the table might leave the room, no one discloses their real position.

## Elements of the Offence

**Perpetrator.** The offence can be committed by more than the mediator. Article 33/1 punishes "a person acting contrary to the obligation set out in Article 4 of this Law"; Article 4 in turn binds both the mediator (para. 1) and the parties and other persons attending the sessions (para. 2). A party, party's counsel, statutory representative, an expert kept present at the negotiations and an interpreter can therefore all be perpetrators. The 5th Criminal Chamber of the Court of Cassation has expressly held that information obtained during the mediation process must be kept confidential "by the parties and by the lawyers representing them".

**Conduct.** Acting contrary to the confidentiality obligation in Article 4. For the mediator, this means disclosing the information and documents submitted to them or otherwise obtained within the framework of the activity, and other records. For the parties and attendees, it means failing to observe the same confidentiality. The conduct is not an offence where the parties have agreed otherwise — confidentiality is a protection the parties may jointly lift.

**Result.** This is not a mere conduct offence. Punishment requires that the disclosure **cause damage to a legally protected interest of a person**. In practice this is the limiting element of the offence: a disclosure that harms no one is not punishable under Article 33, even if it contravenes the confidentiality principle.

**Mental element.** The offence may be committed intentionally; negligent commission is not punishable.

**Sanction.** Imprisonment of up to six months. Because the upper limit is low, the sentence may be converted into a judicial fine or another alternative sanction under Article 50 of the Turkish Criminal Code, and a decision to defer the announcement of the verdict may be given.

**Complaint.** Under Article 33/2, investigation and prosecution depend on complaint; the complaint period is six months from learning of the act and the perpetrator, under Article 73 of the Turkish Criminal Code.

## The Limit: Production Commanded by Law Is Not an Offence

The most contested area in the application of Article 33 concerns mediation documents attached to a statement of claim. In mediation as a condition of action, the claimant must attach the original of the final minutes recording that no settlement was reached, or a copy certified by the mediator (Art. 18/A/2; for labour disputes Art. 3/2 of Law No. 7036). Article 5/3 likewise provides that information may be disclosed "to the extent required by a provision of law".

The 7th Criminal Chamber upheld a judgment dismissing on the merits an appeal against an acquittal founded on the view that the minutes annexed to a statement of defence did not fall within the scope of confidentiality, finding that the proceedings had been conducted in accordance with procedure and the evidence was sufficient.

The exception is, however, confined to the minutes themselves. In a judgment of the 5th Criminal Chamber it was established that the accused, a lawyer, had expressly written in the statement of claim the **monetary offer** made by the employer side at the mediation stage and the complainant's **telephone number**. According to the judgment, although attaching the final minutes to the statement of claim is a condition of action, disclosing an offer made within the scope of mediation is prohibited and cannot be assessed within that exception; taking into account also that the telephone number was written although it was in no way necessary, the existing evidence was sufficient for the opening of the final investigation.

Read together, the two judgments yield a clear criterion: what is decisive is not that the document entered the case file, but whether **content going beyond the scope commanded by law** was disclosed.

## Article 33 and Its Relationship with Other Offences

A breach of confidentiality rarely stands alone.

**Unlawful processing of personal data (Arts. 135–136 of the Turkish Criminal Code).** Writing the other party's telephone number, address and identity details into a pleading, or sharing them with third parties, where this is not necessary raises these offences. In the judgment of the 5th Criminal Chamber referred to above, the personal-data dimension was also separately assessed.

**Listening to and recording conversations between persons (Art. 133 of the Turkish Criminal Code).** Recording mediation sessions without the knowledge and consent of one of the parties may constitute this offence. A judgment of the 12th Criminal Chamber concerning the recording, without consent, of a conversation about the possibility of mediation illustrates this field. The recording, moreover, cannot be relied upon as evidence under Article 5 of Law No. 6325.

**Disclosure of official secrets (Art. 258 of the Turkish Criminal Code).** Because the mediator is not regarded as a public official, this provision does not, as a rule, apply; a separate assessment is required, however, for commission members representing a public administration.

**Attorney–client secrecy.** For a lawyer acting as mediator or as a party's counsel, the duty of secrecy under Article 36 of the Attorneyship Law and the attendant disciplinary liability operate in addition.

## Consequences Beyond the Criminal Route

Beyond criminal liability, a breach of confidentiality has two further consequences. **Evidentiary bar**: under Article 5, disclosed statements and documents cannot be relied upon in the judgment even if they have been produced as evidence. **Discipline and registry**: for the mediator, the warning and removal-from-registry process under Article 21/2 may be triggered; for lawyer-mediators, bar disciplinary proceedings also arise. The injured person may in addition claim damages under the general provisions.

## Distinction from Reconciliation in Criminal Procedure

Although similar in name and logic, [reconciliation](/articles/reconciliation-turkish-criminal-procedure-code) in criminal procedure is governed by a different statute (Art. 253 of the Code of Criminal Procedure) and has its own confidentiality regime: under Article 253/20, statements made during reconciliation negotiations cannot be used as evidence in any investigation, prosecution or action. That provision, however, creates an evidentiary bar; it does not establish a separate offence. The offence in Article 33 is specific to mediation in civil disputes.

## Case Law

The following judgments show the practical boundaries of the offence of breach of confidentiality.

**1. Court of Cassation, 5th Criminal Chamber, Case No. 2023/12472, Decision No. 2024/814, dated 25.01.2024**

Assessing Articles 4 and 33/1 together, the Chamber held that information obtained during the mediation process must be kept confidential by the parties and by the lawyers representing them. Given that the accused lawyer had expressly written the offer made at the mediation stage and the complainant's telephone number in the statement of claim, it was concluded that although attaching the final minutes is a condition of action, disclosing the offer is prohibited and cannot be assessed within that scope, and that the existing evidence was sufficient for the opening of the final investigation.

**2. Court of Cassation, 7th Criminal Chamber, Case No. 2024/6804, Decision No. 2026/7678, dated 29.06.2026**

The file concerned a public prosecution for the offence of breach of confidentiality under Article 33/1. The first-instance court acquitted, and the appeal was dismissed on the merits. After establishing that the decision was subject to cassation under the final paragraph of Article 59 of the Attorneyship Law, the Chamber found that the procedural steps had been taken in accordance with procedure and the law and that the claims and defences had been discussed in the reasoned decision together with the evidence collected, and upheld the judgment on the ground that it disclosed no unlawfulness.

**3. Court of Cassation, 12th Criminal Chamber, Case No. 2020/1083, Decision No. 2023/769, dated 13.03.2023**

The decision concerns the recording by one party, without the knowledge and consent of the other, of a conversation about the possibility of mediation between them. The dispute demonstrates the criminal-law dimension of unauthorised recording of discussions in a mediation context.

**4. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2022/7271, Decision No. 2022/8056, dated 20.06.2022**

In this dispute the mediator refused a request to include a third company in the process on the grounds that the applicant's counsel had not consented and that the request would constitute a breach of confidentiality. The decision is significant in showing the limits within which the mediator may invoke confidentiality as a ground.

**5. Council of State, 10th Chamber, Case No. 2018/3449, Decision No. 2021/1714, dated 08.04.2021**

In an annulment action against the mediation legislation, the decision addresses the principles of confidentiality (Art. 4) and inadmissibility of statements and documents (Art. 5) as a whole, and sets out the statutory framework of the confidentiality regime protected by Article 33.
