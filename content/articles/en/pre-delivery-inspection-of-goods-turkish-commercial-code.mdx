TTK Art. 1184 gives carriers, masters, and consignees a tool for locking in evidence of a cargo's condition before delivery — one that, used well, can save a claimant from a much harsher notice requirement down the line.

## The Inspection Right

Before the consignee takes delivery, any of the carrier, master, or consignee may have the cargo's condition, measurements, count, or weight inspected by a court, a competent authority, or a duly appointed expert. This is optional, not mandatory — a party can protect its position through ordinary written notice instead — but an inspection carries far more evidentiary weight than a unilateral written claim. The law encourages, without requiring, both sides to attend: "the other party should be present where possible." An inspection both sides witnessed carries much stronger adversarial credibility than a one-sided one that a later court can easily second-guess.

## Cost Allocation as a Behavioral Nudge

Inspection costs fall on whoever requested the inspection — unless the consignee requested it and the inspection turns up loss or damage the carrier is liable for, in which case the carrier pays. This two-way structure discourages both frivolous requests (a consignee gambling on finding something) and stonewalling (a carrier resisting inspection gains nothing, since it pays anyway if damage is confirmed, and risks a weaker evidentiary record if it obstructs).

## Why This Article Matters More Than It Looks

Art. 1184's real significance shows up in the next provision: if the inspection was conducted with both parties' participation by a court, authority, or officially appointed expert, no separate written damage notice is required at all. A party that invokes this formal inspection route — especially by inviting the counterparty — sidesteps the risk of missing the ordinarily strict short notice deadline entirely. For high-value or damage-prone cargo, using Art. 1184 at delivery can substantially lighten the claimant's evidentiary burden in any later dispute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can the condition of the goods be ascertained before delivery is taken?

Yes. Under TTK Article 1184/1, before the consignee takes delivery of the goods, <b>the carrier, the master or the consignee</b> may have the condition, measure, number or weight of the goods examined by <b>the court, other competent authorities or experts authorised for the purpose</b>. Where possible, the other party is present at the examination; this ensures that the ascertainment is adversarial and not open to later challenge.

### Who bears the cost of the examination?

Under TTK Article 1184/2 the cost of the examination is as a rule borne by <b>the applicant</b>. There is, however, an important exception: <b>where the consignee applies for the examination and a loss or damage for which the carrier must pay compensation is ultimately established, the cost of the examination falls on the carrier</b>. This rule prevents a consignee who turns out to be right from bearing the cost of the ascertainment.

### Is an examination compulsory?

No. The examination under Article 1184 is a <b>voluntary</b> means of proof; not carrying one out carries no sanction of itself. By contrast, the duty of <b>notification</b> in TTK Article 1185 is subject to a time limit, and failure to comply gives rise to the presumption that the goods were delivered as stated in the bill of lading. In practice, an ascertainment made under Article 1184 gives concrete substance to the notification under Article 1185 and greatly strengthens its evidential force.
