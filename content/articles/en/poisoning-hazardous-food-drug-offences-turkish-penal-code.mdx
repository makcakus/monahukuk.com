Foreign businesses operating food production, catering, pharmaceutical distribution or import operations in Turkey are usually well aware of the administrative food-safety and pharmaceutical regulations that govern their sector. Fewer appreciate that a serious contamination incident, a batch of spoiled goods that reaches the market, or the unlicensed handling of a hazardous substance can also trigger criminal liability under the Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237, "TCK"), independently of any administrative fine. Articles 185 through 194 of the TCK form a distinct group of offences against public health that sit alongside, and in some respects go beyond, the regulatory framework administered by food-safety and health authorities. This article explains how that group of offences is structured, what conduct it targets, and why the criminal exposure it creates is often broader than businesses expect.

## The Interest Being Protected: Public Health as a Collective Good

The offences in this chapter of the Penal Code do not protect a single identified victim. They protect the public's ability to trust that the water, food and medicine in general circulation are safe, on behalf of an indeterminate group of potential victims — anyone who might consume the product. Because the class of potential victims is undefined, it does not matter whether the offender knew, or could have known, exactly who would be harmed. This is also why most of these provisions are drafted as "danger offences" rather than "harm offences": criminal liability attaches once life or health has been placed at risk, without necessarily requiring proof that anyone actually fell ill. This design means the Penal Code operates as the criminal-law backstop to administrative food and pharmaceutical legislation — regulatory bodies set and police day-to-day production and safety standards, while the Penal Code reserves criminal sanctions for the most serious breaches of those standards. Within the group, the provisions split along two lines: some (Articles 185-187) attach to a specific contaminated product — a particular batch of water, food, drink or medicine — while others (Articles 193-194) target conduct tied to licensing controls or to especially vulnerable categories of victim, without being limited to any single product.

## Adding a Poisonous Substance to Water, Food or Medicine (Article 185)

Article 185 is the core provision of the group. It criminalises adding poison, or otherwise spoiling or altering, any water intended for drinking or any food, drink or other substance meant to be eaten, drunk, used or consumed. Anyone can commit this offence — it is not limited to producers or professionals — and the class of potential victims is again the undefined public exposed to the product. Two forms of conduct are covered: introducing poison, or "spoiling by other means," a phrase interpreted broadly enough to reach any intervention that renders a product unsafe, not only classic chemical poisoning. The offence is only complete, however, once the spoiling or poisoning actually places people's life or health in danger — a requirement that makes this a "concrete danger" offence. In practice, this means a court must find, based on the specific facts, that a real danger arose; a merely theoretical or abstract risk is not enough to convict. The offence normally requires intent, but a second, separate paragraph punishes the same conduct when committed negligently — that is, in breach of a duty of care — with a considerably lighter sentence. This negligence variant is what most often applies to lapses in food and drug production chains: a contamination that results from carelessness rather than a deliberate act. The intentional form of the offence carries a maximum sentence that can exceed ten years, a threshold that matters both for the severity of the case and, as discussed below, for which court has jurisdiction.

## Trading in Spoiled Goods and Producing Hazardous Medicine (Articles 186-187)

Article 186 addresses a different link in the chain: not the act of spoiling a product, but putting an already spoiled or altered food, drink or medicine — one capable of endangering life or health — into commercial circulation. Three forms of conduct are covered: selling, supplying, or simply possessing such goods. The inclusion of mere possession is significant, because it means a business can face criminal exposure for holding hazardous stock with a view to sale, even before any sale takes place. Article 187 targets a related but distinct act: manufacturing or selling medicine in a way that endangers life or health — the focus here is on the drug itself having been produced in a harmful condition, rather than on a previously safe product having been contaminated afterward. Both articles treat professional status as an aggravating factor: under Article 186, committing the offence in the course of a licensed trade or profession increases the penalty by one third, and under Article 187 the same increase applies where the offender is a physician or pharmacist, or otherwise acting within a licensed profession. This reflects a deliberate policy choice to punish more severely the betrayal of the heightened trust the public places in health-sector professionals. Both provisions also combine imprisonment with a judicial fine, adding a financial dimension that is particularly relevant for commercial actors.

## Unlicensed Trade in Poisonous Substances (Article 193) and Supplying Vulnerable Groups (Article 194)

Article 193 is structurally distinct from Article 185. It does not concern adding poison to a food or medicine; it concerns the substance containing poison itself, where its production, possession or sale is subject to official licensing. The offence consists of producing, possessing, selling or transporting such a substance without the required licence. Because the text does not require proof that life or health was actually endangered, this is classed as an "abstract danger" offence — the breach of the licensing regime is itself treated by the legislature as sufficient proof of danger, without a case-specific finding. Article 194 follows the same abstract-danger logic from a different angle: it punishes supplying, or making available for consumption, substances capable of endangering health to children, persons with a mental illness, or persons who use volatile substances, without requiring that any actual harm materialise. Because the potential victims here are people with limited capacity to protect themselves, this provision shifts the protective focus from public health in general to individual vulnerability. Both Articles 193 and 194 carry lighter penalties than Articles 185-187, but the fact that prosecutors do not need to establish a concrete danger meaningfully eases the evidentiary burden in these cases.

## Attempt, Participation, Concurrence and Which Court Has Jurisdiction

All of the offences in this group require intent, with the sole exception of the negligence-based variant in Article 185(2), which by its nature cannot be attempted. For the concrete-danger offences — Article 185(1), Article 186 and Article 187 — an attempt is conceptually possible where the underlying conduct can be broken down into distinct stages of execution. For the abstract-danger offences in Articles 193 and 194, the point at which the offence is treated as complete is drawn much earlier, leaving little room for an attempt to be recognised. General rules on participation under the Penal Code apply throughout, and Articles 186 and 187 in particular tend to generate chains of participants spanning production and distribution. Where the same conduct satisfies both the production element of Article 186 and elements of Article 187, the applicable rules on apparent concurrence of offences must be worked through to determine which provision governs. None of these offences require a private complaint — because the protected interest is collective rather than individual, prosecutors investigate and prosecute them on their own initiative. Jurisdiction is not uniform across the group: because Article 185(1) in its intentional form carries a maximum sentence exceeding ten years, it falls under the general rule assigning offences of that severity to the Heavy Penal Court, whereas the negligence-based Article 185(2), along with Articles 186, 187, 193 and 194 — all of which carry lower maximum sentences — are, as a rule, tried before the Criminal Court of First Instance. Notably, the legislature has not extended to this offence group the effective-repentance provisions or corporate security-measure rules that apply to the neighbouring narcotics offences (Articles 188-192); for legal entities, only the general security-measure provision of Article 60 may apply, and only if its own conditions are independently met.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a company need to prove someone actually got sick before facing liability under Article 185?**
For the intentional form of Article 185, the courts must find that a concrete, case-specific danger to life or health arose — actual illness is not required, but a purely theoretical risk is not enough either. For the negligence-based form, and for the abstract-danger offences in Articles 193 and 194, no such case-specific danger finding is needed at all.

**Is holding spoiled stock in a warehouse enough to trigger liability, even without a sale?**
Yes. Article 186 criminalises selling, supplying or simply possessing food, drink or medicine that has already been spoiled or altered and is capable of endangering life or health, so mere possession with commercial intent can already amount to the offence.

**Are these food and drug safety offences treated more harshly when committed by a licensed professional?**
Yes. Both Article 186 and Article 187 increase the penalty by one third where the offender acted within a licensed trade or profession, or, under Article 187, specifically as a physician or pharmacist.

**Which court hears these cases — can they always be handled in the ordinary criminal courts?**
Not always. Because the intentional form of Article 185 carries a maximum sentence above ten years, it is tried before the Heavy Penal Court, while the negligence-based form of Article 185 and the offences under Articles 186, 187, 193 and 194 are, as a rule, tried before the Criminal Court of First Instance.
