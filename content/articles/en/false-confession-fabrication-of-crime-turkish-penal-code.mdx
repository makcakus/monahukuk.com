Turkish criminal law penalizes two distinct forms of misleading the justice system that are easy to confuse but rest on different conduct: falsely claiming responsibility for a crime someone else actually committed, and reporting or staging a crime that never happened at all. These offenses are set out in Articles 270 and 271 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), within the chapter on offenses against the administration of justice. Neither offense requires an identifiable victim in the ordinary sense — what the law protects is the integrity of criminal investigations and the efficient use of the state's investigative resources. For foreign nationals or counsel encountering these charges in the course of a Turkish criminal proceeding, understanding the distinction between the two provisions, and between both of them and the separate crime of false accusation, is essential to assessing exposure and strategy.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Articles 270 and 271 do not protect a private individual's rights the way most offenses in the criminal code do. Instead, they safeguard the proper functioning of the investigative and judicial process itself — the ability of prosecutors, police, and courts to direct their limited resources toward events that actually occurred and toward the people who actually caused them. A false confession under Article 270 misdirects the authorities toward the wrong person for a real event; fabrication of a crime under Article 271 misdirects them toward an event that never took place at all. Both waste investigative capacity, distort case outcomes, and — cumulatively — erode public confidence in the reliability of the justice system. Because the protected interest is institutional rather than personal, these are prosecuted regardless of whether any private party asks for prosecution, and no individual complainant is treated as the "victim" for purposes of participating in the case.

## False Confession Under Article 270: Taking On Someone Else's Crime

Article 270 punishes a person who falsely tells a competent authority that they committed, or participated in, a crime they did not actually commit or participate in. The defining feature is that the false claim is directed at the speaker's own conduct — the person confesses to something they did not do. If the false claim instead accuses a different, identifiable person of a crime that person did not commit, the applicable offense is false accusation under Article 267, not Article 270. The statement must be made to an authority with the power to investigate or prosecute — police, a prosecutor's office, or a court — a confession made only to private individuals does not satisfy this element. Anyone can commit this offense, and it is complete once the false statement reaches an authority capable of acting on it; the authority does not actually need to open a formal investigation for the crime to be established. The false confession can be made orally or in writing, including during a recorded statement given for another purpose.

## Fabrication of a Crime Under Article 271: Inventing an Event

Article 271 covers two distinct ways of misleading the authorities about an event, rather than about who committed it. The first is reporting to a competent authority that a crime has occurred when the person making the report knows that no such crime actually took place. The second is fabricating evidence or traces of a crime that never happened, in a manner capable of triggering an investigation — for example, staging signs of forced entry, planting objects, or creating false physical traces. Unlike a false confession, the false claim here is not directed at a specific person; the report may not name any suspect at all, or may describe an unknown perpetrator. What matters is that the report or the fabricated evidence reaches the authorities and is capable of prompting an investigation — whether an investigation is actually opened is not decisive for the offense to be complete. A person who reports a burglary that never occurred, or who stages break-in marks at their own home to support a false claim, commits this offense.

## The Mental Element and the Family-Protection Reduction

Both offenses require intent — the person must know that the confession or the reported event does not correspond to reality, and must nonetheless act to mislead the authorities. Carelessness or a genuine mistaken belief is not enough; someone who mistakenly reports an event they honestly, if wrongly, believed to be real cannot be convicted under either article. Article 270 carries one distinctive feature tied to motive: where the false confession is made to shield an ascendant, a descendant, a spouse, or a sibling from punishment, the court has discretion to reduce the sentence by up to three-quarters, or to waive punishment entirely. This is not an automatic entitlement — it is left to the judge's assessment of whether the motive was genuinely one of protecting a close relative — and it applies only to the specific family relationships the law lists; a fiancé, an in-law, or a close friend does not qualify. For fabrication of a crime under Article 271, the underlying motive is generally irrelevant to whether the offense is established, whether the aim was to collect on an insurance claim, to divert suspicion from someone else, or simply to attract attention.

## Distinguishing the Two Offenses and Overlap With False Accusation

The core distinction between the two provisions lies in the target of the false statement: a false confession redirects blame onto oneself for something that happened, while fabrication of a crime invents an event that never happened. Both are, in turn, distinguished from false accusation under Article 267 by whether the false claim names a specific person as the perpetrator. In practice, a person may both invent a nonexistent event and, within that same false narrative, name a specific individual as the culprit — in that scenario, the conduct can carry elements of both fabrication and false accusation, and which characterization controls depends on whether the essence of the wrongdoing is the invented event itself or the accusation against the named person. Each offense is treated as complete upon a single report or confession; repeated statements about the same underlying claim do not multiply the number of offenses.

## Penalties and Procedure

Neither offense requires a private complaint — both are prosecuted by the authorities on their own initiative, since the interest at stake is institutional rather than personal. A false confession under Article 270 carries a prison sentence of up to two years. Fabrication of a crime under Article 271 carries a higher maximum of up to three years, reflecting the view that inventing an entire event places a heavier burden on investigative resources than falsely confessing to something that actually occurred. In practice, these offenses are frequently uncovered as a byproduct of a separate investigation — the falsity of the confession or the report typically comes to light only once the real underlying facts are established, meaning charges under Articles 270 or 271 often emerge from within another case file rather than as a standalone starting point.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If I falsely confess to protect my brother, will I definitely receive a reduced sentence?**
No. The reduction — up to three-quarters of the sentence, or a full waiver — is discretionary. The court examines whether the motive was genuinely to shield the qualifying relative (ascendant, descendant, spouse, or sibling) from punishment; the reduction is not automatic simply because such a relationship exists.

**Does the crime have to actually be investigated for a false confession or fabrication charge to apply?**
No. Both offenses are complete once the false statement or fabricated evidence reaches an authority capable of acting on it. Whether an investigation is formally opened afterward does not determine whether the offense was committed.

**What is the difference between fabricating a crime and falsely accusing someone?**
Fabrication under Article 271 involves inventing an event that never happened, without necessarily naming anyone as responsible. False accusation under Article 267 involves falsely blaming a specific, identifiable person. The two can overlap where a fabricated event also names a particular suspect.

**Can someone be charged if they honestly believed the event was real?**
No. Both offenses require intent — knowledge that the confession or the reported event is false. A person who was simply mistaken, without knowing the claim was untrue, does not meet the mental element required for either crime.
