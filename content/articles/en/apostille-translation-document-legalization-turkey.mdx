A birth certificate, power of attorney, diploma, or court judgment issued abroad does not become an official document in Türkiye simply by being brought there. From residence permit applications to title deed transactions, from citizenship files to court proceedings, a significant share of refused files fail not on their legal substance but on a gap in the document's legalisation and translation chain. This article sets out, step by step, how that chain is built.

## The Basic Rule: Article 224 of the Code of Civil Procedure

Article 224 of the Code of Civil Procedure states the framework in a single sentence: **"For official documents prepared by foreign state authorities to have that character in Türkiye, they must be certified by the competent authority of the state that issued them or by the relevant Turkish consular authority."** Paragraph two of the same article preserves the provisions on the legalisation of foreign official documents contained in international conventions to which Türkiye is a party.

Read together, the picture is clear: legalisation is the rule, and the facilitations introduced by international conventions are the exception. Foremost among them is the apostille.

## The Apostille: Legalisation by a Single Certificate

Türkiye is a party to the **1961 Hague Convention Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents**. The Convention removes the chain of consular legalisation between contracting states and replaces it with a single certificate — the **apostille** — issued by the competent authority of the state where the document was made. A document bearing an apostille needs no further certification by a Turkish consulate.

The scope of the apostille deserves attention. The certificate verifies **the authenticity of the signature, the capacity in which the signatory acted, and, where appropriate, the identity of the seal**; it does not confirm the accuracy of the content or the legal validity of the document. An apostilled document may therefore still be assessed on its substance by the Turkish authorities.

The authorities competent to issue apostilles for documents made in Türkiye are also divided: **governorates for administrative documents**, and **judicial commission presidencies at centres with a heavy penal court for judicial documents**. Confusing this distinction on a Turkish document destined for use abroad leads to outright refusal.

## Where There Is No Apostille: The Consular Chain

If the state where the document was issued is not a party to the Hague Convention, or Türkiye has objected to its accession, the apostille route is closed. The classical **legalisation chain** then applies: the document is first certified by the relevant ministry of the issuing state (usually the ministry of foreign affairs) and then legalised by the **Turkish consulate** in that state. On the express wording of Article 224, the character of an official document in Türkiye is acquired through that second certification.

Concrete examples of this dual structure appear in Turkish legislation. The Ministry of National Education's Equivalence Regulation, for instance, expressly requires from graduates an **apostille** or, where no apostille exists, a diploma certified by the education ministry, foreign ministry, or embassy/consulate of the country concerned. The same regulation provides that applications may be made with copies bearing an apostille in member states, and with copies certified by that country's embassy where no apostille exists. That is a concrete illustration in the context of the [equivalence of foreign school records](/en/articles/foreign-children-school-enrollment-turkey).

## Translation: The Notary's Annotation and the Sworn Translator

A legalised document is still insufficient on its own if it is not in Turkish. Article 103 of the Notaries Law governs translation as follows: on translation from one language to another, **an annotation is added by the notary beneath the text**; where the notary has used a sworn translator, that annotation must contain **the translator's identity and address**, and must be dated, signed, and sealed by the notary.

Three practical consequences follow. First, the legal meaning of a "sworn translation" is that the translator has taken an oath before the notary's office concerned; the stamp of an independent translation agency does not by itself confer that character. Second, the notary's annotation must contain the translator's identity and address; translations with an incomplete annotation may be returned by the administration. Third, the translation must also cover the apostille or legalisation certificate — translating only the main text and omitting the certificate is a frequent omission.

For translations made abroad, certification by the relevant Turkish consulate may also arise. In court proceedings, Article 223 of the Code of Civil Procedure applies: a party relying on a document written in a foreign language **must also submit its translation to the court**, and the court may, of its own motion or at the other party's request, require an **official translation** of the document.

## Powers of Attorney Executed Abroad

The most critical document for conducting matters in Türkiye remotely is the power of attorney. Two routes exist. The first is to execute it before a notary in the country of residence, obtain an **apostille**, and have a sworn translation made in Türkiye. The second is to execute it directly at a **Turkish consulate**; since the document is then drawn up in Turkish, no further translation or legalisation is required.

The scope of the authority granted matters equally. For matters requiring special authority — title deed transfer, commencing proceedings, a [citizenship application](/en/articles/citizenship-application-documents-turkey), or banking transactions — a general power of attorney may not suffice; express powers should be set out according to the type of transaction, and attention paid to cases requiring a photograph on the instrument.

## Common Mistakes

Recurring deficiencies fall into a few categories:

- Obtaining the apostille from a local notary rather than the authority that issued the document,
- The apostille bearing a date earlier than the copy of the document submitted,
- Apostilling a photocopy rather than the original,
- Omitting the apostille certificate from the translation,
- A notary's annotation that omits the translator's identity and address,
- The document exceeding the validity period accepted by the relevant administration (six months for certain documents, for example),
- In non-Convention states, skipping the first link of the consular chain — certification by that country's foreign ministry.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an apostille show that the content of the document is accurate?

No. The apostille verifies the authenticity of the signature, the capacity of the signatory, and, where appropriate, the identity of the seal. It does not confirm the accuracy of the content or the legal validity of the document; the substance is assessed separately by the Turkish authority handling the matter.

### Where do I obtain an apostille for a document issued in Türkiye?

Applications are made to governorates for administrative documents and to judicial commission presidencies at centres with a heavy penal court for judicial documents. Misclassifying the nature of the document leads to refusal.

### My country is not a party to the Hague Convention — what should I do?

The legalisation chain then applies: the document is first certified by the competent authority of the issuing state (usually the foreign ministry) and then legalised by the Turkish consulate in that state. Under Article 224, the character of an official document in Türkiye arises from that certification.

### Can I have the translation made in my own country?

You can, but acceptance in Türkiye usually requires certification by the relevant Turkish consulate. In practice, the safest route is to bring the apostilled original to Türkiye and have it translated there by a sworn translator before a notary; this guarantees full compliance with the annotation requirements of Article 103 of the Notaries Law.
