TTK Art. 1199-1202 close out the freight-collection chapter by settling what happens to freight when cargo is lost, who actually owes it, and how the carrier can secure payment through a lien on the goods.

## No Freight for Cargo Lost by Accident, But an Exception for Its Own Nature

Under Art. 1199, freight is not owed — and must be refunded if prepaid — for cargo lost through an accident before the end of discharge time; for lump-sum freight, a partial loss triggers a proportional reduction. But cargo lost through its own inherent characteristics — spoilage, natural shrinkage, ordinary leakage, or livestock dying en route — still earns full freight regardless of delivery, because that loss reflects the cargo's nature rather than any shortfall in the carrier's performance. Cargo deliberately sacrificed for general average follows the separate general average cost-sharing regime instead.

## The Shipper Is the Default Freight Debtor

Art. 1200 states a short but load-bearing rule: the shipper who contracted with the carrier owes freight, regardless of who physically receives the cargo. Turkish courts apply this strictly — a consignee is not liable for freight merely by taking delivery; liability requires that the bill of lading or contract specifically authorizes the consignee to pay. This is why the next set of rules on third-party consignees (covered separately) had to be written: consignee liability is never automatic.

## The Lien: Scope, Duration, and Cash-Deposit Release

Under Art. 1201, the carrier holds a lien over the cargo for all freight-contract claims, so long as possession continues — and, remarkably, even for up to thirty days after delivery if court proceedings are filed in time and the consignee still holds the cargo. The lien only secures claims from that specific voyage, and is generally limited to cargo sufficient to cover the debt — except for general average and salvage claims, where the carrier may hold the entire cargo. Courts read this possession requirement strictly: a lien cannot attach to cargo the carrier never actually held, even if it belongs to the same debtor. Where the freight claim is disputed, Art. 1202 lets the carrier obtain release of the cargo once the disputed amount is deposited with the court, later withdrawing it against adequate security — a practical escrow that keeps trade moving without abandoning the carrier's security.

## Why This Matters

For carriers, these rules define exactly when payment is guaranteed and how to enforce it without releasing cargo prematurely; for shippers and consignees, they clarify who actually bears freight risk. Turkish case law consistently insists on documented authorization before a consignee can be held liable, and on actual possession before a lien can be enforced against any given cargo.
