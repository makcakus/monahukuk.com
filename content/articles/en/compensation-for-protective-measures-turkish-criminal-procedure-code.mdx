Criminal proceedings in Turkey routinely involve measures that restrict a person's liberty before any conviction is reached — arrest, police custody, pre-trial detention, judicial control conditions, and searches or seizures of property. These are known in Turkish law as "protective measures," and they are lawful tools that prosecutors and courts use to secure an investigation or trial. But when such a measure turns out to have been applied wrongly, kept in place too long, or followed by an acquittal or a decision not to prosecute, the person affected is not left without a remedy. Article 141 of the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (Ceza Muhakemesi Kanunu, "CMK"), together with Articles 142 and 144, creates a direct compensation claim against the State — a claim that foreign nationals facing criminal process in Turkey, and their families and counsel, are often unaware exists.

## What This Compensation Regime Covers

CMK Article 141 lists a defined set of situations in which a person may claim both pecuniary and non-pecuniary damages from the State. Rather than requiring proof that a judge or prosecutor was personally at fault, the provision creates a form of strict liability: the State answers for the consequences of protective measures once one of the listed situations is established, regardless of whether any individual official acted negligently or in bad faith. This matters because it removes the burden of proving personal misconduct — the claimant only needs to show that their case falls within one of the statutory categories.

The situations set out in Article 141 fall broadly into three groups. The first covers measures that were unlawful from the outset: being arrested or detained outside the conditions the law requires, not being brought before a judge within the legal custody period, being detained without having one's rights explained or without being allowed to exercise them, having the reasons for arrest or detention withheld, having one's family left uninformed of the arrest or detention, being subjected to a disproportionate search, or having property seized or mishandled without proper cause. The second covers cases where the measure began lawfully but the outcome later favored the person concerned — most importantly, a lawful arrest or detention followed by a decision not to prosecute or by an acquittal, including after judicial control conditions were imposed, and situations where time already spent in custody or detention exceeds the sentence eventually imposed. The third covers breach of the reasonable-time principle: a person lawfully detained who is not brought before the trial court within a reasonable period and whose case remains undecided during that time has an independent basis for a claim. A further, separate ground covers being denied access to the objection procedures the law provides against arrest, judicial control, or detention decisions — for example, the objection route available against detention orders.

## The Duty to Inform and the State's Liability

Where a decision favorable to the person is reached — a decision not to prosecute, an acquittal, or an acquittal following judicial control — the authority issuing that decision is required to notify the person that they have a right to compensation, and to record this in the decision itself. In practice, courts sometimes fail to include this notice in the reasoned judgment or the operative part of the decision, and this omission is a recurring ground for appellate correction: the appellate court will typically fix the defect directly rather than send the case back for a full retrial.

Because liability rests on the State's responsibility for exercising public power rather than on any individual's fault, the compensation claim is always brought against the State — never against the judge or prosecutor personally. This structural choice protects judicial independence: it shields judges and prosecutors from being sued directly by disappointed litigants over the decisions they make in the course of their duties. If a judge or prosecutor is later found to have acted improperly — in breach of the duties of their office — the State can recover what it paid from that official through a right of recourse it must exercise within one year of payment. This dual structure guarantees the victim's claim through the State's financial strength while still holding the individual official ultimately accountable.

## Deadlines, Competent Authority and Filing the Claim (CMK Article 142)

A compensation claim under this regime is subject to two cumulative time limits, both of a forfeiture nature: it must be filed within three months of the decision or judgment becoming final and being notified to the claimant, and in any event within one year of the date the decision or judgment became final. Missing either deadline results in the claim being dismissed without an examination of its merits, so calculating these dates correctly is critical.

As a general rule, jurisdiction lies with the Heavy Penal Court in the place where the claimant resides. There is an important carve-out, however: claims based on a lawful arrest or detention followed by acquittal, a decision not to prosecute, or acquittal after judicial control — corresponding to the second group of grounds described above — are not decided by the ordinary courts at all. Instead, they fall under the separate Compensation Commission established by Law No. 6384, a specialized body created specifically to handle this category of claims. Filing with the wrong authority can itself cause a claimant to lose their rights, so identifying which grounds apply is a necessary first step. A properly filed petition must identify the claimant, describe the measure and the nature and extent of the harm suffered, and attach supporting documents; if deficiencies are not corrected within one month, the petition is rejected, subject to a right of objection. The competent court examines the file, hears the State Treasury's representative, and decides after a hearing, with an appeal available against its decision.

## Situations Where Compensation Cannot Be Claimed (CMK Article 144)

The right created by Article 141 is not unconditional. Article 144 excludes certain categories of people — who were otherwise lawfully arrested, placed under judicial control, or detained — from claiming compensation even where the proceedings against them ultimately end favorably. The common thread running through these exclusions is that the favorable outcome did not result from the measure having been wrongly applied, but from a cause attributable to the person themselves or otherwise unconnected to the measure's legality: a later, more lenient law that retroactively changes the person's position, a general or special amnesty, withdrawal of a complaint, a settlement, suspension or discontinuation of the prosecution, a finding that the person lacked criminal capacity, or — most strikingly — a case where the person's own false statement to the judicial authorities, admitting to a crime they did not commit, caused their own arrest, judicial control, or detention. This last exclusion exists to prevent the compensation mechanism from being exploited by someone who brought the measure upon themselves through a knowingly false confession; it does not apply merely because a suspect later changes their account or maintains innocence throughout.

## Compensation Claims Against the State More Generally, and the Right of Recourse

Beyond the specific grounds listed in Article 141's first paragraph, any other claim arising from a judge's or prosecutor's decisions or acts during a criminal investigation or trial — whether based on personal fault, tort, or another basis of liability — can likewise only be brought against the State, not against the individual official. As noted above, the State that pays such compensation is not left to absorb the loss permanently: it is obliged to exercise its right of recourse against the judge or prosecutor within one year, where that official is found to have misused their office in breach of their duties. It is also important to understand that a compensation case is a separate proceeding from the criminal case itself. An acquittal or a decision not to prosecute in the underlying criminal matter does not automatically entitle the person to compensation — the conditions set out in Articles 141 and 144 must still be assessed concretely and independently in the compensation proceeding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to prove that the judge or prosecutor acted improperly to get compensation?**
No. Liability under Article 141 does not depend on proving personal fault by any official. It is enough to show that the situation falls within one of the statutory grounds — for example, an unlawful arrest or a subsequent acquittal following lawful detention. The claim is brought against the State, which answers regardless of individual fault.

**How long do I have to file a claim after my case ends?**
Two deadlines apply together: three months from when the final decision or judgment is notified to you, and in any case no later than one year from the date the decision became final. Missing either window generally results in dismissal without a review of the substance.

**Where do I file — an ordinary court or somewhere else?**
It depends on the ground for the claim. Most claims go to the Heavy Penal Court where the claimant resides. Claims based on a lawful arrest or detention followed by an acquittal, non-prosecution, or acquittal after judicial control instead go to the specialized Compensation Commission set up under Law No. 6384, not to the ordinary courts.

**If I falsely told the police I committed the crime and was later cleared, can I still claim compensation?**
Generally, no. If your own false statement to the judicial authorities — genuinely claiming to have committed or participated in the offense — caused your arrest, judicial control, or detention, the law excludes you from claiming compensation for that measure. This exclusion is narrow, however, and does not apply simply because you gave inconsistent statements or maintained your innocence.
