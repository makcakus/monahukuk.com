Article 1298 opens the "Salvage" chapter of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK Art. 1298-1319), which governs the reward owed to a person who assists a vessel or property in danger at sea. Unlike general average (a solidarity regime) or collision (a fault-based liability regime), salvage is an incentive regime: the law rewards a successful rescuer to encourage assistance in the first place. That logic is captured in maritime law's classic principle, "no cure, no pay" — introduced here for the first time in the series, and given its concrete statutory expression later in the chapter, at [Art. 1304](/articles/salvage-parties-obligations-and-salvage-award-principles-turkish-commercial-code).

## A Deliberately Broad Definition

Under Art. 1298(1), any act or activity carried out to assist a vessel or other property in danger in navigable waters constitutes salvage. The wording is intentionally open-ended — towing, anchoring, firefighting, dewatering, or even more limited interventions like guidance can qualify — because what matters is that the intervention was aimed at reducing or eliminating the danger and produced a useful result, not the specific technique used. The danger itself must be objective: a vessel that could resolve the situation using its own resources is not "in danger" for these purposes, a distinction that matters most in separating ordinary towage services from salvage.

## "Vessel" and "Property"

Art. 1298(2) defines "vessel" broadly to include any ship and any structure fit for navigation. "Property" covers anything not permanently and voluntarily fixed to the shore — plus, notably, unearned freight. Freight that is conditional on the cargo reaching its destination is itself an asset exposed to the same peril as the ship and cargo, so it can be "salved" in the same sense and factored into the award.

## What Falls Outside "Property" and "Salvage Activity"

Art. 1298(3) excludes two categories from "property": offshore platforms and drilling units while in use for extracting mineral resources (a specialized, separately regulated activity), and prehistoric, archaeological, or historically significant cultural artifacts on the seabed (kept out to avoid turning cultural heritage into a target for commercial "treasure hunting").

Art. 1298(4) excludes three situations from "salvage activity" altogether: (a) intervention against the clear and reasonable objection of the vessel's or property's owner or master; (b) efforts by the crew of the endangered vessel itself, since protecting the ship is already part of their job; and (c) services performed under a contract concluded before the danger arose — typically towage contracts, though case law confirms a towage service can convert into salvage if an unforeseen danger emerges and the response goes beyond what the towage contract covered.

## Practical Takeaway

For P&I clubs, shipowners, and salvage operators, the takeaway is that salvage status turns on voluntariness and exceptionality — a pre-existing legal or contractual duty, or crew action, generally will not support a salvage claim, but a service that starts as routine assistance can still ripen into salvage if the danger escalates beyond what was originally contracted.
