TTK Art. 743-747 address a niche but doctrinally important mechanism: letting a single bill of exchange be physically represented by more than one document, historically useful when a distant drawee needed the bill for acceptance while a duplicate stayed in circulation.

## Counterparts (Nüsha)

Under Art. 743, a drawer may issue a bill in several identical counterparts, but each must carry a consecutive serial number written into the text — without numbering, each counterpart is treated as a separate, independent bill, risking multiple undisclosed obligations from a single transaction. If the bill does not state it was drawn as a single original, the holder may request additional counterparts at their own expense, obliging the chain of endorsers to cooperate up to the drawer. Payment on any one counterpart discharges obligations on all of them (Art. 744) — except that a drawee remains liable on any counterpart bearing an acceptance notation that was never returned to them, since it may still be circulating with a bona fide third party. An endorser who distributes different counterparts to different people remains liable on each undelivered counterpart bearing their signature.

## The Acceptance Counterpart's Fate

Where one counterpart is sent for acceptance while another continues circulating, Art. 745 requires the sender to note who is holding the acceptance counterpart on the other counterparts, and requires that holder to deliver it to the rightful holder of the other counterpart on request. If delivery is refused, the holder can only pursue recourse after protesting both that the acceptance counterpart was not delivered on demand and that acceptance or payment could not be obtained on the counterpart in hand.

## Copies (Suret)

Unlike counterparts, any holder — without the drawer's involvement — may make a copy of a bill (Art. 746), provided it faithfully reproduces the original including all endorsements and shows where the original record ends. A properly formed copy can itself be endorsed and carry aval, just like the original. Art. 747 requires the copy to show who holds the original, obliges that holder to deliver it to the copy's rightful holder on demand (subject to the same protest safeguard as Art. 745), and allows the original to be marked "endorsements valid only on the copy from here on" — channeling further circulation exclusively through the copy and preventing two parallel, conflicting endorsement chains.

## Practical Takeaway

Counterparts and copies are rarely litigated today — modern banking has largely eliminated the practical need for physically duplicated instruments — but the mechanism remains relevant in cross-border transactions requiring long-distance acceptance.
