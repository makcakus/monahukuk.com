Foreign nationals facing criminal proceedings in Turkey — or their families and lawyers watching from abroad — often assume that any competent Turkish criminal court can hear a given case, so long as it has jurisdiction over criminal matters generally. Turkish procedural law does not work that way. The Criminal Procedure Code (CMK) draws a sharp line between two related but distinct concepts: which category and level of court is authorized to try a given offense (competence, "görev"), and which specific court, among courts of the same category, is territorially authorized to try it (jurisdiction, "yetki"). Both rules trace back to the constitutional guarantee of the natural, lawfully predetermined judge — the idea that a defendant should never be able to be shopped in front of a court chosen for convenience, and equally should never be tried before a court that was never legally empowered to hear the case at all. Getting this distinction wrong, or missing the narrow procedural window in which it can be challenged, can have real consequences for a defense.

## Competence: Which Type of Court Is Authorized

Competence answers a structural question: given the offense charged, is this a matter for a criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza) or a heavy penal court (ağır ceza)? The allocation is fixed by statute — principally the law establishing the first-instance judicial courts and regional courts of appeal, read together with the maximum penalties set out in the Turkish Penal Code — and it is not something the parties can alter by agreement. A defendant cannot consent to be tried by a court that lacks competence, and a court that discovers it lacks competence must raise the issue on its own motion, at any stage of the proceedings, without needing either party to object. No party acquires a vested right in an incorrect competence determination merely because the case has already progressed. If, after the indictment has been accepted, it becomes apparent that the case exceeds or falls outside the court's competence, the court must transfer the file to the competent court, and that transfer decision is itself open to challenge through an objection (itiraz). One practical limit exists: a court cannot declare itself incompetent and send a case down to a lower-level court merely because the legal characterization of the offense changed during trial — a safeguard against cases being repeatedly bounced between courts and the proceedings dragging on indefinitely.

## Territorial Jurisdiction: The Place of the Offense

Once competence is settled, jurisdiction asks a narrower question: among the courts of the correct type, which specific location has authority over this case? The default rule is that jurisdiction belongs to the court of the place where the offense was committed. This is more than an administrative convenience — trying a case where the offense occurred keeps evidence and witnesses accessible while they are still fresh, and it embodies the natural-judge guarantee in concrete form: a person generally knows, at the moment they act, which pre-existing court would have authority over that place, which prevents the outcome of a case from being engineered through selective court-shopping after the fact. The general rule has built-in variations for less straightforward scenarios: for an attempted offense, jurisdiction follows the place where the last act of execution occurred; for a continuing offense, the place where the unlawful conduct ceased; and for a series of offenses treated as a single continuing crime, the place of the final act in the series. Offenses committed through a printed publication distributed nationally follow a special rule tied to the place where the publication is centrally issued, with the printing location also gaining jurisdiction if that particular print run was produced away from the publication's central place of issue. Turkish appellate case law has also confirmed that a later administrative reorganization of a public prosecutor's territorial reach does not retroactively strip a court of jurisdiction that had already vested at the time the offense was committed — any other reading would undermine the natural-judge principle itself.

## When the Place of the Offense Cannot Be Determined

The law anticipates that the place of an offense will not always be obvious, and it supplies a cascading fallback sequence rather than leaving the question open. Where the place of the offense cannot be established, jurisdiction passes, in order, to the court of the place where the suspect or defendant was apprehended; failing that, their place of residence; failing that, their last known address in Turkey; and, if none of those can be established either, the place where the first procedural act in the case was carried out. The same fallback sequence governs offenses committed abroad that must be prosecuted in Turkey; if the suspect or defendant has not been apprehended, has no residence, and no address can be identified in Turkey, the Court of Cassation determines the competent court at the request of the Minister of Justice. Offenses committed on, or by means of, sea, air or rail transport are handled separately: jurisdiction lies with the court of the place of the first Turkish port the vessel reached, or the port where it is registered. Where related criminal cases fall within the territorial reach of different courts, they may be consolidated and heard together before one of those courts; if the courts involved cannot agree on consolidation, a shared higher court decides whether joining the cases is warranted.

## Resolving Disputes Between Prosecutors and Courts

A recurring practical problem arises when one public prosecutor's office declines jurisdiction and forwards a file to another office, which in turn also declines it — a negative jurisdictional conflict. The law addresses this directly: when a prosecutor's office that received a file after a declination of jurisdiction also considers itself lacking jurisdiction, it must send the file to the heavy penal court nearest to the one in whose district it operates, so that court can determine which prosecutor's office actually has jurisdiction; that determination is final and not subject to further appeal. A comparable mechanism exists for disputes directly between judges or courts — whether the dispute is "positive" (two courts both claim jurisdiction) or "negative" (both decline it) — in which case a shared higher court identifies the judge or court that must actually hear the matter.

## The Deadline for Raising a Jurisdictional Objection

Unlike competence, which is treated as a matter the court must police at every stage on its own initiative, an objection to territorial jurisdiction is subject to a firm cut-off point, after which it can no longer be raised by anyone — including the court itself. A defendant must raise a jurisdictional objection before being formally questioned at first instance, before review begins at a regional court of appeal, or, in cases handled by written review, before the examination report is read out; a court's own decision declining jurisdiction must likewise be issued before those same points. Once that stage has passed, neither the defendant nor the court may revisit the jurisdiction question — jurisdiction, unlike competence, is not treated as an absolute matter of public order, but as a limited procedural objection that expires with the progress of the case. This time limit exists to prevent jurisdictional challenges from being used late in proceedings to disrupt an otherwise advanced trial, and the same rule extends by reference to minor offenses (kabahatler) under the Misdemeanors Law. As with decisions on competence, a decision on jurisdiction can be challenged through an objection, allowing an erroneous jurisdictional finding to be corrected before the case reaches the merits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a defendant simply agree to be tried in a more convenient Turkish court?**
No. Competence rules are treated as a matter of public order and cannot be altered by agreement between the parties, however convenient that might be for a foreign defendant, their counsel, or the prosecution. A court that lacks competence must recognize this on its own and transfer the case.

**What happens if a jurisdictional objection is not raised early enough?**
It is lost. Once questioning has begun at first instance (or the equivalent stage on appellate review), neither the defendant nor the court can raise or decide a jurisdictional objection. This makes early legal advice, ideally before or immediately at the first hearing, particularly important for anyone unfamiliar with Turkish procedure.

**If the offense location is unclear, does that mean no Turkish court can hear the case?**
No. The law provides a fallback sequence — the place of apprehension, then residence, then last known address in Turkey, then the place of the first procedural step — so that a court can always be identified even when the exact location of the offense is disputed or unknown.

**Can a decision on competence or jurisdiction be appealed immediately?**
Yes. Both a competence decision and a jurisdiction decision can be challenged through an objection (itiraz) before the case proceeds further, allowing an incorrect court determination to be corrected without waiting for a final judgment on the merits.
