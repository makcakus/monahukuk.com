For a foreigner facing criminal proceedings in Turkey, few moments are as disorienting as learning that a judge can order pretrial detention before any trial, and often before any indictment exists. Turkish law treats this measure as the most severe interference with personal liberty available in criminal procedure, and it surrounds the decision with cumulative conditions set out in the Criminal Procedure Code (CMK), primarily Article 100. Detention is meant to be the exception, not the default: release is the rule, and a judge may only depart from it when several distinct legal thresholds are met at once. Understanding those thresholds — and the procedural safeguards that keep detention under continuous review — is essential for defendants, their families, and any lawyer coordinating with Turkish counsel.

## The Threshold of "Strong Suspicion"

Turkish law does not require the same certainty for detention that it requires for a conviction. A criminal court can only convict once every reasonable doubt has been eliminated, but detention rests on a lower, intermediate standard usually translated as "strong suspicion of guilt." That said, this standard is still meaningfully higher than a mere hunch or an unverified tip. The law is explicit that this suspicion must rest on concrete evidence in the file — a 2014 amendment specifically replaced looser statutory language referring to "facts" with a requirement of "concrete evidence," precisely to prevent detention orders built on abstract assumptions or unverified intelligence. In practice, judges retain discretion in weighing the file's evidence, but that discretion is not immune from review: an appellate or constitutional court can and does examine whether the finding of strong suspicion is actually anchored in something concrete in the case file, rather than simply asserted.

## The Two Recognized Grounds for Detention

Strong suspicion alone is never enough. The law also requires an independent "ground for detention," and Article 100(2) recognizes two categories of such grounds. The first is a real risk of flight — concrete circumstances suggesting the suspect has fled, is hiding, or is likely to abscond. The second is a real risk of interference with the investigation: concrete indications that the suspect is destroying, concealing or altering evidence, or attempting to intimidate witnesses, victims or other people. Critically, the statute uses permissive language: even when a judge finds these circumstances present, detention does not follow automatically — the judge must still evaluate the specific facts of the case. When assessing flight risk in particular, Turkish and European Court of Human Rights case law both look to concrete indicators such as a fixed residence, family and social ties, and travel history, rather than allowing the mere severity of the potential sentence to serve, by itself, as an abstract presumption of flight.

## The Statutory Catalogue of Serious Offenses, and the Floor Below Which Detention Is Barred

Article 100(3) lists a catalogue of offenses for which a detention ground may be presumed once strong suspicion is established, without separate proof of flight or evidence-tampering risk. This catalogue covers the most serious categories in the Turkish Criminal Code and related special legislation — offenses such as genocide and crimes against humanity, human trafficking and migrant smuggling, intentional homicide, torture, sexual assault and sexual abuse of minors, aggravated theft and robbery, drug trafficking, forming a criminal organization, offenses against state security and the constitutional order, and a number of others enumerated by name. Even within this catalogue, however, Turkish Court of Cassation case law consistently holds that the proportionality test is never switched off — a judge must still separately assess whether detention is actually necessary in the specific case, even for a listed offense.

At the opposite end of the spectrum sits an absolute statutory floor: under Article 100(4), detention cannot be ordered at all for offenses punishable only by a judicial fine, or for offenses whose maximum prison term does not exceed two years — unless the offense was an intentional one committed against bodily integrity. This is not a matter of judicial discretion; it is a categorical legal bar that removes detention entirely from the table for minor offenses.

## Who Decides, and the Mandatory Reasoning Requirement

During the investigation stage, a magistrate judge (sulh ceza hâkimi) orders detention on the prosecutor's request; once the case reaches trial, the court itself decides, either on the prosecutor's motion or on its own initiative. The law is unusually detailed about what the decision must contain: both the prosecutor's request and the judge's or court's order must set out, with reference to concrete facts in the file, the existence of strong suspicion, the existence of a detention ground, why detention is a proportionate measure, and why the lighter alternative of judicial supervision (a bail-like regime of reporting and travel restrictions rather than physical custody) would be insufficient. Turkish appellate practice has repeatedly struck down boilerplate reasoning — formulaic phrases repeated from one file to the next without engaging with the specific case — as failing to meet this requirement; a defectively reasoned detention order can itself be found unlawful and can ground a later compensation claim. A suspect or defendant facing a detention request is also entitled, as a matter of course, to the assistance of defense counsel, whether privately retained or assigned by the local bar association.

## Maximum Duration and Periodic Review

Detention is not open-ended. The law sets different maximum periods depending on which court would have jurisdiction over the underlying offense. For offenses that fall outside the jurisdiction of the heavy penal courts, the ceiling is one year, extendable by a further six months for compelling, specifically reasoned circumstances. For offenses within the jurisdiction of the heavy penal courts, the base ceiling is two years, extendable in principle up to a total of three years — or, for organized-crime and terrorism-related offenses, up to five years. A separate cap applies specifically to the investigation stage (as opposed to the trial stage), guaranteeing that an investigation cannot be dragged out indefinitely before charges are even filed. These periods are reduced for minors: to half for those under fifteen at the time of the offense, and to three-quarters for those under eighteen. Where a single case file involves multiple charges, Turkish Court of Cassation precedent holds that the maximum period is calculated once for the file as a whole, not separately for each offense — the number of defendants or charges and the case's complexity may be weighed in assessing reasonableness, but they do not change the statutory ceiling itself.

Detention is also never a fixed, one-time determination. A suspect or defendant may request release at any stage of the investigation or trial, and such a request must be decided within three days (seven days for organized-crime offenses). Independently of any request, the law requires periodic review on the court's own motion — at least every thirty days during the investigation stage, and at every hearing during trial — so that continued detention is never simply rubber-stamped without a fresh look at whether the original grounds still hold. Every one of these decisions can be challenged through an objection (itiraz) to a higher court, which keeps the entire process open to review at each stage.

## Proportionality Review Under ECHR and Constitutional Standards

The European Court of Human Rights, in cases concerning Turkey, has repeatedly held that the seriousness of the alleged offense and the severity of the anticipated sentence cannot, by themselves, justify continued detention. Each case requires a fresh, concrete assessment of flight risk, risk of evidence tampering, and whether the proceedings are being conducted with due diligence. Turkish Constitutional Court and Court of Cassation case law tracks this same approach: a detention measure is only proportionate where the record concretely shows that judicial supervision would be inadequate — otherwise, the balance between individual liberty and the public interest is treated as disturbed. This proportionality check is not limited to the moment detention is first ordered; every subsequent decision extending or continuing detention must independently confirm that all three elements — strong suspicion, a detention ground, and proportionality — remain current.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a suspect be detained solely because the charge carries a heavy potential sentence?**
No. Turkish and European Court of Human Rights case law both reject the idea that the severity of the potential sentence, standing alone, can justify detention or its continuation. A concrete, case-specific risk of flight or evidence tampering must also be shown.

**Is there any offense for which detention is legally impossible?**
Yes. Under Article 100(4), detention cannot be ordered for offenses carrying only a judicial fine, or for offenses with a maximum prison term of two years or less, unless the offense was an intentional one committed against bodily integrity.

**How long can pretrial detention last in Turkey?**
It depends on the court with jurisdiction over the offense: up to one year (extendable to eighteen months) for offenses outside the heavy penal courts' jurisdiction, and up to two years (extendable to three, or five for organized-crime and terrorism offenses) for offenses within their jurisdiction. Separate, shorter limits apply specifically to the investigation stage.

**Does being charged with an offense on the statutory catalogue in Article 100(3) make detention automatic?**
No. For catalogue offenses, a detention ground may be presumed once strong suspicion is shown, but Turkish courts still require a separate, case-specific finding that detention is proportionate and that judicial supervision would be insufficient.
