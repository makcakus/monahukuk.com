Turkish criminal law treats the formation of an armed organization as a distinct and more serious offense than ordinary organized crime, and it punishes the supply of weapons to such an organization, as well as an agreement between two or more people to commit a serious offense, as freestanding crimes in their own right — even before any underlying act is carried out. These offenses, set out in Articles 314 through 316 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), sit immediately after the chapters dealing with crimes against state security and the constitutional order, and they are frequently the basis of large, multi-defendant prosecutions in Turkey's heavy penal courts. For foreign counsel encountering these charges for the first time, understanding how Articles 314-316 relate to the general organized-crime offense in Article 220, and what evidentiary threshold Turkish courts actually apply to membership and conspiracy allegations, is essential to assessing exposure and building a defense.

## What Interest the Law Protects

Articles 314 to 316 appear directly after the sections of the Penal Code that criminalize offenses against state security and the functioning of the constitutional order. The legislative logic connecting them is that organized structures and preparatory conduct which make those more serious offenses easier to carry out are punished on their own, without waiting for an actual offense to occur. The interest being protected is not any individual victim but the security of the state and the orderly functioning of its constitutional institutions. For that reason, all three offenses are classified as abstract-danger offenses: no actual harm or even a concrete risk of harm needs to be shown, and the underlying crimes the organization is aiming at do not need to have been attempted. It is worth noting that a paragraph added to Article 314 in 2024 was later struck down by the Constitutional Court, so the article currently in force is limited to the provisions on founding or leading an organization, membership, and the cross-reference to the general organized-crime rules.

## How Article 314 Differs From the General Organized-Crime Offense in Article 220

Article 220 of the Penal Code is the general provision covering the formation of an organization for the purpose of committing crimes, and it does not limit the kind of offense the organization must be aiming at. Article 314 narrows that general offense in two specific ways: the organization's purpose must be one of the particular offenses against state security or the constitutional order found in the relevant chapters of the Code, and the organization must additionally be armed. Because both conditions must be present together, Article 314 operates as the specific provision (lex specialis) relative to Article 220, and under the ordinary rule governing the relationship between general and specific provisions, only Article 314 applies once its conditions are met — there is no separate, additional conviction under Article 220. The more difficult question in practice is what "armed" actually requires. It does not mean that every member of the organization must personally carry a weapon; it is enough that the organization, viewed as a whole, has the capacity to acquire, use, and resort to weapons in pursuit of its aims. Article 314 expressly imports the general rules developed for the Article 220 organized-crime offense — including the criteria for identifying leadership, hierarchical structure, and the existence of an organization as such — so that body of law continues to apply. What is unique to Article 314, and absent from Article 220, is the armed element; when a structure cannot be shown to have reached that level of armament, prosecutors and courts typically fall back on the general Article 220 offense instead.

## The Conduct Punished: Founding, Leading and Membership

Article 314 criminalizes three distinct forms of conduct: founding an organization, leading it, and being a member of it. Founding and leading carry the heavier penalty under the first paragraph, while membership is punished separately and less severely under the second. No special status is required of the offender — anyone can commit this offense — and the victim, again, is not an individual but public order and state security as such. For a structure to qualify as an "organization" at all, it must show some degree of continuity over time, a recognizable hierarchy, and a membership and level of resources genuinely capable of carrying out the offenses it is aimed at; a loose or temporary association of people without any real organizational intent does not meet that threshold.

Proving membership is where most of the litigation actually happens. Turkish courts have consistently held that sympathy for an organization's cause, reading its publications, or attending its meetings or demonstrations does not, by itself, establish membership. What is required is concrete evidence that the individual voluntarily integrated into the organization's hierarchical structure and acted within it through conduct that is continuous, varied and sufficiently intense to show organizational participation, rather than isolated or occasional contact. This evidentiary standard exists precisely to keep the line between protected expression and association on one hand, and criminal liability on the other, from collapsing — otherwise, an expression of opinion or mere sympathy risks being treated as if it were membership itself, which would be disproportionate to the conduct actually punished.

## Arms Supply Under Article 315 and Criminal Conspiracy Under Article 316

Article 315 punishes, as a separate offense, producing, purchasing, importing, transporting or storing weapons intended for the use of an armed organization as defined in Article 314, where the person doing so knows the purpose the weapons will serve. This provision reaches people who are not necessarily members of the organization but who materially contribute to its ability to arm itself, cutting off logistical support that might otherwise fall outside the membership offense. Because knowledge of the organization's purpose is a required element, this offense can only be committed intentionally — negligent supply of weapons does not fall within it. A point debated in Turkish legal writing is whether a person who is both a member of the organization and personally supplies it with weapons can be convicted of both offenses at once; the prevailing view is that where the arms supply is not simply an incidental part of membership but a separate and additional act of execution, the ordinary rules on concurrence of offenses require separate convictions for both.

Article 316 defines a distinct offense of agreement: where two or more people agree, using means genuinely capable of achieving the result, to commit one of the offenses in the relevant chapters of the Code, that agreement is itself treated as a completed crime. This is an unusual departure from the general Turkish rule that mere preparatory acts are not punishable — here, the legislature has chosen to punish the agreement itself, without requiring that the intended offense actually be attempted. What matters is that the agreement is concrete, serious, and backed by means genuinely capable of carrying it out, rather than a casual or purely verbal statement of intent. A second paragraph of Article 316 provides a form of active repentance: anyone who withdraws from the arrangement before the intended offense is committed and before any investigation into the agreement has started is not punished — a policy choice that mirrors the voluntary-abandonment rule found elsewhere in the Penal Code, aimed at giving participants an incentive to step back before matters progress further.

## Intent, Attempt, and How These Charges Combine With Other Offenses

All three offenses can only be committed intentionally. For Article 314 specifically, the founder or leader must be aware both of which offenses the organization is aimed at and of its armed character. Founding and leading an organization are considered complete once the organization comes into existence, which leaves limited room for argument about an unfinished attempt. Where a member commits a specific offense in furtherance of the organization's aims, Turkish practice convicts that person separately both for membership and for the underlying offense actually committed — a straightforward application of the general rule on the concurrence of offenses, reinforced by the express cross-reference Article 314 makes to Article 220. Procedurally, none of these offenses require a complaint from a victim; they are prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office as a matter of course, fall within the jurisdiction of the heavy penal courts given their gravity and organizational character, and the evidence relied on is typically technical surveillance material, cooperating-witness statements, and digital records rather than testimony from an identifiable injured party.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it enough to show sympathy for an organization to be convicted of membership under Article 314?**
No. Turkish case law is clear that expressing sympathy, reading an organization's materials, or attending its meetings or demonstrations does not by itself amount to membership. Courts require concrete evidence of voluntary integration into the organization's hierarchy through continuous and substantial conduct.

**Can someone be prosecuted under both Article 314 and Article 220 for the same organization?**
No. Article 314 is the specific provision that applies whenever the organization's purpose falls within the relevant offenses and the organization is armed. Where those conditions are met, only Article 314 applies; Article 220, the general organized-crime offense, does not apply on top of it.

**Does the agreement offense under Article 316 require that the planned crime actually be attempted?**
No. Article 316 punishes the agreement itself as a completed offense, provided it is concrete and backed by means capable of carrying it out. The underlying offense does not need to be attempted, though a person who withdraws before it is committed and before any investigation begins is not punished.

**If someone is both a member of an armed organization and personally supplies it with weapons, can they be convicted of both offenses?**
This is debated, but the prevailing position is that they can, provided the arms supply is a separate and additional act rather than something inherent to ordinary membership — in which case the general rules on concurrence of offenses apply.
