Turkey criminalizes organ and tissue trafficking through Article 91 of the Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237), a provision that reaches far beyond the physical removal of an organ without consent. It also targets buyers, sellers, brokers, custodians, transporters, and even advertisers who participate anywhere along the chain that turns a human organ or tissue into a commodity. For medical professionals, foreign clinics referring patients to Turkey, and international counsel handling cross-border transplant arrangements, understanding how broadly this article is drafted — and how narrowly its one escape route is construed by the courts — is essential to assessing criminal exposure in Turkey.

## What Article 91 Actually Covers

Article 91 sits within the Penal Code's chapter on offenses against bodily inviolability, and it is built around a list of distinct acts rather than a single offense. Removing an organ or tissue from a living person without legally valid consent is punished more heavily when the object is an organ than when it is tissue, reflecting the greater physiological impact of losing an organ. Separately, and less severely, the law punishes removing an organ or tissue from a deceased person unlawfully. A third category targets the commercial side directly: selling, buying, or brokering the sale of an organ or tissue is punished at the same level as the unauthorized removal itself, regardless of who actually performs the extraction. A further provision criminalizes storing, transporting, or implanting an organ or tissue known to have been obtained unlawfully — an offense aimed at everyone downstream of the original wrongdoing, including clinical staff who receive the material without asking the right questions. Finally, publishing or issuing an advertisement seeking to obtain an organ or tissue in exchange for a benefit is a stand-alone offense, even if no transaction ever follows.

The legislature also built in two aggravating rules. If any of the buying, selling, brokering, or unauthorized-removal conduct is carried out through the activity of an organized group, the applicable sentence increases substantially, reflecting the heightened danger of trafficking networks that operate on a continuing, often cross-border basis. And if the victim dies as a result of an unlawful organ removal, the case is no longer treated under Article 91 at all — the homicide provisions of the Penal Code take over entirely.

## Why the Law Treats the Human Body This Way

The provision protects more than physical integrity. At the most immediate level, it protects a person's control over their own body against invasive removal carried out without genuine consent. Beyond that, it embodies the principle that the human body — and any part separated from it — is not a form of property that the legal system will treat as tradeable, regardless of what the person concerned might wish. A third interest is systemic: transplantation medicine depends on a regulated, verifiable, and medically supervised process, and black-market organ deals corrode public trust in that system. This is why the law criminalizes the sale of an organ even when the person conveys full and voluntary agreement to the transaction — consent to a sale does not neutralize the offense, because what the law is protecting is not reducible to the individual's own preference.

## Consent, and Why a Paid Transaction Cannot Cure It

The unauthorized-removal offense turns entirely on whether the person who gave up the organ or tissue gave legally valid consent. For consent to count, it must come from someone with the legal capacity to give it, be given after the person was properly informed about the nature of the organ involved and the consequences of the procedure, and follow the form and procedure that transplant regulations require. If any one of those elements is missing, the removal is treated as if no consent existed at all. Critically, Turkish law treats consent given in exchange for payment as inherently defective for this purpose: an agreement reached because someone needed the money is not viewed as a free exercise of will, and the underlying transaction is itself independently criminalized as organ or tissue trafficking. This is a deliberate policy choice — it removes any argument that a seller's own willingness legitimizes the deal, and it is the reason the buying, selling, and brokering offense exists as a separate charge layered on top of the consent analysis.

The offense is treated as complete at different points depending on the conduct involved. Removal-based conduct is complete once the organ or tissue separates from the body. The buying, selling, or brokering offense, however, is treated as complete once the parties reach an agreement for a benefit in exchange for the organ or tissue — Turkish appellate practice has confirmed that neither payment nor the physical transfer needs to occur for the offense to be finished. Storage, transport, implantation, and advertising offenses are each complete once that specific act occurs, independent of whether an unauthorized removal or sale ever took place.

## The Special Position of the Seller: Sentence Reduction and Effective Repentance

Turkish law treats the person who sells their own organ or tissue as occupying a distinct position from everyone else involved in a trafficking scheme. Article 92 of the Penal Code allows a court, taking into account the seller's social and economic circumstances, to reduce the sentence or to forgo imposing any penalty on the seller at all. This is not a general defense available to buyers, brokers, or intermediaries — it is a personal, seller-specific allowance built on the premise that a person driven to sell part of their own body by poverty is, in a meaningful sense, also a victim of the exploitation the law is trying to stop.

Article 93 goes a step further and offers the seller a route to effective repentance. If the seller reports the matter to the authorities before the offense comes to official attention, and that report facilitates the capture of the other offenders, no penalty at all is imposed on the seller. If the offense has already come to light but the seller voluntarily assists in exposing the offense and helping catch the remaining offenders afterward, the sentence is reduced — by an amount ranging from one quarter to one half, depending on how substantial the assistance was. Both mechanisms apply only to the seller, not to buyers, brokers, organized-group participants, or anyone on the storage-transport-implantation side of the offense.

Turkish courts have been notably strict about keeping Article 92 within its intended lane. In a case involving a kidney transplant arranged through brokers for a payment of 35,000 Turkish lira, the Court of Cassation's General Assembly on Criminal Matters held that the general necessity defense under Article 25 of the Penal Code cannot be imported into organ trafficking cases beyond the narrow allowance Article 92 already gives the seller — extending a broader necessity defense, the court reasoned, would end up encouraging the very conduct the law is designed to suppress. In separate rulings, the Court of Cassation's 12th Criminal Chamber has likewise rejected attempts by sellers to avoid punishment under Article 92 where the seller had brought on their own financial difficulty through their own choices and had other ways of addressing it — for instance, debt from an unrelated purchase — holding that Article 92 must be applied cautiously enough that it does not blunt enforcement against organ trafficking, and specifically noting that the seller's medical circumstances (such as being a kidney patient in need of a transplant) must be independently verified through a formal forensic medical report before any necessity-based leniency can even be considered.

## Sentencing, Aggravating Circumstances, and Corporate Liability

The applicable sentence varies by which specific conduct is at issue. Unauthorized removal of an organ from a living person carries the heaviest range; removal of tissue instead of an organ carries a materially lower range, reflecting the different degree of bodily harm involved. Unlawful removal from a deceased person carries a comparatively light sentence. Buying, selling, or brokering a sale is punished at the same level as unauthorized removal of a living person's organ, regardless of whether the buyer, seller, or broker personally touched the removal process. Storing, transporting, or implanting unlawfully obtained material carries its own intermediate range, and advertising for organs or tissue in exchange for a benefit carries the lightest sentence in the article. Where any of the removal, buying, selling, or brokering conduct is carried out through an organized group, the sentence rises sharply, and a substantial judicial fine is added on top of imprisonment. Where the offense is committed within the activity of a legal entity — for example, a clinic or intermediary company — the entity itself is subject to the security measures the Penal Code reserves for corporate liability, separate from the individual criminal responsibility of the people who acted.

This is an offense prosecuted on the state's own initiative; it is not contingent on a victim's complaint, and it falls outside the categories of offenses eligible for mediation between victim and offender. That has practical consequences for anyone drawn into a criminal investigation on this basis: withdrawal of a complaint, or a private settlement with the other side, does not cause the case to be dropped.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the buyer or broker in an organ sale get the benefit of Article 92's sentence reduction?**
No. Article 92 is written specifically for the person who sells their own organ or tissue. Buyers, brokers, and anyone involved in storage, transport, implantation, advertising, or organized-group activity are excluded from this allowance entirely.

**If a patient genuinely needed the transplant to survive, can that be raised as a defense to the payment itself?**
Turkish courts have consistently rejected using the general necessity defense to excuse participation in organ trafficking beyond what Article 92 already provides for sellers, and even Article 92 has been denied where the person's own conduct contributed to the financial pressure that led to the sale, or where other options existed. A genuine medical necessity must be documented through an official forensic medical report before it carries any weight, and even then it does not extend to buyers or intermediaries.

**Is an organ trafficking deal already a crime before any organ actually changes hands?**
Yes, for the buying, selling, and brokering offense. Turkish appellate courts treat this offense as complete once the parties agree on a benefit in exchange for an organ or tissue — payment and physical transfer are not required for the offense to be finished.

**Can a victim's family end the prosecution by withdrawing a complaint or settling privately?**
No. This offense is prosecuted on the state's own initiative and is not subject to victim-offender mediation, so withdrawing a complaint or reaching a private settlement does not cause the criminal case to be dropped.
