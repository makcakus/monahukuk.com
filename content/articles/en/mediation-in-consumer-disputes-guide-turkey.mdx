A defective product, a cancelled package tour, an unfairly charged file fee — in consumer disputes the first question is usually: "do I go to the arbitration committee, to a mediator, or straight to court?"

The answer depends on the monetary size and the subject matter of your claim. This guide explains which route is open in which situation, and the special protections introduced in the consumer's favour.

## First, Choose the Right Route

Turkish law provides three separate routes for consumer disputes, and they are not alternatives but **successive stages**.

**1. The consumer arbitration committee.** Application is compulsory in disputes below the monetary threshold. The thresholds are reassessed each year and published in the Official Gazette; the current figure should be followed from the Ministry of Trade's announcements. In disputes within that scope, **mediation is not used.**

**2. Mediation.** In disputes above the arbitration committee's jurisdictional threshold and falling within the consumer court's jurisdiction, applying to a mediator before bringing an action is compulsory.

**3. The consumer court.** The action is brought once the mediation stage has been completed.

Taking the wrong route costs time: just as there is no need to go to mediation in a dispute within the arbitration committee's competence, skipping mediation and bringing an action in a dispute above the threshold leads to procedural dismissal.

## Scope: The Court Criterion

Article 73/A of Law No. 6502: in **disputes heard before the consumer courts**, having applied to a mediator before bringing an action is a condition of action.

The criterion here is not the type of action but whether the dispute falls within the jurisdiction of the consumer court. Under Article 73/1 of the Consumer Protection Law, every dispute that may arise from consumer transactions and practices directed at consumers falls within the consumer court's jurisdiction:

- defective goods and defective services;
- instalment sales, pre-paid housing sales, timeshares, package tours;
- consumer credit and housing finance contracts, file fees and insurance refunds;
- subscription contracts;
- distance contracts and contracts concluded off business premises, the right of withdrawal;
- after-sales services and warranties.

## The Five Exceptions

Cases in which the mediation condition is **not required**:

**(a) Disputes within the competence of the consumer arbitration committee.** Those below the monetary threshold.

**(b) Objections to decisions of the consumer arbitration committee.** No mediation is required for an objection lodged with the consumer court against a committee decision.

**(c) The actions in Article 73/6 of the Consumer Protection Law.** Prevention, cessation and declaratory actions brought by consumer organisations, the relevant public institutions and organisations and the Ministry, concerning consumers generally.

**(d) The actions in Article 74 of the Consumer Protection Law.** Actions concerning the determination and prevention of unfair terms.

**(e) Disputes which are in the nature of a consumer transaction and arise from a right in rem over immovable property.** This is the exception most frequently confused in practice.

## The Right-in-Rem Exception: The Critical Distinction

In a dispute arising from the sale of a dwelling, the distinction is drawn as follows:

**Outside the scope (no mediation required):** claims concerning the **right in rem** over the property, such as cancellation of title and registration, transfer of ownership of immovable property, or creation of a right in rem over it.

**Within the scope (mediation required):** monetary claims in the nature of a **personal right** arising from the same sale, such as delay compensation, price reduction for defects, or restitution of the price following rescission.

The 3rd Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation assesses the condition of action by drawing this distinction in disputes arising from real-estate sale contracts in the nature of a consumer transaction. Actions brought for delayed delivery in pre-paid housing sales are the field in which this distinction is applied most often.

## Three Protections in the Consumer's Favour

The mediation regime in consumer disputes differs markedly from the others.

**The non-attendance sanction is not applied against the consumer.** Article 73/A/2 of the Consumer Protection Law provides this expressly: the sanction in Article 18/A/11 of Law No. 6325 — under which a party who fails without excuse to attend the first meeting is liable for half the litigation costs even if successful, and an attorney's fee is awarded against them — cannot be operated against the consumer. For the seller or supplier it applies as usual.

In practice this means, for the consumer: even if you do not attend the session, you face no financial sanction. Attending is nonetheless in your interest, because what is said at the table cannot be used as evidence in the action and the possibility of settlement arises.

**The fee is met from the budget of the Ministry of Justice.** Article 73/A/3: in all cases — the parties cannot be reached, there is no attendance, **settlement** or non-settlement — the mediation fee payable by the consumer is met from the Ministry's budget. The ceiling is the amount of the two-hour fee under the First Part of the Tariff.

This protection is broader than the general rule: in other disputes the Ministry steps in only where no settlement is reached, whereas in consumer disputes the consumer's share is met from the budget **even where a settlement is reached**.

**The fee is collected from the defendant.** If the action concludes in the consumer's favour, the mediation fee met from the budget is collected from the defendant under Law No. 6183 and recorded as budget revenue.

## The Competent Bureau: Your Own Place of Residence

Under Article 73/5 of the Consumer Protection Law, consumer actions may also be brought before the consumer court **of the place where the consumer is domiciled**.

Since the mediation bureau's jurisdiction follows the court's, the consumer may apply to the mediation bureau at their own place of residence. This means the session can be held in your own city even if the other party is a large company headquartered in Istanbul.

If the other party objects to that jurisdiction, the objection must be raised at the latest at the first meeting with the relevant documents submitted; the civil court of peace decides finally within one week.

## Time Limit and Limitation

The general rule applies: the mediator concludes the process within **three weeks** of the date of appointment; in compulsory circumstances this may be extended by a maximum of one week.

In the period from the application until the date the final minutes are drawn up, **limitation is suspended and no forfeiture period runs**. That matters for the limitation periods running under Articles 12 and 16 of the Consumer Protection Law in relation to defective goods and services.

## The Settlement Document and Unfair-Term Review

If a settlement is reached, the document signed jointly by the parties and their lawyers together with the mediator is deemed a document having the character of a judgment without an annotation. If the consumer has no lawyer, an annotation must be obtained.

There is a point of attention specific to consumer disputes: the protective provisions introduced in the consumer's favour by Law No. 6502 are largely mandatory, and the consumer cannot waive in advance rights that have not yet arisen. General phrases such as "the consumer waives all future rights" should therefore be avoided in the settlement document; such a clause is both unnecessary and a source of later invalidity arguments.

By contrast, a settlement concerning a **claim that has arisen and is determined** is valid. Showing the items one by one in the document clarifies that distinction; and since the court's review in the annotation-of-enforceability examination is limited to the settlement's amenability to mediation and to enforcement, ensuring clarity of scope is entirely the parties' responsibility.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Can I go to a mediator in a dispute below the arbitration committee threshold?

Not as a condition of action; in such disputes you must apply to the arbitration committee. The parties may resort to voluntary mediation if they wish, but it must not be forgotten that the settlement reached will be binding and that no further claim may be made on the matters agreed.

### Q: I am going to object to an arbitration committee decision — is mediation required?

No. Objections to decisions of the consumer arbitration committee are expressly excepted under Article 73/A/1(b) of the Consumer Protection Law.

### Q: Will I pay a mediation fee?

As a rule, no. Under Article 73/A/3, in all cases — the parties cannot be reached, there is no attendance, settlement or non-settlement — the fee payable by the consumer is met from the budget of the Ministry of Justice; the ceiling is the amount of the two-hour fee.

### Q: What happens if I do not attend the session?

The non-attendance sanction is not applied against the consumer. But attending is in your interest: what is said at the table cannot be used as evidence in the action, and the process is costless for the consumer.

### Q: My home was delivered late — which route should I take?

If your claim is for a monetary sum such as delay compensation, it falls within the consumer court's jurisdiction and the mediation condition applies. If it is a claim concerning the right in rem over the property, such as cancellation of title and registration, mediation is not required under Article 73/A/1(d).
