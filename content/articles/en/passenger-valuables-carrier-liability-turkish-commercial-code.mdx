Sea carriers already answer for a passenger's ordinary baggage under a fault-based regime. Art. 1260 carves out a narrower, stricter rule for one special category: cash, negotiable instruments, gold, silver, jewelry, artwork, and other valuables the passenger carries in person.

## Default Rule: No Liability for Valuables

Under TTK Art. 1260(1), first sentence, the carrier is simply not liable for the loss of or damage to a passenger's valuables — unless the passenger specifically handed the items over to the carrier for safekeeping. The policy logic is straightforward: valuables carry a value-to-bulk ratio that ordinary security measures are not calibrated for, and a carrier typically has no practical way of knowing a bag contains cash or jewelry unless told. Without this carve-out, carriers would face an unmeasurable, effectively uninsurable exposure to hidden high-value items.

## The Escape Valve: Deposited for Safekeeping

The exception is deliberate: once a passenger affirmatively hands valuables to the carrier for safekeeping — the classic example being a purser's safe or a cruise ship's deposit box — the carrier is put on genuine notice and given a real opportunity to protect the item. In that scenario, liability revives, but only up to the limit set in Art. 1263(3) (currently 3,375 SDR per passenger per voyage), unless the parties agreed a higher limit under Art. 1264(1).

## Why This Matters for Carriers and Travelers

For carriers operating passenger vessels in Turkish waters, the practical takeaway is to maintain and document a genuine safekeeping facility — a purser's office or safe-deposit service — since only items formally entrusted this way trigger any liability at all. For passengers carrying high-value items, the message is equally direct: leaving valuables in a cabin or hand luggage provides no recourse if they are lost, while formally depositing them opens a (capped) claim, and separately purchased declared-value insurance can cover anything above the statutory cap.

The structure mirrors a familiar principle in Turkish law found in bailment (*vedia*) rules and hotelkeeper liability regimes: a service provider's responsibility for a guest's valuables generally depends on an explicit act of entrustment, not mere physical proximity.

No Turkish Court of Cassation decision squarely applying Art. 1260 to a maritime passenger case has yet been identified — this is a recent and narrow provision, and the case law has not developed accordingly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the carrier liable for money or jewellery lost on board?

As a rule, no. Under the first sentence of TTK Article 1260/1 <b>the carrier is not liable for the loss of or damage to a passenger's money, negotiable instruments, gold, silver, jewellery, works of art, ornaments and other valuables.</b> The sole exception is where such items have been <b>deposited with the carrier for safekeeping</b> — in practice, handed to the ship's safe or to the purser.

### What is the liability limit for valuables placed in the safe?

Under the second sentence of TTK Article 1260/1, where valuables have been deposited for safekeeping, the carrier is liable <b>within the limits laid down in the third paragraph of Article 1263, unless a higher limit has been agreed under Article 1264/1</b>. The limit in Article 1263/3 is <b>3,375 Special Drawing Rights per passenger per carriage</b> for luggage other than cabin luggage. The parties may agree a higher limit expressly and in writing under Article 1264/1.

### What is the rationale of this rule?

To place the risk on the party able to control it. The carrier cannot know what a passenger keeps in their cabin and cannot price that uncertain risk. Once valuables are <b>deposited</b>, however, the carrier both knows of their existence and can take appropriate security measures; hence liability arises. The same logic underlies the regime of the <b>hotel-keeper's liability</b> in Articles 576 et seq. of the Turkish Code of Obligations: unless valuables are deposited with the keeper, liability is limited.
