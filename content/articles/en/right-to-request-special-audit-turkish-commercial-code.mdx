When ordinary information rights are not enough to get to the bottom of something, TTK Art. 438-444 offer shareholders a stronger tool: a court-appointed independent expert who investigates specific events and reports back. It is a step up from the information and inspection right covered under TTK Art. 437, and it is deliberately structured as a secondary remedy — Art. 438 requires the shareholder to have already exercised the information or inspection right before requesting a special audit.

## Two Paths, Depending on the Assembly's Answer

Under Art. 438, any shareholder can ask the general assembly to approve an investigation into specified events, even if not on the agenda. If the assembly approves, either the company or any shareholder can apply to the commercial court within thirty days for appointment of a special auditor — no further threshold required. If the assembly rejects the request, Art. 439 raises the bar considerably: only shareholders holding at least 10% of capital (5% in listed companies, or a combined nominal value of at least TRY 1,000,000) may apply to the court within three months, and they must persuasively demonstrate that founders or corporate organs violated the law or the articles and caused loss. Turkish courts have applied this "persuasiveness" standard strictly, requiring concrete, documented factual allegations rather than general or abstract claims.

## What the Auditor Does, and What Happens to the Report

The court hears both sides before deciding (Art. 440), and its decision on whether to appoint an auditor is final. The appointed auditor investigates without unnecessarily disrupting company operations, and the board must grant access to books, correspondence, and assets; company insiders — founders, organs, agents, employees — must provide relevant information (Art. 441). The auditor reports to the court while protecting company secrets, and the court decides whether disclosure would harm the company before releasing it to the requesting shareholders (Art. 442). The board must then present the report to the next general assembly, and any shareholder may request a copy within a year of that meeting (Art. 443).

## Costs

If the court approves an application made after an assembly rejection, costs generally fall on the company, though the court may shift some or all costs to the requesting shareholders in special circumstances — for instance, if the claim proves unfounded. Where the assembly itself approved the audit, costs are always borne by the company (Art. 444).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What conditions must be met to request a special audit?

Under TTK Article 438/1 every shareholder may ask the general assembly, <b>even if it is not on the agenda</b>, to have specific matters clarified by a special audit, <b>provided this is necessary for the exercise of shareholder rights</b> and <b>provided the right to information or examination has already been exercised</b>. The two preconditions apply together: necessity, and prior exercise of the right to information under Article 437.

### What can be done if the general assembly refuses?

An application may be made to the court. Under TTK Article 439/1, where the general assembly rejects a request for a special audit, <b>shareholders representing at least one tenth of the capital, or one twentieth in publicly held companies</b>, or <b>shareholders whose shares have a total nominal value of at least one million Turkish liras</b>, may, <b>within three months</b>, ask the commercial court of first instance at the place of the company's seat to appoint a special auditor.

### Who bears the costs of a special audit?

As a rule, the company. Under TTK Article 444/1 the costs and fee of the special auditor appointed by the court are borne by the company. The court may, however, having regard to the particular circumstances of the case, decide that the costs be borne in whole or in part by the applicants. This arrangement ensures both that the special audit is an effective minority right and that it deters unfounded requests.
