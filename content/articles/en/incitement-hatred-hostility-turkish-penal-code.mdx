Turkish criminal law addresses public speech that targets identifiable segments of society through Article 216 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), a provision that sits at the intersection of public-order protection and freedom of expression. Foreign journalists, commentators, and businesses operating in Turkey increasingly encounter this article in connection with social-media posts, press statements, or public remarks that touch on ethnic, religious, or social divisions. Because the offense is built around a graduated system of danger thresholds rather than a single test, understanding how Article 216 actually works — and how far it can reach into ordinary public debate — requires unpacking three distinct forms of conduct the article separately punishes.

## The Interest Article 216 Protects

Article 216 is designed to prevent different segments of the Turkish population from turning against one another to the point of open conflict. The value the provision protects is generally described as "public peace," but this is not a single, uniform interest: the article splits it into three separate paragraphs, each defining a distinct wrongful act and pairing it with its own danger threshold and its own penalty range. The first paragraph reaches speech that actively incites one part of the population against another and requires proof that the speech created a concrete risk to public safety. The second and third paragraphs are concerned less with an imminent breakdown of order than with protecting the dignity of groups defined by social, ethnic, religious, or other characteristics, and the dignity attached to religious values held by a segment of the public. This layered structure explains why Article 216 is one of the most frequently litigated provisions where prosecutors' theories collide with expression rights.

## Three Distinct Offenses, Three Different Thresholds

Article 216 does not describe one crime — it describes three, each requiring different proof.

The first paragraph criminalizes publicly inciting one segment of the population, distinguished by social class, ethnicity, religion, sect, or region, to hatred or hostility against another segment. Critically, this paragraph only applies where the incitement actually generates a clear and imminent danger to public safety — the mere existence of inflammatory rhetoric is not enough. Courts require the speech to create a real, tangible risk that conflict or a breakdown in public order will actually follow. Because of this requirement, the first paragraph is understood in Turkish legal doctrine as a "concrete danger" offense, as opposed to an offense that punishes speech based on its abstract content alone. Where the concrete-danger element is established, the penalty ranges from one to three years' imprisonment.

The second paragraph operates on a different model. It punishes publicly demeaning a segment of the population based on social class, ethnicity, religion, sect, gender, or region — but, unlike the first paragraph, it does not require any showing that a concrete danger actually materialized. The penalty here is lower, six months to one year of imprisonment, reflecting that the offense is defined by the humiliating content of the statement itself rather than by any resulting risk to public order.

The third paragraph addresses public denigration of religious values held by a segment of the population. Here the danger requirement is softer than under the first paragraph: rather than demanding proof of an actual clear and imminent danger, the law asks only whether the conduct was capable of disturbing public peace. The penalty mirrors the second paragraph — six months to one year. Taken together, the three paragraphs show that the legislature deliberately calibrated different evidentiary burdens and different sentencing ranges to different categories of harmful speech, rather than treating "hate speech" as a single undifferentiated wrong.

## Who Can Commit the Offense, and the Publicity Requirement

Any person can be held liable under Article 216 — it is not limited to a particular category of offender, such as public officials or media figures. On the victim side, there is no requirement that a specific, individually identifiable person be targeted; the law protects a distinguishable segment of society, defined by characteristics such as ethnicity, religion, or region, rather than a named individual. A further requirement running through all three paragraphs is publicity: the act must be committed openly. Remarks made in a closed setting, to a limited audience, with no realistic possibility of reaching the wider public, do not satisfy this element and fall outside the offense. Turkish courts have applied the publicity requirement to online conduct as well — content posted where an indeterminate number of people can access it, including social media, is treated as public for these purposes.

## Intent Requirements

Article 216 requires direct intent: the person must act consciously and deliberately, aiming to provoke hatred or hostility toward a group, or to demean it. Whether the lower threshold of possible (reckless) intent can suffice is debated in Turkish legal scholarship, but the prevailing view requires that the speaker have acted knowing that the statement was objectively capable of generating hostility, and either desired that outcome or accepted it as a known consequence of the statement. This intent requirement matters in practice: harsh criticism, offensive or provocative language, or commentary raising a genuine social issue does not, by itself, satisfy the mental element unless it is also driven by a discriminatory purpose directed at singling out one group against another.

## Where Free Expression Sets the Boundary

Turkish law addresses the relationship between Article 216 and freedom of expression directly. A related provision, Article 218 of the Penal Code, increases the penalty for offenses connected to Article 216 (and several neighboring provisions) by up to half when they are committed through the press or broadcast media, reflecting the greater reach of mass communication. The same article, however, also states that expressions of opinion made for the purpose of reporting news or criticism, and that stay within the recognized limits of legitimate reporting, do not constitute a criminal offense. This carve-out functions as the central interpretive tool for applying Article 216 in practice: whether a statement was made in the course of journalism, academic critique, or informing the public is directly relevant to whether the underlying conduct crosses into criminal territory at all.

## Related Provisions: Incitement to Defy the Law and Abuse of Religious Office

The legislature has placed related but distinct forms of dangerous public speech in neighboring articles rather than folding them into Article 216. Article 217 of the Penal Code punishes publicly inciting the population to disobey the law, provided the incitement is capable of disturbing public peace, with a penalty of six months to two years' imprisonment or a judicial fine. The interest protected here is different from Article 216 — it is obedience to the legal order itself, not the risk of conflict between social groups. Article 219 creates a separate, narrower offense that applies only to religious officiants — imams, preachers, priests, rabbis, and similar figures — who, while performing their duties, openly disparage the government's administration, state laws, or government actions; this carries a penalty of one month to one year of imprisonment together with a judicial fine, or one of the two. Where such an officiant uses their position to incite the public to disobey the law or government orders, the penalty rises to three months to two years of imprisonment plus a judicial fine, and also results in a temporary or permanent bar from carrying out that religious function — reflecting that the abuse of an official religious role is treated as an aggravating circumstance in its own right.

## Procedure, Attempt, and Overlap With Other Offenses

Article 216 contains no complaint requirement, meaning prosecutors can investigate and prosecute ex officio, without the need for a victim to file a formal complaint — a natural consequence of the fact that the interest protected is public rather than purely individual. Because the first paragraph is a concrete-danger offense, Turkish legal scholarship debates whether attempt is even conceptually possible, since it depends on whether the act of incitement can be separated from the point at which the danger actually materializes. General rules on complicity apply where more than one person participates in the same act of incitement or humiliation. Where the same conduct also satisfies the elements of another offense — such as insult or threat — the rules on concurrence of offenses determine which provision, typically the one carrying the heavier penalty, actually applies. In cases involving the press or broadcast media, courts must carefully assess, on the specific facts, whether the statement exceeded the limits of legitimate criticism and news reporting before applying the increased penalty under Article 218.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does every offensive or provocative public statement about a group fall under Article 216?**
No. The offense requires either a concrete danger to public safety (first paragraph) or content that genuinely demeans a protected group (second and third paragraphs), combined with intent tied to a discriminatory purpose. Harsh criticism or commentary on a social issue, without more, does not satisfy these elements.

**What is the difference between the first and second paragraphs of Article 216?**
The first paragraph punishes inciting one group to hatred or hostility against another and requires proof that a clear and imminent danger to public safety actually resulted. The second paragraph punishes publicly demeaning a group and does not require any such danger to be shown — the offensive content itself is sufficient.

**Can journalists or academics be prosecuted for reporting on or criticizing social tensions?**
Article 218 of the Penal Code exempts opinions expressed for the purpose of news reporting or criticism that remain within the accepted limits of those activities. Whether a statement qualifies depends on the specific facts, including whether it was made in a genuine journalistic or academic context rather than to incite hostility.

**Is a complaint from a victim required to start a prosecution under Article 216?**
No. Because the article protects public peace rather than an individual's personal interest, prosecutors can investigate and prosecute on their own initiative, without any complaint being filed.
