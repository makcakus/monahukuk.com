Turkish labour law gives employees strong protection against dismissal, but it also gives employers a narrow and clearly defined escape hatch: the right to end an employment contract immediately, without notice and without waiting for a fixed term to expire, when one of a specific list of serious circumstances arises. This right is set out in Article 25 of Labour Code No. 4857, and it mirrors — almost bent for bent — the employee's own right to terminate for just cause under Article 24. For foreign employers and HR teams operating in Turkey, understanding exactly which facts fall within Article 25, and which do not, is essential: get the classification wrong and a termination that felt obviously justified can turn into a costly reinstatement claim or an unexpected severance liability.

## Health-Related Grounds and Why the Two Sub-Clauses Are Not Interchangeable

Article 25/I addresses situations where the employee's own health becomes an obstacle to continuing the employment relationship, but it does so through two very different mechanisms. The first covers an illness or disability that the employee has effectively brought on themselves — through intentional conduct, a disorderly lifestyle or alcohol dependency — provided the resulting absence reaches three consecutive working days or exceeds five working days within a month. The defining feature here is that the health problem traces back to the employee's own fault.

The second mechanism is structurally different and considerably stricter: it applies only where a health board (Sağlık Kurulu) formally determines both that the employee's illness is incurable and that continuing to work is hazardous in light of it. Turkish courts have consistently held that these two findings must be established together; the mere existence of a difficult-to-treat illness, without a separate finding that working is actually dangerous, does not on its own support an immediate termination under this ground.

Outside these two specific cases — for ordinary illness, an accident, childbirth or pregnancy — the law does not allow the employer to terminate immediately. Instead, the employer's right to terminate without notice only arises once the employee's absence exceeds the applicable statutory notice period (set out in Article 17) by six weeks. Until that point is reached, the contract remains suspended rather than terminated: the employee is not paid, but the employment relationship continues.

## Breach of Morality and Good Faith: Unexcused Absenteeism

One of the most litigated grounds under Article 25/II is unexcused absenteeism. The law treats an employee's failure to attend work — without permission and without a valid excuse — as just cause for immediate termination only when the absence fits one of three specific patterns: two consecutive working days, twice within a month on the working day following a holiday, or three working days total within a single month. Turkish case law is strict about the shape of this rule: an employer cannot add up scattered partial absences (late arrivals, early departures) to reach the equivalent of a full day, and an employee who shows up but simply refuses to perform the assigned work is not "absent" in the sense this provision requires — that is a different kind of breach altogether. Where the absence is backed by a legitimate excuse, such as illness, the death of a relative, or a court summons, the right to terminate immediately does not arise, and a medical report issued by a private healthcare provider should ordinarily be accepted as proof unless the employer can show it is fraudulent.

## Breach of Morality and Good Faith: Violations of Honesty and Loyalty

The broadest and most frequently invoked ground in practice sits in Article 25/II(e), which allows immediate termination where the employee abuses the employer's trust, commits theft, discloses the employer's trade secrets, or otherwise acts in a manner inconsistent with honesty and loyalty. The listed examples are illustrative rather than exhaustive — any conduct that breaches the employee's duty of fidelity can, depending on how serious it is, fall within this ground. Turkish courts have, for example, treated a pharmaceutical sales representative's false entry in a company system — recording a client visit that never took place — as a breach of this duty, because it amounted to deliberately inaccurate reporting. By contrast, a genuine clerical slip, such as entering the wrong name by mistake, has not been treated the same way, because it lacks the element of deliberate misrepresentation. This ground is the direct mirror of the employee's own right under Article 24/II: both parties owe each other a duty of good faith, and a serious breach of that duty by either side opens the door to immediate termination by the other.

## Force Majeure and Detention or Arrest

Article 25/III addresses a different category entirely: a force majeure event that prevents the employee from working at the workplace for more than a week. This is the employer-side mirror of Article 24/III and covers an obstacle that arises independently of the employee's will or conduct. Article 25/IV then deals with a separate and specific scenario — where the employee is taken into custody or arrested and the resulting absence exceeds the statutory notice period referred to above. Turkish case law confirms that the employer's right to terminate only crystallises once that notice period is actually exceeded, and if the employment contract provides for extended notice periods, those extended periods apply for this purpose as well. An employee dismissed on this basis is not entitled to wages for the period they could not work, and the employer is not obliged to pay notice pay (ihbar tazminatı). However, this does not extend to severance: because the grounds in Article 25/I, III and IV all rest on an objective impossibility rather than employee fault, severance pay (kıdem tazminatı) remains due even where the termination itself is fully justified.

## The Severance Pay Divide Between Fault-Based and No-Fault Grounds

This last point deserves emphasis because it is the single most important practical consequence of choosing the right sub-clause. A termination based on the employee's breach of morality and good faith under Article 25/II — dishonesty, absenteeism, misconduct — relieves the employer of any obligation to pay severance, precisely because the law treats the employee as at fault. By contrast, terminations under Article 25/I (health), III (force majeure) and IV (detention or arrest) are, in the eyes of the law, not about employee wrongdoing but about an objective obstacle to continuing the relationship — and severance remains payable even though the employer was entirely within its rights to end the contract. Employers who terminate under the wrong heading, or who assume that "justified" automatically means "no severance owed," frequently discover this distinction only after a dispute has already been filed. In every case, regardless of the ground relied upon, the employee retains the right to challenge the termination in court under Articles 18, 20 and 21, arguing that the facts did not actually fit within Article 25.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does terminating for just cause under Article 25 always mean no severance pay is due?**
No. Only terminations based on the employee's breach of morality and good faith (Article 25/II) — such as dishonesty or unexcused absenteeism — relieve the employer of the severance obligation. Terminations for health reasons, force majeure, or detention/arrest (Article 25/I, III and IV) still require severance pay, because those grounds rest on an objective obstacle rather than employee fault.

**Can an employer terminate immediately the moment an employee falls ill?**
Only in the two specific situations covered by Article 25/I(a) and (b): a self-inflicted illness causing defined absence thresholds, or an incurable illness combined with a health board finding that continued work is hazardous. For other illnesses, accidents, pregnancy or childbirth, the employer must wait until the absence exceeds the statutory notice period by six weeks before terminating without notice.

**How many absent days does an employee need before an employer can terminate for absenteeism?**
The absence must fit one of three defined patterns — two consecutive working days, twice in a month on the day after a holiday, or three days total in a month — rather than simply reaching a cumulative total of hours or days. Absences backed by a valid excuse, such as illness or a court summons, do not count toward this ground.

**Is disclosing confidential information always grounds for immediate dismissal?**
It can be, since Article 25/II(e) covers conduct that breaches the duty of honesty and loyalty, including disclosure of trade secrets. Whether a specific act qualifies depends on the seriousness and deliberateness of the conduct — an honest mistake is treated differently from a knowing breach of trust.
