This article covers the mutual obligations of salvor and owner during a salvage operation, and the foundational principles governing the salvage award itself (TTK Art. 1303-1304).

## The Salvor's Duties to the Owner

Under Art. 1303(1), a salvor owes the owner of the property in danger four duties: to carry out the operation with due care; to exercise the care needed to prevent and limit environmental damage while doing so (a duty that, per Art. 1301-1302 discussed in the prior article, is mandatory and cannot be contracted away); to seek help from other salvors where reasonable given the circumstances; and, most notably, to accept the intervention of other salvors when reasonably requested by the owner or master — even if the original salvor does not think it necessary — *without this changing the amount of the salvage award it is owed*. That last safeguard is what makes the duty workable: a salvor asked to admit another rescuer loses nothing financially by doing so, which is precisely what makes the obligation enforceable rather than self-defeating.

## The Owner's Duties to the Salvor

Art. 1303(2) mirrors this with three duties running the other way: to cooperate fully with the salvor during the operation; to likewise exercise care to prevent and limit environmental damage; and to take delivery of the salved property once the salvor reasonably requests it, so the salvor is not left indefinitely responsible for safeguarding it.

## "Environmental Damage" Defined

Art. 1303(3) defines "environmental damage" broadly: substantial physical damage to human health or to marine life or resources in coastal waters and adjacent areas, caused by pollution, contamination, fire, explosion, or similar major incidents. The open-ended "or similar" wording and the coverage of both coastal waters and adjacent areas reflect how modern salvage law (echoing the 1989 International Convention on Salvage) has broadened beyond pure property recovery to account for environmental risk.

## The Salvage Award: "No Cure, No Pay"

Art. 1304 codifies salvage law's core principle. Under Art. 1304(1), any salvage operation that produces a useful result gives rise to a claim for an award. Conversely, Art. 1304(2) provides that, unless the chapter states otherwise, an operation producing no useful result generally earns no award at all — a sharp contrast with service relationships (like legal fees) typically compensated for effort regardless of outcome. Salvors effectively bear the risk of the operation's success. Art. 1304(3) then caps the award: it cannot exceed the value of the property after it was salved (disregarding interest and litigation costs), ensuring the owner always retains some net economic benefit from having been salved rather than losing everything to the reward itself.

## Practical Takeaway

For owners and salvors alike, this means fee and cooperation duties are not merely aspirational — a salvor who obstructs additional help risks its own claim, while an owner who refuses to cooperate risks liability, and a salvor's ultimate award turns entirely on producing a useful result, capped by the value actually saved.
