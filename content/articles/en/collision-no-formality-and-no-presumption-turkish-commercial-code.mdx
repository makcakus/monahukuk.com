Having covered pre-action evidence preservation, the series now turns to two short but complementary rules governing how collision lawsuits are filed and how fault must be proven (TTK Art. 1293-1294).

## No Formal Notice Required Before Suing

Art. 1293 provides that no prior formal notice, warning letter, or other formality is required before filing a lawsuit for collision damages. This stands out against the general civil-law practice of requiring a formal notice of default before certain claims can proceed. The reasoning traces back to the nature of collision itself: a collision is, by its nature, an immediately obvious event — the party who caused the damage already knows about it, since they were there. There is no need for a formal notification step whose only real function elsewhere is to alert an unaware party. This dovetails with the urgency built into Art. 1292's evidence-preservation rules: just as evidence can be preserved quickly through a tiered court hierarchy, a claim itself can be filed quickly, without any procedural prerequisite standing in the way.

## No Presumption of Fault

Art. 1294 states a short but consequential rule: no presumption may be relied on in establishing fault in a collision. This matters because presumptions of fault are common elsewhere in Turkish maritime carrier liability — where the burden can shift to the carrier to disprove negligence. Collision law deliberately departs from that model: there is no shortcut inference, such as "the overtaking vessel is presumed at fault" or "the maneuvering vessel is presumed at fault." Each party's fault must be established independently, based on concrete facts — typically a detailed, rule-by-rule review of navigation-rule (COLREG) compliance by expert evidence.

This rule also underpins the no-fault collision provision discussed elsewhere in the series: where the cause of a collision cannot be determined, each side bears its own loss precisely because no presumption steps in to assign blame in the absence of proof.

## Practical Takeaway

Together, these two provisions strike a deliberate balance: Art. 1293 makes it easy to get into court quickly, while Art. 1294 makes sure that once there, fault must be proven rigorously and case-by-case — there is no shortcut based on which vessel was in which position.
