Foreign companies and individuals who deal with Turkish public administration — whether as contractors, account holders at state-linked banks, or parties to a public tender — occasionally find themselves on either side of an embezzlement allegation against a public official. Turkish law addresses this through Article 247 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), a provision that punishes officials who convert assets placed in their custody by virtue of their office, and through a linked set of provisions on aggravating fraud, voluntary restitution, and a lighter regime for cases where the official always intended to return what was taken. Because the offense turns on technical distinctions — who counts as a public official for this purpose, how custody must have arisen, and whether an intent to return the asset existed from the outset — understanding its structure matters for anyone assessing exposure or defending against a complaint in Turkey.

## What the Offense Protects and Who Can Commit It

Article 247 sits in the part of the Penal Code dealing with offenses against the reliability and proper functioning of public administration. What the provision protects is not simply the state's financial interest in a particular sum of money, but the public's confidence that officials entrusted with assets or funds will not divert them for private gain. This is why Turkish doctrine treats embezzlement as more than a property crime: it is also viewed as a breach of the trust inherent in holding public office.

Embezzlement is what Turkish criminal law calls a special-capacity offense — it cannot be committed by just anyone. Under Article 247, the perpetrator must be a public official who has custody of the asset because of their duties, or who is responsible for guarding or supervising it. A private individual who has no public office cannot be the principal offender. That said, if a private individual participates in the scheme — for example by assisting or instigating the official — general rules on participation in special-capacity offenses mean that the outside participant can still be held liable under Article 247 alongside the official.

## The Physical Elements of the Offense

**The offender.** Simply being a public official is not enough on its own. The asset in question must have come into the official's hands specifically because of the position held — either through a formal transfer of custody tied to the role, or through a duty to guard and supervise the property. This link to the office is what separates embezzlement from ordinary theft, where the offender never lawfully held the property in the first place, and from breach of trust between private parties, where the custodial relationship arises from a private contract rather than a public function.

**The object.** Anything of economic value that belongs to, or is in the custody of, the public administration can be the object of the offense — cash, securities, receipts, or physical goods. The asset does not need to be owned by the state itself; it is enough that the administration holds it or is responsible for safeguarding it, even where a private third party is the true owner. What matters is that the official came to hold the asset through the public function, not who ultimately owns it.

**The victim.** The direct victim is the public administration to which the asset belonged or was entrusted, which is why the offense is classified as a crime against public assets.

**The conduct.** The statute describes the prohibited conduct as converting the asset to the offender's own use or that of a third party — treating it as though it were personal property, spending it, or otherwise disposing of it outside the purposes for which the administration held it. The critical turning point is that custody began lawfully but was later turned into an unlawful act of disposal against the interests of the rightful holder.

## The Mental Element

Embezzlement can only be committed intentionally; a negligent misappropriation does not qualify. The official must knowingly and willingly use an asset entrusted to them by virtue of their office for a purpose the administration did not authorize. The particular motive behind the act — paying off a personal debt, meeting a private need, or benefiting someone else — has no bearing on whether the offense is made out; direct intent to divert the asset is sufficient on its own. The one place where intent is examined more closely is the misappropriation-for-use variant discussed below, where what matters is whether the official intended, from the very start, to give the asset back.

## The Lighter Regime: Misappropriation Intended for Temporary Use

Article 247(3) carves out a distinctly milder version of the offense. Where the misappropriation was carried out with the intention of using the asset temporarily and then returning it, the sentence may be reduced by up to half. Turkish commentators refer to this as "misappropriation for use." The defining feature is that the official's intent to give the asset back must have existed from the outset — this is what separates it from ordinary embezzlement, where no such intent to restore the asset is present. Whether a claimed intent to return the asset is genuine, and whether this scenario is best understood as a separate category of wrongdoing or simply a mitigated form of the same offense, remains debated in legal scholarship; the legislature chose to treat it not as a distinct crime but as a discretionary sentence reduction left to the trial judge's assessment of the facts.

## The Aggravated Form: Fraudulent Concealment

Under Article 247(2), where the embezzlement is carried out through deceptive conduct aimed at keeping the misappropriation from coming to light, the sentence is increased by half. What this requires is something beyond the act of conversion itself — the official must engage in an additional deceptive maneuver, such as misleading auditors or inspectors, falsifying records, or preparing fraudulent documents. Because such conduct makes the embezzlement harder to detect, it deepens the wrongdoing and inflicts a second injury on public confidence in the administration's own oversight mechanisms, which is why the law responds with a materially harsher sentencing outcome than for the ordinary form of the offense.

## Voluntary Restitution and the Low-Value Reduction

Turkish law also builds in incentives for an official to make the administration whole after the fact, and it scales the benefit to how early that happens. If the misappropriated asset is returned in kind, or the loss is fully compensated, before any investigation begins, the sentence is reduced by two-thirds. If the same happens voluntarily after an investigation has opened but before formal prosecution begins, the reduction drops to one-half; if it occurs after prosecution has started but before judgment, the reduction falls further to one-third. This graduated structure rewards earlier restitution more generously than later restitution, and in every case the restitution or compensation must be voluntary and complete — a partial return of the asset does not qualify for this benefit. Separately, and regardless of any restitution, the law also reduces the sentence by between one-third and one-half where the value of the misappropriated asset was low, reflecting a general proportionality principle that ties the severity of punishment to the financial scale of the wrongdoing; this reduction can be applied independently of, and in addition to, the restitution-based reductions.

## Attempt, Participation, and How the Offense Relates to Other Crimes

An attempt to commit embezzlement is possible where the official has begun the conduct required to convert the asset but has not completed the diversion. On participation, private individuals who are not public officials can still be held liable under Article 247 if they assist or instigate the official, applying the general rules that govern participation in special-capacity offenses; their individual sentence is then set according to the principles that apply to participants who lack the special capacity required of the principal offender. As for how the offense interacts with other crimes, fraudulent concealment is treated as an aggravating circumstance within Article 247 itself rather than as a separate offense charged in parallel, and because embezzlement and misconduct in public office (Article 257) stand in a specific-general relationship, conduct that satisfies the elements of embezzlement is prosecuted under Article 247 alone rather than under the more general misconduct provision. Embezzlement is prosecuted automatically by the public authorities; it is not a complaint-dependent offense, meaning investigation and prosecution proceed regardless of whether the victimized administration files a complaint. As for punishment, the base sentence is imprisonment of five to twelve years, against which the aggravating and mitigating provisions described above are then applied.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a private contractor or bank employee be prosecuted for embezzlement under Article 247?**
Only a public official can be the principal offender, since the custody of the asset must arise from a public office or duty. A private individual with no public role cannot be prosecuted as the principal, but can still be held liable under the same provision if they knowingly assist or instigate the official's conduct.

**Does returning the misappropriated funds make the case go away?**
No, but it substantially reduces the sentence depending on timing. Full restitution or compensation before an investigation opens yields the largest reduction, with progressively smaller reductions if restitution happens after an investigation opens but before prosecution, or after prosecution begins but before judgment. A partial return does not qualify for these reductions.

**What is the difference between ordinary embezzlement and "misappropriation for use"?**
Misappropriation for use applies where the official intended, from the beginning, to use the asset temporarily and then return it — allowing a sentence reduction of up to half. Ordinary embezzlement involves no such original intent to restore the asset. Whether a claimed intent to return is genuine is assessed on the specific facts.

**Is embezzlement only about state-owned money?**
No. The asset does not need to belong to the state itself — it is enough that the public administration held custody of it or was responsible for safeguarding it, even if a private third party is the true owner. What matters is that the official's custody arose from the public function.
