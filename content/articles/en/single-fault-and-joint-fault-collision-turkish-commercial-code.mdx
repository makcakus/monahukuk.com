Having covered the scope of collision rules and the no-fault scenario, the series now turns to the more common case: collisions where fault can actually be established (TTK Art. 1288-1289).

## Single-Fault Collision: Full Liability

Under Art. 1288, if the collision results from the fault of one ship's owner or crew alone, that shipowner must compensate the loss in full. The shipowner is liable not only for personal fault but for the fault of the crew — masters, officers, and ratings — acting in the course of their duties.

## Joint Fault: Liability by Degree, Not Joint and Several

Art. 1289(1) covers the more complex scenario: where both (or all) colliding ships' owners or crews are at fault, each shipowner is liable for cargo and ship damage only in proportion to the degree of their fault — not the full amount. If the proportion cannot be determined, or the parties turn out to be equally at fault, liability is split equally as a default rule.

The most striking feature of this provision is its third sentence: for these claims, the shipowners' liability toward third parties is **not joint and several**. This is a deliberate departure from the general tort-law principle that multiple parties jointly causing harm are each liable for the full loss. In practice, a third-party claimant — say, a cargo owner on one of the colliding ships — cannot recover the full amount from whichever shipowner has deeper pockets; each shipowner answers only for its own share of fault. This gives shipowners and insurers a predictable, capped exposure tied to their own fault, rather than exposure to another party's insolvency risk.

## Navigational Fault: A Special Carve-Out (Art. 1289/2)

A more technical rule addresses collisions caused by a "nautical fault" — an error in navigation or the technical management of the ship. In that scenario, the shipowner is not liable to its own cargo interests for that navigational error (mirroring the carrier's general exemption for nautical faults under other TTK provisions). If that cargo owner instead recovers from the other at-fault shipowner under a foreign law that does not recognize this exemption, a chain of recourse allows the paying shipowner to recover the excess from the exempted shipowner, who can then pass that cost back onto its own cargo owner — preserving the exemption's economic effect despite the international dimension of the dispute.

## Practical Takeaway

Turkish courts apply the fault-proportion rule mechanically: in one prominent case involving two colliding vessels, fault was split 60/40 based on a detailed COLREG rule-by-rule analysis, and each shipowner's recovery was capped at the other's fault percentage — not the full loss.
