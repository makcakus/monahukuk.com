Foreign nationals living, working, or banking in Turkey are sometimes surprised to learn that using a lost, found, or otherwise misappropriated bank or credit card is not prosecuted as theft or fraud, but as a distinct cybercrime offense under Article 245 of the Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237). This offense sits in the code's chapter on information-technology crimes rather than among the classic property crimes, reflecting the legislature's view that the real wrong lies in exploiting a payment instrument without authorization, not in how the card was obtained. Anyone facing an accusation under this article — whether as a suspect, a cardholder victim, or counsel advising a client abroad — needs to understand how the offense is structured, because the applicable subsection can change the sentencing range dramatically.

## What the Offense Protects and Why It Is Classified Separately

Article 245 protects two interests at once: the financial interest of the cardholder and the issuing bank, and the broader public confidence in the payment-card system as a reliable means of exchange. Because the offense safeguards this second, institutional interest and not merely private property, prosecutors pursue it on their own initiative — a victim's complaint is not a precondition for an investigation to proceed. This dual protection also explains why the legislature separated card misuse from ordinary theft and fraud: the focus of Article 245 is not the method by which a card ends up in the wrong hands, but the act of using it, or causing it to be used, as a payment or withdrawal tool without the rightful holder's permission.

## The Basic Offense: Possessing and Using Someone Else's Card

The core version of the offense, set out in the first paragraph of Article 245, criminalizes obtaining or holding another person's bank or credit card by any means whatsoever, and then using it — or having someone else use it — without the consent of the cardholder or of the person to whom the card was meant to be delivered (for example, the intended recipient of a card sent by mail), in order to obtain a benefit for oneself or another. The phrase "by any means whatsoever" is deliberately broad: it makes no difference whether the card was stolen, found after being lost, obtained through deception, or acquired in some other way. What matters is that the person did not lawfully hold the card and then used it, or enabled its use, to derive a financial advantage. Because the offense also requires that a benefit actually be obtained, merely holding someone else's card without using it does not complete this version of the crime — the unauthorized use, and the resulting gain, must actually occur. The basic offense carries a sentence of three to six years' imprisonment together with a judicial fine of up to five thousand day-units.

This structure is what separates Article 245 from theft or fraud. In those offenses, the focus falls on how the property or the card was taken. Here, the method of acquisition is treated as largely irrelevant, and the offense turns instead on the unauthorized use of the card as a payment instrument.

## Intent Requirement

Article 245 can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent version of the offense. The person must know that the card belongs to someone else and that neither the cardholder nor the person entitled to receive it has consented to its use, and must act with the purpose of securing a benefit for themselves or a third party. A person who genuinely and reasonably believes the card is their own, or that consent existed, may be able to raise a mistake-of-fact defense, which is assessed under the general rules on error rather than under Article 245 itself — but this is a fact-specific inquiry, and the burden of establishing a credible mistake typically falls on the defense.

## Aggravated Forms: Manufacturing Counterfeit Cards and Using Them

Beyond the basic offense, Article 245 contains two significantly harsher variants. The second paragraph punishes manufacturing, selling, transferring, purchasing, or accepting counterfeit bank or credit cards that are linked to accounts belonging to others, with three to seven years' imprisonment and a judicial fine of up to ten thousand day-units — the highest ceiling for a monetary fine anywhere in the article. Notably, this offense does not require that the counterfeit card ever actually be used; simply taking part in any link of the production or trafficking chain for a fake payment card is enough. The third paragraph addresses the flip side: a person who obtains a benefit by using a counterfeit card, or a genuine card that has been fraudulently altered, faces four to eight years' imprisonment and a judicial fine of up to five thousand day-units — the heaviest prison term in the entire article. This third-paragraph offense applies only where the conduct does not amount to a more seriously punished crime, such as forgery of an official document or aggravated fraud, so overlapping charges need to be resolved according to the specifics of the case rather than assumed.

## Family Exemption and Effective Repentance

For the basic offense in the first paragraph only, the law grants a personal exemption from punishment when the victim is a close relative of the offender — a spouse not judicially separated from the offender, an ascendant or descendant (or an in-law of the same degree), an adoptive parent or adopted child, or a sibling living in the same household. This exemption is narrow in two respects: it is a personal bar to punishment rather than a defense that erases the offense itself, and it does not extend to the aggravated counterfeit-card offenses in the second and third paragraphs — only to unauthorized use of a genuine card under the first paragraph. Separately, and again limited to first-paragraph conduct, the general effective-repentance provisions that apply to property crimes are also applicable here, meaning that compensating the victim during the investigation or prosecution can reduce the sentence.

## Attempt, Participation, and How Card Offenses Combine With Other Crimes

Because completion of the offense depends on both use of the card and an actual resulting benefit, a use attempt that fails for reasons outside the offender's control — for instance, a transaction declined by the bank — can bring the general rules on attempted offenses into play. The general rules on complicity also apply in full: where the person who obtained the card is not the same person who knowingly used it, questions of instigation or aiding may arise for whoever was involved short of direct use.

The most consequential question in practice is how card-use offenses combine with the offense committed to obtain the card in the first place. Turkish case law treats the acquisition of a card (for example, by theft) and the later unauthorized use of that same card as two separate acts violating two separate legal interests. As a result, courts apply the rule of genuine concurrence, sentencing the offender separately for both crimes rather than treating the card offense as absorbed into the underlying theft or fraud. In practice, this means a defense has to examine the acquisition stage and the use stage independently, since the evidence supporting each may differ, and a client might be exposed on one stage but not the other.

## Sentencing Ranges and Procedure

Prosecution under Article 245 does not depend on a victim's complaint; the public prosecutor's office investigates and pursues the case ex officio. Where a legal entity benefits unlawfully from the offense, separate security measures applicable to legal entities may also be imposed. The sentencing ranges differ meaningfully by subsection: three to six years' imprisonment with a fine of up to five thousand day-units for the basic unauthorized-use offense; three to seven years with a fine of up to ten thousand day-units for manufacturing or trafficking counterfeit cards; and four to eight years with a fine of up to five thousand day-units for using a counterfeit or altered card. Determining which paragraph actually applies — whether the card was counterfeit, whether the conduct occurred at the production/trafficking stage or the direct-use stage — is therefore central to both the potential sentence and the defense strategy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does it matter how the defendant obtained the card in the first place?**
Not for establishing the basic offense itself — Article 245/1 applies regardless of whether the card was stolen, found, or acquired some other way. However, the way the card was obtained may constitute a separate offense (such as theft), which Turkish courts sentence independently alongside the card-use offense rather than merging into it.

**Can a case proceed under Article 245 without the cardholder filing a complaint?**
Yes. This is not a complaint-dependent offense; prosecutors can and do open investigations on their own initiative because the offense is understood to protect the reliability of the payment-card system as a whole, not just the individual cardholder's property.

**Is there any situation where a family member cannot be punished for this offense?**
Yes, but it is narrow. If the victim of the basic first-paragraph offense is the offender's non-separated spouse, an ascendant or descendant (or equivalent in-law), an adoptive parent or child, or a cohabiting sibling, that relative benefits from a personal exemption from punishment. This exemption does not apply to the counterfeit-card manufacturing or use offenses under the second and third paragraphs.

**What is the difference between using someone else's real card and using a counterfeit one?**
Using a genuine card without the holder's consent falls under the basic offense (three to six years). Manufacturing or trafficking a counterfeit card is punished separately and more severely (three to seven years, with the highest fine ceiling), and actually using a counterfeit or altered card to gain a benefit carries the harshest imprisonment range in the article (four to eight years). Which category applies significantly changes the exposure a defendant faces.
