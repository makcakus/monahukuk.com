Foreign businesses and counsel dealing with Turkish public administration — whether in a licensing dispute, a public tender, or a criminal investigation involving a state agency — will eventually run into the confidentiality obligations that bind Turkish public officials. Article 258 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) makes it a criminal offense for a public official to disclose, publish, or otherwise make accessible a document, decision, order or other official communication that reached them through their duties and that was required to remain confidential. The provision sits alongside two related offenses in the same part of the Code — trading on official influence under Article 259 and collective abandonment of public duty under Article 260 — all three aimed at preserving public trust in the integrity, impartiality and continuity of the civil service. This article focuses on Article 258 and explains how the offense is structured, who can commit it, and what the practical consequences are.

## The Interest Article 258 Protects

Article 258 protects the confidentiality expectations built into how Turkish public administration functions, and the trust relationship that depends on those expectations being honored. Indirectly, it also protects the interests of third parties who could be harmed if confidential information reaches the wrong hands — for example, a party to an administrative file whose personal or commercial information is meant to stay within the file. The offense does not require that any such harm actually materialize; the law is concerned with keeping official communications and decisions within the boundaries the administration itself has set for them, regardless of whether a concrete victim can be identified afterward.

## What Counts as Prohibited Disclosure

The statute describes three distinct ways the offense can be committed, and any one of them is enough to trigger liability: disclosing the confidential material, publishing it, or facilitating — in whatever manner — its coming to the knowledge of others. The material in question must be a document, decision, order or other official communication that the official either received in the course of their duties or otherwise learned of because of those duties, and it must genuinely carry a confidentiality requirement. This last point matters in practice: releasing a document that is routine, already public, or simply not marked or treated as confidential does not satisfy the offense, however sensitive it might look to an outsider. Because the law lists disclosure, publication and facilitation as separate acts with the same legal consequence, prosecutors do not need to show that the official personally handed the material to a specific recipient — creating the practical conditions for someone else to obtain it can be enough.

Only a public official can be the principal offender, since the duty being breached flows directly from the official function itself. A person outside the civil service who receives or spreads the leaked information cannot be prosecuted as a principal under this article, though they may face liability on other grounds depending on what they did with it, or as an accessory to the official's offense.

## Intent and the Absence of a Harm Requirement

The offense requires intent (kast): the official must know the material is confidential and choose to disclose it, publish it, or ease its path to others regardless. Turkish doctrine treats Article 258 as an abstract danger offense — meaning the law does not require proof that the information was actually misused or that anyone suffered concrete harm as a result. The completed act of disclosure, publication or facilitation is sufficient in itself; the risk to public trust that the confidentiality rule was designed to prevent is treated as having occurred once the information leaves the boundaries the administration set for it.

In terms of how the offense unfolds over time, disclosure or publication is generally treated as complete at the moment the act occurs, while the facilitation variant — because it can involve a more extended course of conduct — leaves more room for an attempt to be identified where preparatory steps were taken but the information did not, in the end, reach anyone. Because this is an offense that only a public official can commit as principal, a private individual who assists or induces the official can be held liable only as an inciter or accomplice, never as a co-principal.

## Liability Continues After Leaving Office

One of the more consequential features of Article 258 is temporal: the duty of confidentiality does not end when the public official leaves their post. The same conduct — disclosing, publishing or facilitating access to material that was confidential because of the official's former duties — remains punishable even after the person's official capacity has ended, and the law applies the identical penalty range to a former official as to a serving one. In practical terms, this means a retired civil servant, a former licensing officer, or someone who has since moved to the private sector can still face prosecution years later if they release information they were only ever entitled to know because of a position they no longer hold. For businesses relying on former officials as consultants or advisors, this is a point worth flagging: the confidentiality obligation travels with the person, not the post.

## Penalties and How This Fits with the Neighboring Offenses

A conviction under Article 258 carries a prison sentence of between one and four years, applying equally whether the offense was committed while the person held office or after they left it. This sits within a family of three offenses that the Turkish Penal Code groups together because they each punish a public official for instrumentalizing their position for a purpose other than the honest performance of duty: Article 258 addresses breach of confidentiality, Article 259 addresses using the influence of one's office to attempt to sell goods or services to others, and Article 260 addresses the collective, unauthorized abandonment of public duties by more than three officials acting together. All three are offenses that only a public official can commit as principal, all three are prosecuted ex officio (they do not depend on a private complaint), and all three tend to be tried before the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi) given their sentencing ranges. A criminal case under any of these articles proceeds independently of any parallel disciplinary investigation the same conduct might trigger within the civil service — one process does not substitute for or bar the other.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the leaked information have to actually be misused for Article 258 to apply?**
No. The offense is treated as an abstract danger offense, meaning the act of disclosing, publishing or facilitating access to confidential official material is sufficient on its own. No proof of actual misuse or concrete harm to a third party is required.

**Can someone who is not a public official be prosecuted under Article 258?**
Not as a principal. Only a public official — or a former public official acting on information tied to their prior duties — can be the principal offender. A private individual who receives, encourages, or assists in the disclosure can potentially be liable as an accomplice or inciter, but not as a co-principal under this article.

**Does resigning or retiring end a public official's confidentiality obligations under this article?**
No. Article 258 expressly extends liability to a person who discloses, publishes or facilitates access to the same category of confidential material after their official capacity has ended, and the penalty range is identical to that applied to a serving official.

**Is disclosing a document that is technically confidential but not genuinely sensitive still an offense?**
The offense requires that the material actually carry a confidentiality requirement tied to its content or handling. Releasing material that is routine, publicly available, or not treated as confidential in substance does not meet the elements of the offense, even if it originated from an official file.
