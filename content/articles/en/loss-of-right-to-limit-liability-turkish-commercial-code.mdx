TTK Art. 1187 is the narrow exception that can blow through the SDR liability caps entirely — and Turkish courts have kept it genuinely narrow.

## The Standard: Conjunctive, and Deliberately High

A carrier loses the Art. 1186 liability caps only if it's proven that the loss, damage, or delay resulted from an act or omission committed intentionally *or* recklessly, *and* with knowledge that such loss or damage would probably result. This mirrors the "reckless and with knowledge" standard familiar from the Hague-Visby Rules and Hamburg Rules, and it sits well above ordinary negligence — even serious negligence isn't enough on its own. What's required is a knowingly disregarded, probable risk, not mere carelessness.

## Why the Bar Is Set So High

The entire commercial value of Art. 1186's cap system depends on it being reliably applied except in truly exceptional cases. If ordinary negligence claims routinely broke through the caps, carriers could never build an insurable, predictable risk model, and the whole SDR-based system — package limits, container rules, declared value — would lose its point.

## Applied Person-by-Person, Not Automatically Company-Wide

The same standard extends to the carrier's servants, but the loss of the cap is assessed individually: if a specific crew member or agent's conduct meets the reckless-with-knowledge threshold, only that person loses the cap in claims against them personally — it doesn't automatically strip the carrier itself of its own limitation right. Turkish Court of Cassation decisions have repeatedly confirmed that an actual carrier's or servant's recklessness does not, by itself, break the contracting carrier's own cap; the carrier's *own* intent or recklessness must be separately proven, and the burden sits with whoever alleges it.

## What Turkish Courts Have Actually Found

In practice, the Court of Cassation has consistently declined to treat operational mishaps — a forgotten container causing a 24-day delay, a mechanical breakdown causing repeated delays — as meeting this threshold, even when the underlying facts look careless. The recurring theme: Art. 1186's caps are the rule; Art. 1187's unlimited liability is the exception, and the burden of proving that exception rests firmly on whoever invokes it.
