Among the wage claims most frequently litigated before Turkish labour courts are those relating to unused or unpaid weekly rest days and national/public holidays. These two entitlements give financial substance to the constitutional right to rest, and while they are governed by two distinct provisions of the Labour Code — Article 46 for the weekly rest day and Article 47 for national and public holidays — they share a common feature that makes them attractive litigation targets: employers frequently fail to document them properly, and the resulting disputes turn heavily on which side carries the burden of proof. For any business operating in Turkey, and especially in a tourism-driven economy like Antalya's where round-the-clock and seasonal staffing is the norm, understanding exactly when these payments are owed — and how to prove they were made — is essential.

## Who Qualifies for a Weekly Rest Day, and What "Rest" Actually Means

Article 46 sets a single eligibility condition: the employee must have actually worked on the working days determined under Article 63 during the seven-day period preceding the rest day. No minimum seniority or length of service is required — the entitlement arises purely from having worked the scheduled days that week. The rest itself must be at least twenty-four consecutive hours; anything less does not qualify as a lawful weekly rest, and Turkish Court of Cassation case law treats a rest period given for less than twenty-four hours as if no rest had been given at all, meaning the pay obligation still applies in full.

Although Sunday is the conventional weekly rest day, Article 3 of Law No. 2429 on National Holidays and General Holidays does not make this compulsory — depending on the nature of the business, another day of the week may be designated instead. A significant recent development affects the tourism sector directly: Law No. 7553, enacted in 2025, added provisions to Article 46 allowing hotels and other accommodation facilities holding a tourism operation certificate to defer an employee's weekly rest, with the employee's written request or consent, to any of the four days following the day the entitlement accrued. In that case, the portion of the work performed on the original rest day that corresponds to the employee's normal daily working hours is not counted as overtime. This flexibility, clearly designed with high-occupancy periods at coastal resorts in mind, is balanced by the employee's right to withdraw consent at any time by giving thirty days' written notice to the employer.

## Days Treated as "Worked" for Rest-Day Purposes, and Force Majeure Situations

The law also credits certain periods during which the employee was not physically present at work as if they had been worked, for the specific purpose of preserving the weekly rest entitlement. Under Article 46, these include: periods that are counted as working time by law even though no work was performed; paid or unpaid holidays arising from law or contract; the leave periods listed in Additional Article 2 (such as marriage, bereavement, or leave related to a child with a disability); other leave granted by the employer within the same week; and sick or rest leave supported by a doctor's report. None of these reduce the employee's right to weekly rest pay.

A related protection applies where the employer — without any compelling or economic reason — simply closes the workplace for one or more days of the week. The days on which the employee could not work in that scenario are still treated as worked for the purpose of qualifying for paid weekly rest, preventing an employer from defeating the entitlement by arbitrarily halting operations. Where a genuine compelling reason under Articles 24 and 25(III) forces a shutdown lasting more than a week, the half-wage that employees receive for the days not worked during that period is extended to the weekly rest day as well — in that specific scenario, the employee receives half, not full, weekly rest pay.

## National Holiday and Public Holiday Pay Under Article 47

Article 47 works differently and, in practice, more generously than the weekly rest rule. It does not itself list which days count as national or public holidays — that determination is left to Law No. 2429, which designates dates such as 29 October (Republic Day), 23 April, 19 May and 30 August, together with 1 January, 1 May and the religious holidays. If the employee does not work on one of these days, the day's wage is paid in full without any work being required in exchange. If the employee does work on such a day, the employer must pay, in addition to that same full day's wage, a further full day's wage for the actual work performed — meaning an employee who works a national or public holiday effectively earns double pay for that day. In workplaces operating under the percentage (tip-pooling) system common in hospitality, this payment obligation rests squarely on the employer and cannot be offset against percentage collections received from customers.

## Calculating the Pay and Who Must Prove What

The Court of Cassation's 9th Civil Chamber has settled the calculation method for weekly rest days that are actually worked: on top of the daily wage the employee is owed regardless of work, the work actually performed is paid at a fifty percent premium — one and a half days' wages — bringing the total value of a worked weekly rest day to two and a half days' wages. For employees paid on a piece-rate or percentage basis, the daily wage rate is derived by dividing that week's total earnings by six, and the weekly rest payment is calculated on that figure. The calculation must be based on the employee's actual wage during the period in dispute, not projected backward from their final salary.

On the burden of proof, the general rule applies: the party asserting a fact must prove it. An employee claiming to have worked on a weekly rest day or public holiday bears the burden of proving that claim, while the employer bears the burden of proving that the corresponding wage was in fact paid. A signed payroll record showing the relevant accrual, if signed without objection, is treated as conclusive evidence unless its authenticity is successfully challenged. Where the payroll is silent on the point or lacks a signature, the employee may prove the underlying work by any means, particularly workplace records and witness testimony — although testimony from a witness who never worked at the same or a neighbouring workplace, and could not realistically know its working arrangements, will not be given weight. Because these are wage claims under Article 32 of Law No. 4857, they are subject to the general five-year statute of limitations for wage claims, and in practice they are routinely litigated alongside overtime pay and rest-break disputes, since the same expert calculations often cover all of these entitlements together.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can an employer simply schedule the weekly rest day on a weekday instead of Sunday?**
Yes. Law No. 2429 does not require the weekly rest day to fall on Sunday; depending on the nature of the business, any other day of the week may be designated as the rest day.

**Does the 2025 tourism-sector flexibility apply to all employers?**
No. The deferral mechanism added to Article 46 by Law No. 7553 applies specifically to accommodation facilities holding a tourism operation certificate from the Ministry of Culture and Tourism, and only with the employee's written request or consent, which the employee may withdraw with thirty days' written notice.

**If I work on a public holiday, am I entitled to double pay?**
In effect, yes. Article 47 entitles the employee to the full day's wage even without working, plus an additional full day's wage for the work actually performed, so a public holiday worked is paid at twice the normal daily rate.

**Who has to prove that a weekly rest or holiday payment was made?**
The employee must prove that the disputed work took place; once that is established, the employer bears the burden of proving that the corresponding wage was actually paid, typically through signed payroll records.
