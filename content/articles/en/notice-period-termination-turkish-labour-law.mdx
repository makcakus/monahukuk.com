Ending an indefinite-term employment contract in Turkey is not, as a rule, something either side can do without warning. Article 17 of Labour Law No. 4857 builds a notice mechanism into ordinary terminations so that neither the employer nor the employee is caught off guard: unless there is just cause for immediate termination, the party ending the contract must give the other side advance notice, with the length of that notice tied to the employee's length of service. This regime — generally called "termination by notice" — is the default route for ending an employment relationship in Turkey, and it is distinct from termination for just cause, which requires no notice at all but does require a qualifying reason. For foreign employers used to at-will termination or fixed statutory minimums unrelated to tenure, Article 17's sliding scale, its cash alternative for employers, and the separate penalty for bad-faith dismissals are all points worth understanding before any termination decision is made.

## How the Statutory Notice Periods Scale With Seniority

Article 17 sets four notice bands, each keyed to the employee's length of service with the employer: two weeks for an employee with less than six months of service, four weeks for six months up to one and a half years, six weeks for one and a half years up to three years, and eight weeks for more than three years of service. These are minimums — an individual or collective employment contract may lengthen them in the employee's favour, but may not shorten them. The clock starts running once the notifying party's declaration reaches the other side, and the contract ends automatically when the period expires. Critically, the employment relationship continues in full during the notice period: the employee keeps working, the employer keeps paying wages, and the employee retains every right arising from the contract, including the statutory time off to look for a new job.

## Failing to Give Notice: Notice Pay (İhbar Tazminatı)

A party that terminates without observing the required notice period must pay the other side compensation equal to the wages that would have been earned during that period. In practice this is called notice pay (ihbar tazminatı), and it can be claimed by either an employer or an employee who was on the receiving end of a notice-less termination. Turkish courts have been clear that notice pay is owed by whichever party terminated the contract, which means a party who terminates — even for a valid or just reason — cannot itself claim notice pay from the other side; the obligation runs the other way. The Court of Cassation's 9th Civil Chamber (decision of 9 May 2024, no. 2024/5595-8046) reaffirmed this principle: because notice pay compensates the receiving party, the terminating party is never entitled to it, regardless of how justified its own termination was.

Notice pay can also surface in reinstatement disputes. Where a court orders reinstatement under Article 21/4 but the employer does not actually reinstate the employee, the 9th Civil Chamber (decision of 11 May 2015, no. 2015/12722-17351) held that any notice period the employee was denied — or notice pay that was never paid in advance — must still be settled separately at that stage. Conversely, if notice was properly given, or the corresponding pay was already advanced in full, no additional notice pay claim can be made later.

## The Employer's Option to Pay Notice Pay Upfront Instead of Waiting

Article 17 gives employers a further choice: rather than keeping the employee on payroll through the notice period, the employer may terminate immediately and pay the equivalent notice-period wages upfront. This option runs only in the employer's favour — employees have no equivalent right to buy their way out of the notice period with a lump payment. Importantly, using this option (or simply failing to give notice at all) does not exempt the termination from the job security rules in Articles 18 through 21: even where notice pay has been paid in full and upfront, a court can still be asked to review whether the underlying termination had valid grounds. When calculating either the advance notice-period pay or any compensation due under Article 17, the law looks beyond base salary as defined in Article 32/1 — bonuses, meal and transport allowances, and any other benefit with a determinable monetary value that the employee is contractually or statutorily entitled to must also be factored in.

## Abuse of the Right to Terminate: Bad-Faith Compensation (Kötüniyet Tazminatı)

Employees who fall outside the scope of job security protection — typically because they do not meet the statutory conditions for those protections — receive a separate safeguard under Article 17: where the right to terminate is abused, the employer must pay the employee compensation equal to three times the applicable notice period. This is known in practice as bad-faith compensation (kötüniyet tazminatı). The 9th Civil Chamber's decision of 9 September 2013 (no. 2011/28490-2013/21605) illustrates how this plays out: where an employee argued that the real motive behind the dismissal was to make room for another hire, and the stated reason for termination did not match what was argued at the hearing, with a replacement hired for the same role almost immediately afterward, the court found the termination abusive and awarded bad-faith compensation. A separate 9th Civil Chamber decision of 21 January 2010 (no. 2009/46224-2010/603) clarified that employees who do benefit from job security protection cannot claim bad-faith compensation at all, since Article 17's own sixth paragraph reserves it for employees outside that protection — they already have access to the invalid-termination remedies instead. Where notice was also not given at all, notice pay is owed in addition to bad-faith compensation; the two are independent claims resting on different legal grounds, not alternatives to each other.

## How Article 17 Interacts With Job Security Protection

The relationship between the notice regime and Turkey's job security rules defines where Article 17 actually operates. For employees covered by job security protection, an employer's compliance with the notice period — or its choice to pay notice pay upfront — does not remove the requirement that the termination itself rest on valid grounds; the employee can still bring a reinstatement claim arguing the termination was invalid, even where notice was properly given. For employees outside that protection, by contrast, Article 17 is the only real constraint on the employer's freedom to terminate, and bad-faith compensation exists specifically to penalize an employer that exercises that freedom arbitrarily or in violation of good faith. It is also worth keeping termination by notice conceptually separate from termination for just cause, available to either employee or employer under other provisions of the Labour Law: the two categories require entirely different justifications, and confusing them — assuming notice is required where immediate termination is legally justified, or vice versa — is a frequent source of costly mistakes in practice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can an employer skip the notice period entirely?**
Yes, but only by paying the employee's wages for the full notice period upfront. This still does not shield the termination from a job-security review if the employee is covered by those protections.

**Who is entitled to notice pay when notice is not given?**
Whichever party receives a termination without the required notice — employee or employer — can claim compensation equal to the notice-period wages. The party that terminates the contract cannot claim it for itself, even if its own termination was justified.

**What is bad-faith compensation and who can claim it?**
It is compensation equal to three times the applicable notice period, available only to employees who fall outside Turkey's job security protection, where the employer is found to have abused its right to terminate. Employees covered by job security protection cannot claim it, since they already have the invalid-termination remedies available to them.

**Does notice pay only cover base salary?**
No. The calculation includes other monetary benefits the employee is entitled to under the contract or the law — such as bonuses, meal allowances, and transport allowances — in addition to base wages.
