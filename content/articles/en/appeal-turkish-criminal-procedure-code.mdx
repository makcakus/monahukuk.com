A judgment issued by a first-instance criminal court in Turkey is rarely the end of the road. Turkish criminal procedure gives most defendants and prosecutors a route to a second, full review of the case before a regional court of appeal (bölge adliye mahkemesi) — a review that can re-examine both the facts and the law, not merely check for procedural errors. This route is called istinaf, and it is governed by Articles 272 through 283 of the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (Ceza Muhakemesi Kanunu, "CMK"). For a foreign national facing criminal charges in Turkey, or for family members and counsel trying to understand what happens after a first verdict, knowing what istinaf covers, what it excludes, and how strict its deadline is can be decisive.

## What Istinaf Is and How It Differs From Other Remedies

Istinaf is the ordinary appellate remedy against judgments (as opposed to procedural rulings) issued by first-instance criminal courts. It became operative in 2016 with the establishment of Turkey's regional courts of appeal, adding a genuine second layer of judicial review between the trial court and the Court of Cassation (Yargıtay). It is broader than the more limited remedy known as itiraz, which challenges specific judicial orders rather than final judgments, proceeds on the case file without a hearing, and moves more quickly. Istinaf, by contrast, can involve a full hearing, allow witnesses to be heard again, and permit expert examinations to be ordered — the regional court is not confined to checking legal reasoning, it can revisit what actually happened in the case.

## Which Judgments Can Be Appealed and Which Cannot

Under CMK Art. 272, judgments of first-instance courts are, as a rule, subject to istinaf. Judgments imposing fifteen years or more of imprisonment are automatically forwarded to the regional court of appeal for review even without a request from either side. Interlocutory rulings issued before the final judgment that formed the basis of that judgment — or for which no other remedy exists — can also be brought before the appellate court together with the judgment itself. However, the same article carves out three categories that cannot be appealed through istinaf: convictions resulting in a judicial fine of up to 15,000 Turkish lira (unless that fine was itself converted from a prison sentence), acquittals for offenses whose maximum judicial fine does not exceed 500 day-units, and any judgment a statute expressly declares final. Where istinaf is excluded, the judgment becomes final immediately — it is not left completely unreviewable, however: under CMK Art. 279/1(b), if one part of a combined judgment is excluded from istinaf because it involves such a fine while another part of the same case is genuinely appealable, the excluded portion is treated instead as subject to itiraz and routed to the itiraz-reviewing authority. The legislature's aim behind these carve-outs is to keep the appellate courts focused on cases with real consequences, while still ensuring nothing is left with zero review.

## Filing an Istinaf Appeal and the Two-Week Deadline

Under CMK Art. 273, an istinaf appeal must be filed within two weeks of the date the reasoned judgment (not just the verdict as announced in the courtroom) is formally served on the appealing party. The appeal is lodged either by written petition to the court that issued the judgment or by an oral declaration to the court clerk, which is then recorded and approved by the judge. For a defendant held in pretrial detention, a declaration made through the detention facility is also recognized as a timely filing, which matters given the practical difficulty detained defendants face in reaching a courthouse. Defendants and parties recognized as injured parties (katılan) are not required to state grounds in their petition for the appeal to be examined — a bare notice of appeal is enough to preserve the right. Public prosecutors are held to a stricter standard: when a prosecutor appeals, the reasons must be stated explicitly, and that submission is then served on the other party, who receives two weeks to respond in writing — a rule that mirrors the general principle of equality of arms in Turkish procedure. Missing the two-week deadline is not a minor lapse: an appeal filed late will be rejected on timeliness grounds by either the trial court or the regional court of appeal, and that rejection can itself become final, meaning a missed deadline can result in the underlying judgment becoming enforceable.

## How the Regional Court of Appeal Reviews the Case

Under CMK Art. 280, the regional court of appeal reviews the case file and any evidence submitted, then follows one of several paths. If it finds no legal defect in the trial court's judgment, it rejects the appeal on the merits and the judgment stands. If it identifies one of a limited set of legal defects listed in CMK Art. 289 that can be corrected without a fresh hearing — for example, a miscalculated sentencing enhancement — the appellate court may correct the error itself and reject the appeal on the merits without holding a hearing; this is often described as "correcting and confirming." Where a defect on that same list is instead the kind that undermines the fairness of the proceedings itself — such as an improperly constituted court or a denial of the right to a defense — the judgment is reversed and the file is sent back to the trial court (or another first-instance court within the same jurisdiction) to be reheard. In every other situation, the regional court holds its own hearing and reconsiders the case on the merits, ultimately either rejecting the appeal or setting aside the trial court's judgment and issuing a new one itself. This is the feature that most clearly distinguishes istinaf from cassation before the Yargıtay: the regional court of appeal is a full merits tribunal that can reassess the evidence and the underlying facts, not only the legal reasoning applied to them.

## The Ban on Making the Outcome Worse for a Defendant-Only Appeal

CMK Art. 283 provides that when istinaf is pursued solely on the defendant's behalf, the new judgment cannot impose a heavier penalty than the one already fixed by the original judgment. This is the appellate application of a foundational principle of Turkish criminal procedure known as the prohibition on reformatio in pejus — barring an outcome worse than what a defendant already received. Its purpose is to prevent defendants from being deterred from exercising their right to appeal out of fear that doing so could backfire and increase their sentence. Established case law confines this protection to the amount and type of the sentence: the regional court, reviewing an appeal filed only for the defendant's benefit, remains free to reclassify the legal characterization of the offense — for instance, finding that the conduct actually constitutes a more serious crime — but it cannot raise the final penalty above what the original judgment set. This draws a careful line between correctly identifying the nature of the offense and preserving the defendant's confidence in exercising the right to appeal.

For the protection to apply, the appeal must genuinely have been filed only for the defendant's benefit. If the public prosecutor also appeals against the defendant, the regional court is free to increase the sentence. In practice, this comes up most often when only defense counsel files an appeal and the prosecutor neither appeals nor challenges the judgment to the defendant's detriment — in that scenario, even if the regional court reconsiders the case and disagrees with the trial court's characterization of the offense, it cannot set a final penalty higher than what was already imposed. This guarantee lets a defendant pursue an appeal without the added risk of ending up worse off than before.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can every criminal judgment in Turkey be appealed through istinaf?**
No. Judgments imposing judicial fines up to a set threshold, acquittals for low-level fine offenses, and judgments a statute expressly designates as final are excluded from istinaf under CMK Art. 272/3. Where one part of a combined judgment is excluded on this basis while another part remains appealable, the excluded portion is instead treated as subject to itiraz rather than left without any review.

**What happens if the two-week deadline to file an istinaf appeal is missed?**
The appeal will be rejected as untimely by the trial court or the regional court of appeal, and that rejection can become final. In practical terms, missing the deadline can result in the original judgment becoming enforceable.

**If only the defendant appeals, can the regional court of appeal increase the sentence?**
No. Under CMK Art. 283, where istinaf is pursued solely for the defendant's benefit, the new judgment cannot exceed the penalty already fixed in the original judgment, even if the appellate court finds fault with how the offense was legally characterized.

**How is istinaf different from an appeal to the Court of Cassation (Yargıtay)?**
Istinaf, before the regional court of appeal, can involve a full hearing where evidence is reassessed and witnesses re-heard — it reviews both facts and law. Cassation before the Yargıtay is a narrower legal review that does not generally revisit the factual findings in the same way.
