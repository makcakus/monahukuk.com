Timing changes everything once more: TTK Art. 1216-1217 address cargo lost after — rather than before — a voyage begins, and the shift produces one of the most carrier-favorable rules in this part of the Code.

## Total Loss: Contract Lapses, No Damages, No Distance Freight

Under Art. 1216, if cargo is entirely lost to an unforeseen event after the voyage starts, the freight contract lapses without either side owing damages, and the carrier collects only claims already accrued — plus the narrow exceptions under Art. 1199(2)-(3) for cargo lost through its own nature or sacrificed in general average. Notably absent here is any "distance freight" concept: unlike a lost ship (where surviving cargo still has value to prorate against), a total cargo loss leaves nothing to prorate — the very thing that would generate freight is gone.

## Partial Loss: Full Freight Regardless

Art. 1217 is short but consequential: a partial cargo loss after the voyage starts does not end the contract — and the carrier is still owed full freight, even if the lost portion was never actually carried at all, or was jettisoned mid-voyage. The only carve-out remains Art. 1199(2)-(3). This is a deliberate departure from the pre-voyage partial-loss rules (half-freight-or-substitute), because once underway, the carrier has irreversibly committed the ship, crew, fuel, and time to the entire voyage — costs a partial cargo loss does nothing to reduce. Turkish law treats voyage commencement as a point of no return: before it, risk is shared through election rights; after it, the shipper who committed to the voyage stays committed to the full freight.

## Why This Matters for Cargo Interests

Exporters and their insurers should understand that once a ship sails, a partial cargo loss en route is not, by itself, grounds to reduce freight — that risk typically needs to be covered through cargo insurance rather than a freight offset. Reported case law addressing this precise post-voyage partial-loss scenario is limited, again suggesting these disputes are usually absorbed into insurance settlements rather than litigated on the merits.
