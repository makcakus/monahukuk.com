Passenger carriage is not one-sided: passengers owe the carrier a baseline of cooperation, just as shippers do in cargo carriage. TTK Art. 1251-1253 set out three such obligations.

## Obeying the Captain's Instructions

Art. 1251 requires passengers to comply with all of the captain's instructions aimed at maintaining order aboard. A ship is a closed, potentially dangerous environment — in a fire, storm, or emergency evacuation, safety can depend on immediate, uniform compliance. The rule deliberately gives the captain broad, undefined authority, since no statute could anticipate every shipboard emergency scenario in advance. Turkish courts have applied this in allocating fault after accidents: in one criminal case, passengers who ignored repeated announcements to sit down during rough docking bore the majority of fault (60%) for a resulting injury, with the captain found only secondarily at fault.

## Declaring Baggage Accurately

Art. 1252 mirrors the cargo-shipper disclosure duty: a passenger must accurately declare the type, nature, and hazards of baggage brought aboard. Liability for inaccurate declarations runs strictly against the carrier, but only for fault against other affected parties — except where the baggage was hazardous or brought aboard secretly, in which case that fault requirement drops away too. The captain may remove or, if necessary, jettison baggage brought aboard through incomplete or false information or concealment (Art. 1252(2)); if the captain instead chooses to retain concealed baggage rather than remove it, the passenger owes the *highest* fare charged for that kind of voyage and cargo at the port and time of departure — a deterrent penalty mirroring the equivalent rule for concealed cargo (Art. 1252(3)). Knowledge held by the carrier or an authorized representative counts as the captain's own knowledge (Art. 1252(4)).

## Boarding on Time

Art. 1253 requires passengers to be punctual at the departure port and at any intermediate ports. A late passenger still owes the full fare even if the captain sailed without waiting — the ship was ready to perform, and a single late passenger cannot hold up everyone else's voyage. The one softening rule: if the seat was resold to another passenger, that amount is deducted, reflecting the ordinary duty to mitigate loss.
