Turkish criminal law treats public indecency and obscenity as offenses against society's shared sense of decency rather than as crimes against a particular victim, and this distinction shapes almost every aspect of how the offenses are charged and defended. Article 225 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) punishes engaging in sexual intercourse in public or exhibitionism, while Article 226 sets out a much broader and more layered set of rules governing obscene material. For foreign nationals, expatriate residents and international counsel encountering a Turkish criminal file involving these charges, understanding what "public" actually means, how exhibitionism differs from sexual harassment, and where the boundary between protected artistic expression and criminal obscenity lies is essential to assessing exposure and building a defense.

## The Interest Protected: Public Morality, Not a Named Victim

Articles 225 and 226 sit within the part of the Turkish Penal Code dealing with offenses against public morals, not offenses against persons. This placement matters in practice: unlike assault or harassment offenses, these crimes do not require an identifiable individual who was targeted or harmed. Instead, the law is concerned with whether an act was carried out in a setting where an indeterminate number of people could perceive it, disturbing the collective standard of decency that the community is presumed to hold. Because terms like "public," "indecent" and "obscene" are inherently value-laden and shift with social attitudes over time, Turkish courts have had to develop this area through case-by-case interpretation rather than through a fixed checklist. Turkish doctrine classifies both offenses as abstract endangerment offenses, meaning prosecutors do not need to prove that any specific bystander actually saw or was offended by the conduct — it is enough that the act occurred in a setting where perception by others was objectively possible.

## Public Indecency Under Article 225: Conduct Without a Targeted Victim

Article 225 criminalizes two alternative forms of conduct: engaging in sexual intercourse in a public setting, and exhibitionism. Exhibitionism, in this context, means exposing one's genitals or otherwise displaying sexually charged conduct in a manner visible to others; no physical contact with, or verbal address to, any particular person is required — the display itself, carried out openly, is sufficient. The requirement of being carried out "publicly" means the act must take place somewhere an indeterminate number of people could realistically perceive it. Conduct that occurs in an enclosed space with no realistic access by third parties does not meet this threshold, even if it might otherwise be considered indecent.

The clearest way to understand Article 225 is by contrast with sexual harassment, which is a separate offense under the Turkish Penal Code. Sexual harassment requires conduct directed at a specific person, aimed at that person's bodily integrity or peace of mind. Public indecency under Article 225 has the opposite structure: there is no target at all. The offender's conduct is directed at no one in particular, and it is precisely the open display in a setting accessible to the public — rather than any interaction with an individual — that offends the collective sense of decency the statute protects. Because there is no individual victim in the traditional sense, the harmed party is best understood as the indeterminate group of people who shared that public setting, and ultimately society itself. Any person can commit this offense; the law does not restrict it to a particular category of offender. It requires intent — the person must knowingly act in a way that is both indecent and carried out in a public setting — but no further specific purpose needs to be shown.

## Obscenity Under Article 226: A Graduated, Multi-Tier Structure

Article 226 does not describe a single act. It is built as a graduated series of provisions, each addressing conduct involving obscene material with progressively greater severity depending on how the material is produced, distributed and who it reaches.

The base tier addresses conduct such as: giving obscene images, writing or audio content to a minor, or exposing a minor to such content; displaying such material somewhere minors could enter or see it, or displaying it in public generally; offering such material for sale or rental in a way that reveals its content to onlookers; selling or renting it outside authorized points of sale; distributing it free of charge alongside the sale of other goods or services; and advertising such material. The reference to sales taking place "outside authorized points of sale" reflects the underlying policy: obscene material is not banned outright for adults, but its commercial circulation is meant to be confined to specific, controlled channels, and stepping outside those channels is what triggers liability. What unites this tier is that either the material reaches minors, or it is presented to the public at large; the private, consensual exchange of obscene material between adults, kept away from public view, falls outside the scope of this offense.

A separate and more serious tier applies when obscene content is published or broadcast through the press or other media, reflecting the far greater reach of mass communication channels compared to an isolated display or transaction.

The most serious tier by far concerns material produced using real children, simulated child imagery, or persons made to appear as children. Producing such material carries a substantially heightened penalty, and separately criminalized (at a somewhat lower level) are importing, reproducing, offering for sale, selling, transporting, storing, exporting, possessing, or making such material available to others. A defining feature of this tier is that a child's apparent consent has no legal effect: consent to sexual exploitation of a minor never operates as a defense in Turkish criminal law, because what is protected here is the sexual inviolability and healthy development of the child, not merely an abstract sense of public morality. The inclusion of simulated or digitally generated child-like imagery, even without any real child involved, extends the scope of protection well beyond material depicting actual identifiable minors.

A further tier addresses material depicting sexual conduct involving violence, animals, a deceased human body, or acts the law treats as unnatural — production, importation, offering for sale, sale, transport, storage, making available, or possession of such material is treated as a separate, aggravated category.

Publishing or broadcasting the two most serious categories of material through the press or media, or allowing minors to view, hear or read such content, is subject to the harshest penalty the article provides — underscoring that the protection of children is the legislature's overriding priority throughout Article 226. Where a legal entity is implicated in these offenses, the law separately provides for security measures specific to legal persons.

## The Scientific and Artistic Work Exception

Article 226 excludes scientific works from its scope entirely, and — except for the child-exploitation tier — also excludes works of genuine artistic or literary value, provided that access by minors is prevented. This exception is the principal mechanism Turkish law uses to reconcile freedom of expression with the protection of public morals. Notably, the child-exploitation tier of the offense cannot benefit from this exception under any circumstances: the legislature has deliberately closed off the artistic or scientific defense where children are involved, treating their protection as an absolute value that cannot be balanced away by reference to artistic merit. Whether a given work genuinely possesses artistic or literary value is one of the most heavily litigated questions in practice, typically resolved through expert examination and ultimately left to the trial court's assessment of the specific work and context.

## Procedural Features and Related Doctrinal Issues

Both offenses require intent; attempt is possible to the extent the conduct is capable of being divided into discrete stages. General rules on complicity apply, and in the production-and-distribution chains typical of Article 226 cases, whether a given participant is a principal or merely an accessory is assessed according to the specific facts. Where several alternative forms of conduct under the same provision occur as part of a single act, only one offense is generally found to exist; but where the same conduct recurs at different times or against different victims — particularly against multiple minors — questions of continuing offense or cumulative charging are resolved according to the circumstances of the case. These offenses are prosecuted ex officio, meaning no complaint from a victim is required to open an investigation. Because the maximum penalty under each paragraph of Article 226 does not exceed ten years, jurisdiction as a rule lies with the courts of first instance for criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi) rather than the higher criminal courts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the key difference between public indecency (Article 225) and sexual harassment?**
Sexual harassment requires conduct directed at a specific, identifiable person and aimed at that person's bodily integrity or sense of security. Public indecency under Article 225 has no targeted victim at all — the offense consists of engaging in sexual intercourse or exhibitionist conduct in a setting where an indeterminate number of people could perceive it, without the act being directed at anyone in particular.

**Does obscene material shared privately between consenting adults fall under Article 226?**
As a general rule, no. The base tier of Article 226 is triggered when obscene material reaches minors or is presented publicly. Private, consensual circulation of such material strictly between adults, away from public view, falls outside the scope of this provision.

**Can artistic or scientific value be used as a defense to an obscenity charge?**
Yes, but with one critical limit. Article 226 does not apply to scientific works, and does not apply to works of genuine artistic or literary value as long as minors are prevented from accessing them. However, this defense is never available for the tier of the offense involving the sexual exploitation of children — that protection is treated as absolute regardless of any claimed artistic merit.

**Does the setting need to be a physically open, outdoor space to count as "public" for Article 225?**
Not necessarily. What matters is whether the conduct occurred somewhere an indeterminate number of people could realistically perceive it, which can include a vehicle or enclosed space if it is visible from outside — for example, due to lighting conditions or a lack of physical concealment. Conversely, conduct in a space genuinely closed off from third-party access does not satisfy this element, even if it might otherwise seem inappropriate.
