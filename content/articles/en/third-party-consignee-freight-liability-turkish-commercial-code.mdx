Cargo is usually delivered to a consignee who never signed the freight contract. TTK Art. 1203-1207 explain exactly when — and how far — that consignee becomes liable to pay, and what the carrier can still claim from the shipper.

## Liability Is Triggered by Requesting Delivery, Not by Being Named

Under Art. 1203, a consignee becomes liable for freight and related charges only at the moment it invokes the bill of lading (or transport document) to demand delivery, and only for the amounts that document actually assigns to it. Being named as consignee, without more, creates no obligation. Turkish courts have held a party liable for demurrage precisely because it endorsed and presented the bill of lading to claim the cargo — the act of claiming is what triggers liability, layered on top of, not replacing, the shipper's own default liability under Art. 1200.

## The Carrier's Lien Against a Consignee Is Narrower

Because a consignee's liability is document-triggered and limited, Art. 1204 confines the carrier's lien against that consignee to the same limited set of claims — the carrier cannot hold cargo hostage for unrelated debts owed by the shipper. Turkish courts have voided enforcement actions, and in one case awarded bad-faith damages, where a carrier pursued a consignee whose bill of lading contained no authorization to pay. Where a single freight contract results in multiple bills of lading to different consignees, the lien must be exercised separately against each share.

## Carrier's Recourse Against the Shipper

Three scenarios close this chapter. If the carrier already delivered to the consignee, it generally cannot then also pursue the shipper for the same claim (Art. 1205) — except to the extent the shipper was unjustly enriched. If the carrier sold cargo under its lien but the proceeds fell short, it may recover the shortfall from the shipper under the underlying freight contract (Art. 1206). And if the consignee never claims the cargo at all, the shipper's original liability under Art. 1200 simply continues undiminished — courts have confirmed this is unaffected by delivery terms (such as FOB) agreed in a separate sale between consignee and a third party (Art. 1207).

## Practical Takeaway

For carriers relying on cargo as security, and for consignees receiving goods under a bill of lading, the decisive question is always what the specific document authorizes — not titles or labels. Foreign counsel should check bill of lading wording carefully before assuming a consignee bears freight risk.
