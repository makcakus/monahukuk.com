TTK Art. 1117-1118 close out the Code's chapter on ship captains by answering a necessary follow-up question: when a captain lawfully disposes of cargo out of necessity, who pays for the resulting harm — and when does that disposal actually bind the owner as against outsiders?

## The Shipowner Pays, Not the Captain

Art. 1117 provides that when a captain disposes of cargo under the necessity conditions of Art. 1116, the shipowner — not the captain — must compensate cargo interests who suffer loss as a result. This follows the general principle that a captain acting properly within statutory authority does not incur personal liability for the consequences; that liability lands on the party the authority ultimately serves. The compensation amount is calculated under the Code's general cargo-loss valuation rule, except that if the cargo was actually sold and the net proceeds exceed that statutory value, the higher net proceeds figure controls — ensuring the cargo owner isn't shortchanged relative to what the sale actually generated.

## One Good-Faith Test for All Disposal Powers

Art. 1118 ties together every disposal power discussed across this cluster — cargo preservation (Art. 1112), voyage-continuation necessity (Art. 1114), general average (Art. 1115), and the resulting owner liability (Art. 1117) — under a single validity test: the same good-faith standard used for credit transactions under Art. 1105(2). A third party dealing with the captain is protected unless it knew, or was grossly negligent in not knowing, that the captain was acting outside proper authority.

## Practical Takeaway

This consolidation reflects a pattern running through the entire captain's-authority framework: the captain's objective limits matter, but only a counterparty who knew or should have known of an overreach loses the protection that authority normally confers — a rule that consistently favors commercial certainty for good-faith third parties dealing with a ship's captain.
