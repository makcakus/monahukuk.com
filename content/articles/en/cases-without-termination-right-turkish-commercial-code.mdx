Having established when government action or war justifies termination, TTK Art. 1219-1220 mark the boundary of that right: obstacles affecting only part of the cargo, and a captain's justified deviation from course, do not qualify.

## A Partial Legal Obstacle Never Triggers Termination

Under Art. 1219, if a legal restriction — war, an export or import ban, or similar — affects only part of the cargo, neither party gets a termination right. The shipper must simply remove the restricted portion from the ship. But if the voyage hasn't started yet, the shipper gets the same choice seen for partial physical loss elsewhere in the Code: load substitute cargo (without worsening the carrier's position), or cancel by paying half the agreed freight. Whichever happens, the carrier still collects full freight even if the restricted portion was never loaded, or was removed mid-voyage — echoing the "sunk cost" logic that once a voyage is committed to, partial shortfalls in cargo don't reduce what's owed. Partial voyage charter and general cargo (kırkambar) contracts get the opposite treatment here from what they receive for total obstacles: no termination right at all for a partial restriction, since their fungible, shareable cargo commitments can absorb a partial shortfall more naturally than a full cancellation would.

## A Captain's Justified Deviation Doesn't Touch the Contract

Art. 1220 addresses a captain deviating from the planned route to save life or property at sea, or for another justified reason: this has no effect whatsoever on the parties' rights and obligations, and the carrier bears no liability for resulting damage. This builds directly on the carrier's separate exemption from liability for salvage-related deviation found elsewhere in the Code, extending the same protection to the survival of the contract itself — delay or other consequences from a justified deviation give the shipper no basis to treat the contract as breached. That said, the protection is not unlimited: the general good-faith principle under the Turkish Civil Code remains available if a "justified reason" is really just a pretext for bad-faith conduct.

## Practical Takeaway

For shippers dealing with partial export restrictions, the key point is that only a total obstacle to the whole cargo opens a termination right — a partial one requires active removal of the affected goods, with full freight still due. For carriers, a genuinely justified route change carries no contract risk, but the justification must be real. Reported case law specific to these exact provisions remains limited.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### If part of the cargo cannot be carried, may the contract be terminated?

No. Under Article 1219/1 TTK <b>impediments relating only to part of the goods do not give the parties a right of termination</b>. The contract stands and is performed as regards the unaffected part. This is an application of the principle that contracts should be upheld; it prevents a partial impediment from bringing down the whole carriage arrangement.

### Is the carrier liable if the master deviates to save life?

No. Under Article 1220/1 TTK, <b>the master's deviation from the route in order to save life or property at sea, or for another just cause, does not affect the rights and obligations of the parties, and the carrier is not liable for loss arising from it</b>. The provision ensures that the duty to save life at sea (Article 1295 and international maritime law) is not penalised through commercial consequences.

### What follows from a deviation without just cause?

The protection of Article 1220 is granted only to a deviation made <b>for just cause</b>. An unjustifiable deviation is a breach of contract by the carrier: it is liable for loss arising during that deviation, and scholars debate whether, depending on the nature of the deviation, it may still rely on the limits of liability in Article 1186. The burden of proving just cause lies on the carrier.
