Foreign counsel handling a criminal complaint, an extradition matter, or an investigation involving Turkish state actors will sooner or later run into Article 94 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), which criminalizes torture. What sets this offense apart from ordinary violent crimes is not only its severity, but a set of structural features written directly into the statute: it can only be committed by a public official, it cannot be mitigated on the ground that it was committed through omission, and — since a 2013 amendment — it is never extinguished by the passage of time. This article explains how torture is defined and proven under Turkish law, how it is distinguished from related offenses such as ill-treatment (eziyet) and intentional injury, and why the statute-of-limitations rule makes these cases procedurally unusual.

## The Interest the Offense Protects

Article 94 sits within the part of the Penal Code dealing with offenses against persons, in a chapter titled "Torture and Ill-Treatment," with the aggravated consequences of torture addressed separately in Article 95. The value the offense protects is human dignity. Torture certainly involves an attack on the victim's bodily integrity or mental health, but the wrong the law targets goes further: it is the reduction of a person from a subject with will and agency to a mere object under another's control. Because the perpetrator is by definition someone exercising public authority, the offense also protects, secondarily, public confidence in the administration and the integrity of public office. Article 17 of the Turkish Constitution and Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights treat the prohibition of torture as absolute — no state of emergency, security threat, or investigative necessity can justify it — and Article 94 carries that absolute character into the criminal law itself, both by barring any sentence reduction for omission-based commission and by excluding the offense from the statute of limitations altogether.

## Who Can Commit the Offense and Who Can Be a Victim

Torture is a so-called "proper" offense, meaning the class of possible perpetrators is legally restricted: only a public official can commit it. If the same conduct is carried out by someone who does not hold that status, the applicable charge is ill-treatment or intentional injury, not torture. The statute does not expressly require that the official's conduct be connected to the exercise of official duty, but established practice reads that connection into the offense — the public office must have supplied the opportunity or the leverage that made the conduct possible. Where a public official acts entirely within a private, non-official context, the applicable offense (if any) is again ill-treatment or injury rather than torture.

Any person can be a victim. In practice, victims are most often people held in police custody, pre-trial detention, or serving a sentence, but the law imposes no such limitation. Certain victim characteristics raise the penalty: the offense is aggravated when committed against a child, a person physically or mentally unable to defend themselves, or a pregnant woman, and equally when committed against a lawyer or another public official by reason of that person's duties. Where the victim is a woman, the minimum sentence for the base offense itself is raised.

## The Conduct Element and Why Persistence Matters

The statute does not tie the offense to a single defined act; it uses a result-based description instead. Qualifying conduct is any act incompatible with human dignity that causes the victim physical or psychological suffering, impairs their capacity to perceive or exercise free will, or subjects them to degradation. This can cover overtly physical conduct — beatings, electric shock, deprivation of food or water — as much as psychological or degrading treatment such as sleep deprivation, sustained threats, suggesting that harm will come to the victim's family, or forced nudity.

The criterion that Turkish courts and legal commentary treat as decisive is whether the conduct is sustained and systematic. A single, sudden act of violence by a public official is ordinarily charged as intentional injury, not torture. Conduct that unfolds over a period, repeated in a way designed to break down the victim's resistance and induce helplessness, is what qualifies as torture. Persistence alone does not fully separate torture from ill-treatment, since ill-treatment also typically involves repeated conduct — the true dividing line is the identity of the perpetrator: ill-treatment can be committed by anyone, while torture requires a public official acting in connection with official duty.

## Fault Requirement and the Question of Purpose

Torture can only be committed intentionally; it cannot be committed through negligence. The perpetrator must act knowing that the conduct is incompatible with human dignity and will cause the victim suffering, impaired will, or degradation, and must want that outcome. Given the sustained and systematic nature typically required, the prevailing view is that the offense demands direct intent, although whether a lower standard of intent (dolus eventualis) can suffice remains debated in the literature. Importantly, the law does not require that the official act for any specific purpose — extracting a confession, punishing the victim, or intimidating them are all common motives, but proof of a particular purpose is not an element of the offense. Motive may still be relevant later, when the court sets the sentence within the applicable range.

## Aggravated Forms and Consequences Beyond What Was Intended

Beyond the base offense, the penalty rises significantly where the victim's status calls for it (child, defenseless victim, or pregnant woman; a lawyer or public official targeted by reason of duty), and rises further still where the conduct takes the form of sexual harassment. Article 95 then addresses situations where torture produces a graver outcome than the perpetrator necessarily intended: permanent weakening of a sense or organ function, a lasting speech impairment, a permanent mark on the face, a life-threatening condition, or premature birth caused to a pregnant victim each increase the sentence by half. An incurable illness or a vegetative state, loss of a sense or organ function, loss of speech or reproductive capacity, permanent disfigurement of the face, or miscarriage caused to a pregnant victim double the sentence. Where the conduct causes a bone fracture, the penalty is set according to how the fracture affects the victim's vital functions. Where death results, the sentence is aggravated life imprisonment.

## Attempt, Participation, and How Multiple Acts Are Charged

Because torture ordinarily requires conduct repeated over a period, an attempt charge is only conceivable where the perpetrator began the conduct but persistence and system never materialized for reasons outside their control — and in most such scenarios, the partial conduct that did occur will already amount to a completed offense of its own, such as injury or threat. On participation, the statute departs from the Code's general rule for proper offenses: ordinarily, someone who lacks the special status required to be a principal offender can only be liable as an instigator or accessory, but Article 94 expressly provides that any other person who takes part in committing torture is punished as if they were a public official themselves. This exception reinforces the absolute character of the prohibition even in the law of complicity. As for charging multiple acts, repeated conduct over the relevant period is treated as a single offense of torture rather than punished act by act; where several victims are involved, however, the offense is counted once per victim. Where one of the graver consequences listed in Article 95 results and the perpetrator was at least negligent as to that consequence, the aggravated-torture provisions apply instead of a separate charge for intentional injury.

## Sentencing and Procedural Features

The base penalty is imprisonment from three to twelve years; where the victim is a woman, the minimum cannot be less than five years. The offense is not subject to a citizen's complaint requirement — prosecution proceeds automatically — and it falls outside the scope of mediation. Because the statutory maximum exceeds ten years, jurisdiction lies with the heavy penal court. Where the offense is committed through omission — a public official who tolerates or fails to intervene in ongoing mistreatment despite a duty to act — the law expressly forecloses any sentence reduction on that basis, in contrast to certain other offenses (such as intentional homicide or intentional injury) where an omission-based mitigation is available. This closes off leniency for officials who stand by while abuse occurs. Finally, and most distinctively, a 2013 amendment added a rule that the statute of limitations does not run for this offense at all: perpetrators remain liable to prosecution regardless of how much time has passed, making this one of the strongest safeguards in Turkish law against torture going unpunished by the mere passage of years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a private individual be charged with torture under Article 94?**
No. Torture is a proper offense that can only be committed by a public official acting in connection with official duty. The same conduct by a private individual is charged as ill-treatment or intentional injury instead.

**What actually separates torture from ill-treatment or intentional injury?**
The identity of the perpetrator is the decisive factor: torture requires a public official, while ill-treatment can be committed by anyone. Separately, both torture and ill-treatment typically require sustained, repeated conduct, whereas a single sudden act of violence is ordinarily charged as intentional injury.

**Is there any time limit for prosecuting torture in Turkey?**
No. Since a 2013 legislative amendment, torture is expressly excluded from the statute of limitations, so prosecution can proceed no matter how much time has elapsed since the conduct occurred.

**Does a public official who merely fails to stop torture committed by others face a lighter sentence?**
No. The law specifically bars any sentence reduction where the offense is committed through omission, meaning an official who tolerates ongoing abuse despite a duty to intervene is treated without the leniency available for omission-based liability in other offenses.
