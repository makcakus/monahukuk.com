Claimants sometimes sue the individual crew member — a captain or officer — rather than (or alongside) the carrier itself. TTK Art. 1265 determines whether that individual can claim the same protections the carrier enjoys.

## The Rule: Defenses and Limits Extend, With One Condition

Under Art. 1265, if a servant of the carrier or actual carrier is sued individually for damages covered by this Chapter, that servant may invoke the same defenses and liability limits available to the carrier — but only by proving the damage occurred while performing their duties. This mirrors the "defense portability" mechanism the series encountered in the cargo context (Art. 1190(2)), applied here to both the carrier's and the actual carrier's servants in a single, unified provision.

## Why It Matters

Without this rule, claimants could sidestep the Chapter's carefully calibrated protections simply by naming the captain or a crew member personally, exposing that individual to potentially unlimited liability for conduct that would have been capped had the carrier been sued instead. Art. 1265 ties the protective architecture to the *conduct*, not the *identity* of the defendant — the same accident is subject to the same regime whether attributed to the carrier or to its servant acting in the course of duty.

## The Limiting Condition

The protection is not automatic: the servant must prove the damage occurred while performing their duties. Without this condition, an employee could claim the Chapter's protections even for conduct entirely unrelated to their role — undermining the purpose of protecting the carrier's authorized operational activity, whichever employee happens to carry it out.

## Case Law: A Captain Personally Held to the Same Cap

A 2026 Istanbul Regional Court of Appeal decision (12th Civil Chamber, 2026/420-2026/757, not yet final) applied Art. 1265 directly: after finding the carrier subject to the 400,000 SDR cap under Art. 1262, the court stated that "under TTK Art. 1265, the defendant captain's liability is also subject to this upper limit," since the captain — sued personally alongside the carrier — was found to have acted within the scope of his duties. The same decision then found that both the carrier and the captain had acted recklessly under Art. 1267, meaning both ultimately forfeited the cap and were held liable for the full real damages.

## Practical Takeaway

For individually named crew members, documenting that the conduct in question occurred in the course of assigned duties is the key to claiming the carrier's protections. The forfeiture standard under Art. 1267 is assessed person-by-person — a servant's own reckless conduct can independently strip them of the protection, even where the carrier's conduct might not.
