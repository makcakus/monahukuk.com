Turkish criminal law does not only punish people who actively take something that belongs to someone else — it also addresses situations where property or a service reaches a person through chance, error, or a technical loophole, and that person then chooses to keep it or use it rather than return it or report it. Articles 160 and 163 of the Turkish Penal Code cover two such situations: someone who comes into possession of lost or misdirected property without any wrongdoing on their part but then deals with it as if it were their own, and someone who obtains electricity, water, natural gas, a metered service, or a signal-based service without paying for it. Although grouped together in the same part of the code because both fall outside the classic property offenses of theft and fraud, the two provisions rest on different logic and carry different penalties. Foreign nationals and businesses operating in Turkey should understand both, since disputes over misdirected bank transfers, found packages, illegal electricity connections and unauthorized subscription bypasses are common sources of criminal complaints.

## Article 160: Failing to Return or Report Property That Reaches You by Chance

Article 160 targets a person who ends up in possession of an item that its owner lost, or that reached the person by mistake — for example, money sent to the wrong bank account, a package delivered to the wrong address, or a cash machine dispensing more money than requested — and who then deals with that item as though they were its owner, without returning it or notifying the authorities. Conviction requires a complaint from the victim, and the maximum penalty is imprisonment of up to one year or a judicial fine.

What separates this offense from theft is the moment the property changes hands. In theft, the offender actively breaks the owner's possession through a deliberate act taken without consent. Under Article 160, by contrast, the property has already left the owner's possession before the person ever gets involved — through loss or through someone else's mistake — and reaches the person passively. There is no deception involved either, which is what distinguishes it from fraud: the property simply arrives by chance. The law separately addresses two ways this can happen: property that is lost by the owner without any third party's intervention, and property that reaches the person because of someone else's error, such as a courier or bank employee misdirecting it. In both scenarios, nothing in how the person first obtained the item is unlawful.

The interest the law protects is not ownership itself, but a basic duty of good faith to return property or notify the proper authorities once someone realizes an item is not theirs. Legislators treat a failure to honor that duty as a lesser wrong than an outright taking. The offense is essentially one of omission: the wrongdoing is not in acquiring the item but in failing to return it or report it, while instead exercising an owner's rights over it — selling it, using it up, spending it, or otherwise treating it as one's own. Merely holding onto the item is not enough to trigger liability; the person must actually exercise ownership-like control over it. The law sets no fixed deadline for returning the item or notifying the authorities, but a person is expected to act within a reasonable time once they realize the property is not theirs.

The offense requires intent. The person must know that the item does not belong to them and that it reached them by chance, and must nonetheless choose to treat it as their own rather than return or report it. There is no obligation to investigate who the true owner is — what matters is only that the person is aware the item is not theirs. There is no negligent version of this offense. Because the law makes prosecution conditional on a victim's complaint, no investigation can begin without one, and withdrawing the complaint ends the case. This reflects the relatively low degree of social harm involved and the fact that these disputes are often capable of being resolved directly between the parties.

## Article 163: Unpaid Use of Machines, Signals and Metered Utilities

Article 163 addresses three distinct and unrelated forms of using a service without paying for it. The first paragraph punishes using a coin- or token-operated machine — vending machines, parking barriers, automated ticket dispensers and similar devices — without making the required payment, typically through a rigged device or counterfeit token or coin. Unlike fraud under Article 157, the "victim" being deceived here is a machine rather than a person, so the element of deception does not meet the persuasion threshold required for fraud, and the offense is treated as a lesser one. The second paragraph covers using telephone lines, frequencies, or encrypted or unencrypted broadcasts transmitted by electromagnetic waves without the consent of the rightful holder — for example, unauthorized satellite decoders or unlawful use of a phone line. The third paragraph, added by Law No. 6352 in 2012, deals specifically with electricity, water or natural gas supplied on a subscription basis, and applies when the person consumes it without the provider's consent and in a way that prevents the actual amount consumed from being measured — such as tampering with a meter or tapping directly into a line. That last requirement is essential to the third paragraph: if the meter functions correctly but the bill simply goes unpaid, this is a billing or contractual matter rather than a crime under this provision. The paragraph only applies where consumption has been concealed or rendered impossible to measure.

## Why the Penalties Differ Across the Three Paragraphs

The maximum penalties rise according to how significant the interest being protected is. Unpaid use of a machine carries imprisonment of two to six months; unauthorized use of lines or broadcasts carries six months to two years; and interference with metered electricity, water or natural gas carries one to three years. This scale reflects the view that tampering with a subscription-based public utility service causes more social harm than an isolated misuse of a machine. Unlike Article 160, Article 163 does not require a victim's complaint — these offenses are investigated and prosecuted by the public prosecutor on its own initiative.

## Distinguishing These Offenses From Theft and From Each Other

Because attempt requires an unfinished act and both offenses tend to be completed in a single, brief moment or through an omission, the scope for an attempt charge is narrow — under Article 160, the offense is generally considered complete the moment the person begins exercising ownership-like control, leaving little room for an intermediate "attempt" stage. General rules on participation (instigation, aiding) apply as usual; where meter tampering under Article 163(3) is carried out with technical help from a third party, that person may face liability as an instigator or accessory. Whether repeated consumption from the same subscription amounts to a single continuing offense or separate offenses depends on whether the person acted under one overall decision or renewed their intent on different occasions.

Article 160 cannot be applied together with theft or fraud arising from the same set of facts, because the way possession was originally obtained determines which provision governs — the character of that initial moment fixes the applicable offense. Likewise, the three paragraphs of Article 163 exclude one another: it would be unusual for elements of the machine, line/broadcast, and subscription paragraphs to overlap in a single act, but where they do, the paragraph that reflects the true center of gravity of the violation should apply rather than applying every paragraph mechanically.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If money is mistakenly transferred into my bank account, am I committing a crime by not returning it?**
If you realize the money is not yours and use it, spend it, or otherwise treat it as your own without returning it or notifying the relevant authority, this can constitute the offense under Article 160. Prosecution requires a complaint from the account holder or bank, and the maximum penalty is imprisonment of up to one year or a judicial fine.

**Is unpaid electricity use always a criminal matter in Turkey?**
No. Article 163(3) only applies where the consumption has been concealed or made impossible to measure, for example through meter tampering or an illegal line connection. If the meter is working correctly and a bill simply remains unpaid, that is a billing dispute to be pursued through ordinary debt-collection channels, not a criminal offense under this provision.

**Does a victim need to file a complaint for a prosecution under Article 163?**
No. Unlike Article 160, none of the three paragraphs of Article 163 require a complaint. These offenses are investigated and prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative.

**Can someone be charged with both theft and Article 160 for the same item?**
No. The two offenses are mutually exclusive because they turn on how the property was first obtained. If the person actively took the item without consent, theft applies; if the item reached them through loss or another party's mistake and they only later decided to keep it, Article 160 applies instead.
