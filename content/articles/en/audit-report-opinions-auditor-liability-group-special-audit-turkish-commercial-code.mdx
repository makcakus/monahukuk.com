The Turkish Commercial Code closes its statutory audit regime with a cluster of provisions covering what the auditor's report must contain, what happens after it is delivered, and who answers for a defective audit. TTK Art. 402-406 turn the technical work of auditing into concrete legal consequences for the board, the auditor, and minority shareholders.

## Four Opinion Types, One Serious Trigger

Under Art. 402-403, the auditor issues a report on the financial statements and separately assesses whether the board's annual activity report matches them. The report's core is the opinion itself: unqualified, qualified, adverse, or a disclaimer where uncertainty or company-imposed restrictions prevent a sound conclusion — a disclaimer carries the same legal weight as an adverse opinion. The practical trigger is Art. 403(5): an adverse opinion obliges the board to call a general assembly within four business days to elect a new board, which then must produce compliant financial statements within six months. A qualified opinion instead requires the assembly to resolve on corrective measures.

## Auditor Liability and Confidentiality

Under Art. 404, the auditor and any assistants must act honestly and impartially and keep business secrets confidential; intentional or negligent breach triggers joint liability toward the company (and affiliated companies for resulting loss). Liability caps apply for negligence — roughly TRY 100,000 per audit for non-listed companies, TRY 300,000 for listed ones — but cannot be excluded or narrowed by contract, and claims prescribe in five years from the report date. Whether third parties relying on the report (creditors, for instance) can sue the auditor directly under this article remains debated; Turkish courts have so far tended to route such claims through general tort rules and impose procedural prerequisites, such as exhausting remedies against the primary debtor first.

## Group-Level Special Audit

Art. 405 sends disagreements between the company and its auditor over financial statement interpretation to the local commercial court for a final decision, with costs borne by the company. Art. 406 is more distinctive: if the auditor issues a qualified opinion or disclaimer regarding relations with the controlling company or group companies, or the board discloses an uncompensated loss caused by the group, any single shareholder — no minority threshold required — may ask the court to appoint a special auditor to examine that relationship. This is a narrower, more direct route than the general special-audit mechanism under TTK Art. 438-444.
