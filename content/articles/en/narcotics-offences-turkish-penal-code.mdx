Foreign nationals stopped at a Turkish airport or roadside checkpoint with any quantity of narcotics quickly discover that the outcome of their case depends less on the substance itself than on which of two very different legal boxes the police report places them in. Turkish criminal law treats drug trafficking and drug production under Article 188 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) as one of the most severely punished categories of crime in the entire code, while treating simple possession for personal use under Article 191 as a comparatively lenient matter resolved mostly through supervised treatment rather than imprisonment. Because both offences can arise from the same physical act — being found in possession of a bag of pills or a wrap of powder — understanding how Turkish prosecutors and judges draw the line between them is essential for anyone facing a narcotics investigation in Turkey.

## The Underlying Policy: Protecting Public Health, Not Just Punishing Individuals

Turkish narcotics law is built around the idea that drug circulation threatens public health and social order as a whole, not merely the health of the individual user. For that reason, the relevant offences — trafficking under Article 188, facilitating third-party use under Article 190, and personal possession under Article 191 — are all classified as offences of abstract danger. None of them requires proof that any specific person was actually harmed. Manufacturing a narcotic substance, offering it for sale, or simply holding it for consumption is punishable on its own, because the law presumes that the mere circulation of narcotics creates risk for the community, independent of any concrete injury in the case file.

## Three Separate Offences, Not One

Rather than folding every drug-related act into a single crime, the Turkish legislator separated the conduct into three distinct offences of increasing leniency. Article 188 targets production, importation, exportation and domestic trafficking — the commercial end of the chain. Article 190 targets people who facilitate someone else's drug use without using the drug themselves, for example by providing a location or equipment for consumption. Article 191 is reserved for a person who acquires, accepts or holds a narcotic substance, or personally uses it, purely for their own consumption, with no commercial purpose whatsoever. These three provisions carry dramatically different penalties and, critically, trigger entirely different procedural tracks — which is exactly why the classification dispute matters so much in practice.

## How the Trafficking Offence Is Structured

Article 188 can be committed by anyone, and the victim is not a specific person but society at large. The provision punishes several alternative acts, graded by severity. Manufacturing, importing or exporting narcotics without authorisation sits at the top of the scale, carrying a prison term of twenty to thirty years together with a substantial judicial fine. Domestic acts — selling, offering for sale, supplying to others, dispatching, transporting, storing, purchasing, accepting or simply holding narcotics without authorisation — carry a markedly lower but still severe minimum sentence of ten years' imprisonment, again with a judicial fine. Any single one of these acts is enough to complete the offence; committing several of them in relation to the same transaction still amounts to one crime, not several. The same rules extend, with appropriate adjustment, to substances that produce a narcotic effect but whose production or sale is normally subject to official licence or medical prescription, and to precursor chemicals used to manufacture narcotics even though those chemicals have no narcotic effect themselves.

Article 190, by contrast, punishes a different kind of conduct: providing a place, equipment or materials for someone else's drug use, helping users evade detection, explaining consumption methods, or publicly promoting drug use. Here the person facilitating the act is not the one consuming the drug — a structural feature that separates this offence from both Article 188 and Article 191.

## The Trafficking/Personal-Use Distinction and Why It Is So Heavily Litigated

Like the other narcotics offences, Article 191 requires general intent, since none of these crimes can be committed negligently. What sets Article 191 apart is that the intent must specifically be an intent to use the substance oneself. This is precisely where the most contested question in Turkish narcotics practice arises: the same physical act — holding a quantity of a narcotic substance — can fall under the harsh trafficking provisions of Article 188 or the far more lenient personal-use provision of Article 191, depending entirely on the purpose behind it. Turkish courts do not rely on any single decisive factor to make that call. Instead, they weigh the whole picture together: whether the quantity found exceeds what a personal user would realistically consume, whether the substance is packaged as a single unit or split into numerous small packets suggesting individual sale-ready doses, the defendant's economic and social circumstances, whether multiple different types of narcotics were found together, and whether trafficking paraphernalia — a precision scale, empty small bags, unused packaging material — was present alongside the drugs. No one of these factors is automatically conclusive; a judge assesses them cumulatively in light of the specific facts of the case.

## Aggravating Circumstances

Article 188 sets out detailed aggravating factors. The penalty is increased by half where the substance involved is heroin, cocaine, morphine, base morphine, a synthetic cannabinoid or its derivatives, a synthetic cathinone or its derivatives, a synthetic opioid or its derivatives, or amphetamine or its derivatives, and likewise where the act is committed within two hundred metres of a school, dormitory, hospital, barracks, place of worship, or similar institution where people gather for treatment, education, military or social purposes. Committing the offence together with three or more people also raises the penalty by half, and committing it within the framework of an organised criminal group doubles it. Commission by a healthcare professional — a physician, dentist, pharmacist, chemist, veterinarian, nurse, or similar — is treated as an aggravating factor under both Article 188 and Article 190, again increasing the sentence by half. Article 191 preserves its own, separate aggravating circumstance tied to proximity to the same kinds of protected locations, likewise increasing the penalty by half.

## Sentencing and the Distinct Procedure Reserved for Personal Use

Article 191 carries a base sentence of two to five years' imprisonment, but what truly sets it apart from Articles 188 and 190 is the special procedural track that accompanies it. At the investigation stage, prosecutors can defer filing an indictment for five years without needing to satisfy the general conditions that would normally apply to such a deferral. During that five-year period, the suspect is placed under supervised probation for at least one year and, where appropriate, ordered into treatment. Only a breach of these obligations, or a renewed instance of drug use, triggers the filing of formal charges. If a case is instead already before the court under Article 188 or Article 190 and it becomes apparent during trial that the conduct actually falls within Article 191, the court can suspend the announcement of its verdict rather than record a conviction. Both mechanisms reflect a deliberate policy choice: a person found to be a genuine personal user is steered toward supervision and treatment, in sharp contrast to the lengthy prison terms that Articles 188 and 190 impose on trafficking and facilitation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If I am found with a small quantity of drugs, will I automatically be charged only with personal possession?**
No. Quantity is one factor among several that Turkish courts weigh, but it is not decisive on its own. Packaging, the presence of trafficking paraphernalia, the variety of substances found, and the surrounding circumstances of the arrest can still lead prosecutors to pursue a trafficking charge even where the quantity is modest.

**Can a foreign national benefit from the deferred-prosecution and treatment track under Article 191?**
The procedural mechanisms under Article 191 apply based on the legal classification of the offence rather than the defendant's nationality, but foreign nationals typically face additional practical complications — such as travel restrictions during a multi-year supervision period — that make early legal advice especially important.

**Is attempting to import or produce narcotics punished the same as completing the act?**
Because Article 188 involves conduct that can be broken down into distinct stages, an attempt is recognised where the defendant began carrying out the act but could not complete it for reasons beyond their control. The court applies the general attempt provisions of the Penal Code when assessing such cases.

**Does cooperating with the investigation reduce a trafficking sentence?**
Turkish law does contain effective-cooperation provisions that can reduce a sentence where a defendant provides information that genuinely assists the authorities in uncovering other offenders. Whether cooperation qualifies depends heavily on how the investigating authorities acted on the information provided, which is a fact-specific question decided case by case.
