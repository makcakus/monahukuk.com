Foreign nationals and international counsel encountering a Turkish criminal file involving counterfeit banknotes, coins, or stamped instruments are often surprised to learn that the offence is not framed around who lost money, but around the reliability of the currency system itself. Article 197 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), together with the related provisions that follow it, treats currency forgery as an attack on a collective, state-backed interest rather than a private wrong, which explains both its severe sentencing range and its unusually broad reach into preparatory conduct. This article explains how the offence is structured, who can be held liable at each stage of contact with counterfeit money, and what defences and mitigating mechanisms Turkish law makes available.

## The Interest the Law Protects: Public Trust, Not Individual Loss

Currency forgery sits in the section of the Turkish Penal Code dealing with offences against public trust, alongside forgery of official seals and documents. This placement is deliberate. What the law protects is not the wallet of whoever happens to be holding a counterfeit note, but society's confidence that money in circulation is genuine and that the state's monetary system functions as represented. Because currency is a visible symbol of a state's economic sovereignty, an attack on its authenticity is treated as a harm to the public at large rather than to any specific person. As a practical consequence, the offence does not require proof that an identifiable victim suffered financial loss — it is enough that conduct capable of undermining the currency system took place. There is no meaningful "victim" in the ordinary sense; the state, and through it the public, occupies that position.

## The Conduct Covered: Turkish and Foreign Currency Alike

The core offence in Article 197(1) is built around several alternative acts, any one of which is sufficient to complete the crime: producing counterfeit currency, bringing it into the country, transporting it, storing it, or putting it into circulation. A defendant does not need to have done all of these things — a single one of them, proven, is enough. Two features of the provision stand out. First, it protects not only Turkish currency but also currency lawfully in circulation in foreign countries, reflecting the view that international monetary confidence is itself worth protecting under Turkish law. Second, "putting into circulation" — using counterfeit currency in a transaction as though it were genuine — is treated as the point at which the conduct does the most concrete damage to public trust, even though the offence does not require that any particular transaction actually succeed or that anyone actually be deceived. Because the crime is defined by the commission of any one of these acts rather than by a specific resulting harm, no separate "result" needs to be shown for the offence to be complete.

## Graduated Liability for Those Who Receive Counterfeit Money

Articles 197(2) and 197(3) create a scale of liability for people who come into contact with counterfeit currency without having produced or first circulated it. A person who knowingly accepts counterfeit money — for example, taking it in payment while aware it is fake — commits a separate, more lightly punished offence than the person who manufactured or circulated it, because simply accepting counterfeit money poses a more limited danger to the currency system than producing or actively circulating it does. A further, more unusual scenario is addressed separately: someone who receives counterfeit money in good faith, without knowing it is fake, and only later discovers the truth, commits an offence only if they then choose to pass it on despite that later knowledge. Up to the moment of discovery, this person has done nothing wrong; liability attaches specifically to the deliberate decision to circulate the currency after learning of its true nature. The declining severity across these three tiers shows that the law calibrates punishment closely to how much a person knew and how deliberately they acted.

## Instruments Treated as Equivalent to Currency

Article 198 extends the reach of Article 197 to a defined list of instruments that are not currency in the everyday sense but are treated as if they were for purposes of this offence: bearer bonds, shares, debentures and coupons issued by the state; bills, bonds and other instruments issued by authorised institutions and lawfully in circulation; and gold coins recognised as national bullion currency. This is not a matter of loose analogy — it is an explicit statutory instruction, meaning every paragraph of Article 197, including its graduated liability structure, applies directly to these instruments in the same way it applies to ordinary currency.

## Forgery of Valuable Stamps as a Distinct Offence

Article 199 addresses a related but separate category: forgery of valuable stamps, defined to include stamped paper, revenue and postage stamps, and any stamp used to document payment of a particular tax or fee. The structure mirrors Article 197 closely — production, importation, transport, storage, and circulation as alternative acts, knowing acceptance, and later circulation upon discovering the forgery — but the penalties are set at a lower range, reflecting the more limited economic and social impact of a forged stamp compared to forged currency.

## Criminalising Preparation: Tools and Materials for Forgery

Article 200 departs from the usual criminal-law principle that mere preparation is not itself punishable. Producing, importing, selling, transferring, purchasing, accepting, or storing tools or materials used to manufacture counterfeit currency or valuable stamps is criminalised as a standalone offence, even if no counterfeit currency or stamp has actually been produced yet. The legislature carved out this exception because of the seriousness of the threat such tools pose to public trust, allowing criminal law to intervene before any forged item even exists, let alone reaches circulation.

## Fault, Attempt and Related Issues

All of these offences require intent; none of them can be committed through mere negligence. In particular, under Articles 197(2) and 197(3), the defendant's knowledge that the money is counterfeit is a core element of the offence — if a person genuinely did not know the currency was fake, intent is absent and liability cannot be established. Because several of these offences are defined by alternative acts, an attempt is possible where the conduct was begun but not completed for reasons outside the defendant's control — most notably where an attempt to put counterfeit currency into circulation is interrupted partway through. There is no special restriction on liability for participants who act together; the law's effective-cooperation provisions in Article 201, discussed below, in fact presuppose that more than one person may be involved. Where a single person both produces and later circulates the same counterfeit currency, this is generally treated as one offence, though repeated acts carried out at different times and places may instead be charged as a continuing series of offences.

## Effective Cooperation: Avoiding Punishment by Coming Forward

Article 201 provides an unusually generous route out of punishment for anyone involved in producing, importing, transporting, storing or accepting counterfeit currency or valuable stamps, or in supplying the tools used to make them. If, before putting the counterfeit items into circulation and before the authorities have learned of the matter independently, the person reports their accomplices and the location where the counterfeit currency or stamps (or the manufacturing tools) were produced or kept, and that information actually leads to the accomplices' capture and the seizure of the counterfeit material or equipment, no penalty is imposed. This mechanism makes clear that the legislature's principal concern is preventing counterfeit currency from ever reaching circulation and neutralising the threat to public trust, not simply punishing the individuals involved.

## Penalties and Prosecution

Sentencing scales down sharply according to the degree of involvement. Producing or circulating counterfeit currency under Article 197(1) carries a prison term of two to twelve years together with a judicial fine of up to ten thousand day-units; knowingly accepting counterfeit currency carries one to three years; and circulating currency after later discovering it is counterfeit carries only three months to one year. Forgery of valuable stamps under Article 199 is punished more lightly than currency forgery, in a range of one to five years, and the tool-and-material offence under Article 200 carries one to four years. Because these are offences against public trust rather than against a private complainant, they are, as a rule, prosecuted automatically by the public authorities and do not depend on a private criminal complaint being filed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the victim have to prove financial loss for a currency forgery charge to proceed?**
No. Because the protected interest is public trust in the currency system rather than any individual's assets, the offence does not require proof that a specific person suffered a financial loss.

**Is accepting counterfeit money in good faith itself a crime?**
No, not on its own. A person who receives counterfeit currency without knowing it is fake commits no offence at that point. Liability arises only if, after later learning the currency is counterfeit, that person chooses to pass it on to someone else.

**Can someone avoid punishment by reporting a counterfeiting operation to the authorities?**
Yes, under specific conditions. Article 201 exempts a person from punishment if, before the counterfeit currency or stamps are circulated and before the authorities independently discover the matter, they report their accomplices and the relevant production or storage location, and this information leads to the accomplices' capture and the seizure of the counterfeit items or equipment.

**Does possessing equipment used to make counterfeit money require that counterfeit currency already exist?**
No. Article 200 criminalises producing, importing, selling, transferring, purchasing, accepting or storing tools or materials used to manufacture counterfeit currency or valuable stamps as a standalone offence, even where no counterfeit item has yet been produced.
