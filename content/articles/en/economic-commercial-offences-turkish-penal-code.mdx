Foreign businesses operating in Turkey rarely expect ordinary commercial conduct to carry criminal exposure, yet a dedicated chapter of the Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) — Articles 238 through 242 — turns certain supply-chain, confidentiality and lending practices into criminal offences. These provisions sit under the heading of offences against the economy, industry and trade, and they share a single underlying purpose: keeping goods and services flowing to the public, keeping commercial and banking secrets from leaking to unauthorised hands, and keeping unregulated, profit-driven lending in check. Because the interest protected is the economic order itself rather than a single identifiable victim, these offences are prosecuted differently from most property crimes, and the criminal exposure can attach to executives, employees and third parties who might not think of themselves as potential defendants.

## The Protected Interest: Market Confidence, Not a Single Victim

Unlike theft or fraud, the offences in this chapter do not require an identifiable victim who suffers a direct loss. What Articles 238, 239, 240 and 241 have in common is that they safeguard the functioning of the market as a public good — uninterrupted access to essential goods and services, confidence that commercial and banking secrets will not be leaked, and protection against exploitative, unregulated credit relationships. Because the class of people affected is typically the public at large rather than one victim, prosecution for these four offences generally proceeds automatically, without requiring anyone to file a formal complaint — the one significant exception being the basic form of the trade-secret offence, discussed below. Article 242, by contrast, is not a separate offence at all; it is a mechanism that allows courts to impose security measures on a legal entity that benefited from one of these crimes.

## Causing a Shortage of Goods the Public Needs (Article 238)

This offence can only be committed by someone who has undertaken to perform a specific obligation — typically a supply or delivery commitment — and then fails to perform it. Failing to perform the commitment is not, by itself, enough to trigger criminal liability: the law additionally requires that this failure actually cause the disappearance or a significant reduction of goods or foodstuffs that are essential for public institutions, a public service, or averting a general disaster. In other words, this is a result-based offence, not a conduct-based one — the shortage itself has to materialise, not merely be risked. In practice, this provision maps closely onto hoarding or black-market conduct designed to create artificial scarcity. The penalty is imprisonment from one to three years plus a judicial fine of up to one thousand days. Because intent is required, the prosecution must show that the defendant knowingly and willingly failed to perform the obligation and at least foresaw the resulting shortage as a likely consequence.

## Disclosure of Trade Secrets, Banking Secrets or Customer Information (Article 239)

This is a status-based offence: only a person who has access to the secret information by virtue of their title, duties, profession or craft can commit it in its core form. The prohibited conduct is handing over or disclosing that information to unauthorised persons, and the basic version carries imprisonment from one to three years plus a judicial fine of up to five thousand days. The law extends liability beyond the original secret-holder: anyone who obtains the same information unlawfully and then discloses it to an unauthorised person falls under the same provision. A second paragraph clarifies that the offence also covers technical discoveries, inventions and industrial application know-how, not just commercial or financial data in the narrow sense. An aggravated form applies where the secret is disclosed to a foreign national not resident in Turkey, or to that person's staff — the penalty increases by one-third, and unlike the basic offence, prosecution proceeds automatically rather than on complaint. A separate and more serious variant addresses coercion: forcing someone, through violence or threats, to disclose this information is treated as an independent offence carrying imprisonment from three to seven years. In practice, this article is applied alongside Turkey's data protection law (Law No. 6698) and banking-sector confidentiality rules; administrative or disciplinary sanctions under those regimes do not displace criminal liability under the Penal Code.

## Refusal to Sell Goods or Services (Article 240)

Anyone in a position to sell a particular good or service can commit this offence — it is not limited to a defined class of persons. The conduct consists of refusing to make that sale, and completion of the offence requires that an urgent public need for the good or service actually arise as a result. The only sanction is imprisonment from one to three years; unlike Article 238, this provision does not carry a judicial fine. It is worth noting that the current one-to-three-year range replaced a previous six-months-to-two-years range under amendments introduced by Law No. 7413 in 2022, reflecting a legislative tightening of the penalty. Economically, this offence captures conduct that disrupts the balance between supply and demand and, indirectly, distorts pricing; in practice it is frequently charged together with Article 238 in cases involving black-market behaviour and artificially created shortages.

## Loan-Sharking (Article 241)

There is no restriction on who can commit this offence: anyone who lends money to another person for profit can be a perpetrator, and the conduct is punishable by imprisonment from two to six years plus a judicial fine ranging from five hundred to five thousand days. Although the statutory text does not spell out an explicit continuity requirement, both legal commentary and judicial practice treat habitual lending as a necessary element — a single, isolated loan transaction falls outside the scope of the offence. The mental element is a specific intent: the purpose of earning interest-based profit is a constitutive part of the crime. Amendments introduced by Law No. 7242 in 2020 raised the maximum term of imprisonment under the first paragraph from five to six years and set the current judicial-fine range, while a newly added second paragraph treats commission of the offence within the framework of an organised group as an aggravating circumstance that increases the penalty by half again. Interest-based lending carried out by banks, financing companies and factoring institutions operating under official licence does not conflict with the interest this article protects and therefore falls outside its scope entirely.

## Security Measures Against Legal Entities (Article 242)

This provision is not an independent offence. It is a sanctioning bridge that allows the Penal Code's general provisions on security measures — such as revocation of an operating licence or confiscation — to be applied against a legal entity that derived an unjust benefit from one of the offences in this chapter. It addresses the legal entity's exposure to a security measure, not its criminal liability as such.

## Attempt, Complicity and Concurrence

For the result-based offences under Articles 238 and 240, attempt is possible where the course of conduct can be broken down into discrete stages before the required outcome materialises. Under the basic form of Article 239, disclosure is typically completed through a single, instantaneous act, so attempt remains an exceptional scenario there. Because Article 241 requires habitual conduct, a single act of lending does not amount to an attempt — it simply falls outside the definition of the offence altogether; where multiple lending transactions occur in a connected sequence, however, that pattern reinforces the habitual character required for the offence to be made out. The Penal Code's general rules on complicity (Articles 37-41) apply to all of these offences, and the distinction between instigation and aiding becomes particularly relevant to the coercion-based disclosure offence under Article 239(4). On concurrence, where a single course of conduct implicates both the failure to perform an undertaking and a refusal to sell, the case must be assessed under the single-act, single-offence principle; and where a secret-disclosure charge overlaps with personal-data offences, the specific-general norm relationship governs which provision applies.

## Complaint Requirement, Penalties and Procedure

A formal complaint is required only for the basic form of the Article 239 disclosure offence; its aggravated form, along with Articles 238, 240 and 241, are prosecuted automatically. Penalties vary meaningfully across the chapter: Articles 238 and 240 both carry imprisonment from one to three years, but only Article 238 adds a judicial fine; the basic form of Article 239 carries imprisonment from one to three years and a judicial fine of up to five thousand days, rising to imprisonment from three to seven years for the aggravated, coercion-based form; and Article 241 carries imprisonment from two to six years plus a judicial fine of five hundred to five thousand days, increased by half again where the conduct occurs within an organised group. These cases are tried before the courts of first instance for criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi). Establishing whether information genuinely qualifies as a protected secret, or whether lending activity meets the habitual-conduct threshold, frequently requires expert examination in practice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does every failed business commitment risk criminal liability under Article 238?**
No. Article 238 requires that the failure to perform an undertaking actually cause a shortage or significant reduction of goods or foodstuffs essential to a public institution, public service, or disaster prevention. A commercial breach without that specific consequence, and without intent, does not trigger this offence.

**Can a single loan at a high interest rate amount to loan-sharking under Article 241?**
Generally no. Turkish courts and commentary treat habitual, repeated lending for profit as a necessary element of this offence. An isolated, one-off loan between two parties typically falls outside its scope, though a pattern of connected lending transactions can support a finding of habitual conduct.

**Is disclosing a trade secret always prosecuted automatically, or does someone have to file a complaint?**
It depends on the form. The basic disclosure offence under Article 239 requires a complaint from the affected party. Its aggravated form — disclosure to a foreign national not resident in Turkey or to that person's staff — and the coercion-based variant are both prosecuted automatically.

**Does a bank or licensed financing company risk prosecution under the loan-sharking provision for charging interest?**
No. Interest-based lending by banks, financing companies and factoring institutions operating under official licence does not conflict with the interest Article 241 protects, so this regulated lending activity falls outside the definition of the offence.
