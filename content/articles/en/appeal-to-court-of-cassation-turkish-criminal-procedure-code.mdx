Anyone facing criminal proceedings in Turkey — whether a foreign national, an expat resident, or a family member trying to understand a relative's case — eventually runs into a confusing three-tier appellate structure. After a first-instance criminal court issues a verdict, the case can move to a regional court of appeal (bölge adliye mahkemesi), and only after that stage is exhausted does a further appeal to the Court of Cassation (Yargıtay) become possible, and only in some cases. This final-stage appeal is governed principally by Article 286 of the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (CMK), which limits which appellate rulings may reach the Court of Cassation at all, together with Articles 288, 289, 302 and 303, which define what the Court of Cassation actually reviews and what it can do with a case once it accepts it. Understanding this framework matters because it determines, in practical terms, whether a conviction becomes final at the regional court stage or can still be challenged before Turkey's highest ordinary court.

## A Filtered Gateway, Not an Automatic Right

Unlike the appeal from a first-instance court to a regional court of appeal — which generally allows a fresh look at both the facts and the law — the further appeal to the Court of Cassation is not available for every case that has already gone through a regional appeal. Article 286 restricts this last-resort remedy to regional court criminal-chamber decisions that are not themselves remittal rulings; a decision sending a case back down to a lower court for a new hearing is treated as an interim step, not a final judgment, and therefore cannot itself be taken up to the Court of Cassation. Beyond that, the law carves out a substantial list of exceptions based on the severity of the sentence and the nature of the underlying offense. Cases involving relatively short prison terms (broadly, up to five years) or judicial fines regardless of amount, cases where the regional court did not increase a first-instance sentence, minor offenses carrying a statutory ceiling of up to two years' imprisonment, offenses punishable only by judicial fine, and rulings limited to confiscation questions are generally excluded from this final appeal stage — they become final once the regional court of appeal has ruled. The practical effect is to concentrate the Court of Cassation's attention on more serious cases while allowing lower-stakes matters to conclude more quickly.

That said, the legislature carved out its own exception to this exception. A defined set of offenses — most of them touching on freedom of expression, public order, or the standing of core state institutions (including several Turkish Penal Code provisions on insult, incitement, and offenses against state symbols or the president), along with certain terrorism-related offenses and offenses tied to public assemblies and demonstrations — remain appealable to the Court of Cassation even where the sentence involved would otherwise fall below the general threshold. For anyone charged under one of these categories, the case retains access to the full three-tier review regardless of how light the sentence might appear on its face.

## The Standard of Review: Unlawfulness Only, Not the Facts

A defendant who successfully obtains a regional appeal often assumes the next stage works the same way — a fresh factual review. It does not. Article 288 confines the Court of Cassation's review strictly to whether the underlying decision is unlawful, meaning that a legal rule was either not applied or was misapplied. The Court of Cassation is not a third fact-finder; it does not re-weigh witness credibility or re-examine evidence to reach its own view of what happened. This distinction has direct practical consequences for how an appeal petition should be drafted. Established case law from the Grand Criminal Chamber of the Court of Cassation draws a line between two categories of unlawfulness claims. Where the complaint concerns a substantive-law error, a general assertion that the decision is "contrary to procedure and law" is treated as sufficient, and the Court of Cassation may in fact identify and act on substantive-law defects on its own initiative even if the appellant never specifically raised them. Where the complaint instead concerns a procedural irregularity, however, the appellant is expected to identify precisely which procedural rule was breached and to show how that breach affected the outcome — a bare, generic objection will not do.

## Absolute Grounds for Reversal Under Article 289

Certain categories of defect are treated as so fundamental to a fair proceeding that the Court of Cassation must take them into account even if the appellant never mentions them in the appeal petition. Article 289 lists these absolute grounds, which include: a court that was not properly constituted under the law; participation in the judgment by a judge who was legally barred from sitting on the case, or by a judge whose recusal had been properly requested and either wrongly granted (and the judge sat anyway) or wrongly denied; a court that wrongly asserted or denied its own jurisdiction; a hearing conducted without the presence of the public prosecutor or another participant whose presence the law mandates; failure to observe the required openness of a hearing where judgment was delivered orally; a judgment that fails to include the statement of reasons the law requires; a court ruling that improperly restricted the defense's rights on a matter material to the outcome; and a judgment resting on evidence obtained through unlawful means.

In practice, the courts read several of these categories narrowly rather than expansively. For instance, a procedural shortcoming in how evidence was handled during the hearing — such as evidence not being formally read out — does not automatically transform the evidence itself into evidence "obtained through unlawful means"; the unlawfulness in that scenario, if any, lies in how the hearing was conducted, not in the origin of the evidence. Similarly, the requirement that the court be "properly constituted" is understood to cover only the absence of essential participants the law requires (a judge, a prosecutor, mandatory defense counsel, and the like), not every procedural disagreement about how a panel was composed.

## What Happens After Review: Affirmation, Reversal, or a Direct Ruling

Once the Court of Cassation completes its review, three outcomes are possible under Articles 302 and 303. If the reviewed decision is found lawful, the appeal is rejected on the merits and the decision stands. If the Court of Cassation identifies a specific unlawfulness that affected the outcome, it reverses the decision and, ordinarily, sends the case back down for a new ruling — and in doing so, it must record in its ruling not only the defects the appellant raised, but any other unlawfulness it discovered in the file, even if the appellant never raised it. As an exception to sending the case back, Article 303 allows the Court of Cassation, in a narrow and specifically enumerated set of situations, to rule on the merits itself rather than remitting the case: where an acquittal or discontinuation is clearly warranted without further fact-finding, where only the statutory minimum sentence needs to be applied, where a legal provision was simply mis-cited, or where there was a computational error in calculating the sentence. This power to decide a case outright is treated as exceptional and is applied narrowly — it is not a general discretion but is limited to the specific circumstances the law lists, consistent with the broader goal of bringing cases to a final resolution within a reasonable time.

## How This Differs From the Earlier Appeal and From a Retrial

It helps to see where this final appeal sits relative to the other remedies available in a Turkish criminal case. The appeal to a regional court of appeal, which precedes the Court of Cassation stage, is an ordinary remedy available before a judgment becomes final, and it can revisit both the facts and the law. The appeal to the Court of Cassation is also an ordinary remedy pursued before finality, but its scope is narrower — confined to lawfulness — and it represents the last ordinary stage; if no timely appeal is filed, the judgment becomes final. A retrial (yargılamanın yenilenmesi), by contrast, is an extraordinary remedy that can only be pursued against a judgment that has already become final, on a limited set of grounds set out in the law — and it remains available even after a case has been through Court of Cassation review, or even after a sentence has already been served, provided new evidence or a qualifying event later comes to light. The fact that the Court of Cassation upheld a conviction, in other words, does not close the door on a retrial if a genuinely new basis for one later emerges; the two remedies operate at different points in time and serve different purposes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can every criminal conviction be appealed all the way to the Court of Cassation?**
No. Many regional court of appeal decisions involving shorter sentences, judicial fines, or minor offenses are excluded from further appeal and become final once the regional court has ruled, except for a specific category of sensitive offenses (including certain expression-related and public-order offenses) that remain appealable regardless of sentence length.

**Does the Court of Cassation re-examine the facts of the case?**
No. Its review is limited to whether the law was correctly applied — it does not reassess witness testimony or reweigh the evidence to form its own view of what happened, unlike the earlier appeal to the regional court of appeal.

**What if my appeal petition does not specify a particular legal error?**
It depends on the type of error. For substantive legal errors, a general statement that the ruling is unlawful can be sufficient, since the court may identify such errors on its own. For procedural errors, however, the specific rule breached and its effect on the outcome generally need to be identified.

**If the Court of Cassation upholds my conviction, is that truly the end of the case?**
In the ordinary course, yes — the judgment becomes final. However, an extraordinary retrial remedy remains available afterward if new evidence or another qualifying ground under the law later comes to light, even after the Court of Cassation's review or after a sentence has been served.
