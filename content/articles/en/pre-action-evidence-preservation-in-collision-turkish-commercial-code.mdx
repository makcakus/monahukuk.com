Having covered who is liable for a collision and to what degree, the series now turns to a procedural question that arises before any lawsuit is even filed: how is evidence of the collision preserved while it is still fresh (TTK Art. 1292)?

## A Tiered Hierarchy of Competent Courts

Art. 1292(1) sets a three-step hierarchy for the court competent to order pre-action evidence preservation: first, the commercial court of first instance at the place of the collision with specific jurisdiction over maritime trade matters; if none exists there, the general commercial court of first instance; and if neither exists, the civil court of first instance handling commercial cases. This balances two needs — speed and expertise. Evidence like the extent of damage, the vessels' positions, weather conditions, and crew statements can deteriorate or disappear within days, so preservation needs to happen fast at whatever court is locally available; but collision cases are also highly technical, so the law's first preference is still a maritime-specialized court where one exists.

## Notice to the Masters

Art. 1292(2) requires that the master (or the master's representative) of every ship involved in the collision — not just the party requesting preservation — be notified that the preservation process will take place. This mirrors the hearing-notice principle seen elsewhere in maritime procedure: each party affected by an evidentiary step should have the chance to be present, so the resulting record cannot later be dismissed as one-sided. Notice goes to the master (or a local representative such as an agent) rather than the shipowner directly, since the master is the person with immediate, first-hand knowledge of the vessel's condition and is present on the scene — notice to a distant or foreign shipowner could defeat the urgency of the process.

## No Fault Percentages in the Report

Perhaps the most notable rule is Art. 1292(3): the preservation report may not state the colliding ships' percentages of fault. This may seem counterintuitive given how central fault apportionment is to the outcome of a collision case, but it reflects a clean separation of function. Evidence preservation is meant only to capture facts — the state of the damage, positions, conditions, statements — while it is still fresh; determining fault requires a full legal evaluation (of navigation-rule compliance, for example) that belongs to the trial itself, where both sides can be properly heard and cross-examined.
