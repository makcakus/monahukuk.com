Under Turkish criminal procedure, a judgment that has become final is meant to stay final — that finality is what gives the system its stability and gives the parties legal certainty. Yet the legislator recognized that certainty cannot always be allowed to override substantive truth, and built a narrow safety valve into the Criminal Procedure Code (CMK): retrial, known in Turkish as *yargılamanın yenilenmesi*. Regulated primarily by Article 311 and the surrounding provisions, retrial is not a further round of ordinary appellate review. It is a last-resort mechanism that reopens a case only after the ordinary remedies have been exhausted, and only on a short, closed list of serious grounds suggesting that the original judgment rested on a false factual foundation.

## An Extraordinary Remedy, Not a Second Appeal

Turkish appellate courts consistently describe retrial as an extraordinary remedy that operates on a judgment already final — including one that has already been enforced, and even one concerning a defendant who has since died. That is what separates it most sharply from Turkey's ordinary remedies. An appeal on points of fact and law (*istinaf*) or an appeal on points of law (*temyiz*, discussed in our companion articles) can only be used against a judgment that has not yet become final, and both are subject to strict filing deadlines; once those deadlines pass without use, the right to challenge the judgment through those channels is lost for good. Retrial works on the opposite premise. It has no general filing deadline at all, and it is triggered only after the case has run its full ordinary course — meaning that a conviction upheld on appeal is not shielded from retrial if one of the statutory grounds later comes to light. The petition itself is filed with the very court that issued the original judgment, not with a higher instance, and it opens the door to a fresh examination of the same defendant and the same underlying act — not a review of legal reasoning in the abstract, but a substantive re-litigation of what actually happened.

## Grounds for Retrial in Favor of a Convicted Person

Article 311 sets out an exhaustive list of grounds on which a case may be reopened in the convicted person's favor, and Turkish courts do not extend this list by analogy. Four of the grounds concern a discovery that some element the original judgment relied on was corrupted from the outset: that a document used at trial and material to the outcome later turns out to have been forged; that a witness or expert who testified under oath gave knowingly or negligently false testimony that worked against the convicted person and affected the outcome; that a judge who took part in the judgment committed a breach of duty against the convicted person serious enough to warrant criminal prosecution or conviction; or that a civil court judgment which the criminal conviction was based on has since been set aside by another final judgment. A separate ground, discussed below, concerns newly discovered evidence. The final ground is available where the European Court of Human Rights has issued a final decision finding that the conviction violated the Convention or its protocols, or where an application to that Court concerning the conviction was struck out following a friendly settlement or a unilateral declaration by the government. This ECHR-based ground is the one exception to the rule of no deadline: the petition must be filed within one year of the date the European Court's decision becomes final.

## What Counts as "New Evidence": The Boundaries of Article 311/1(e)

The ground invoked most often in practice — and the one that generates the most litigation — is the "new facts or new evidence" provision of Article 311/1(e). Turkish appellate case law applies a demanding two-part test. First, the evidence or fact must be new in a specific sense: it must not have been placed before the court that issued the judgment, so that the court had no opportunity to weigh it at the time. Crucially, newness is judged from the court's perspective, not the convicted person's — it does not matter whether the defendant personally already knew about it. Evidence that was already submitted to the trial court but simply found unpersuasive does not become "new" merely because it is raised again, and a fresh expert opinion that reaches a different conclusion from the same underlying material is not new evidence either, unless it is grounded in genuinely new scientific data. Second, the evidence or fact must be material: taken alone or together with the evidence already in the file, it must be serious enough to have realistically led to an acquittal or a more lenient legal outcome. This combined newness-and-materiality standard exists precisely to stop retrial from becoming an informal third round of appeal in which a conviction is simply argued over again.

## Retrial Against a Defendant or Convicted Person

Retrial is not a one-way street reserved for convicted persons. Article 314 provides for the mirror-image scenario: reopening a case against a defendant or convicted person, on a deliberately narrower set of grounds. These cover a document that was used in the defendant's favor and later turns out to have been forged, a judge who breached their duties in a way that favored the defendant to a degree warranting prosecution or conviction, and — perhaps the most distinctive ground — a defendant who, having been acquitted, subsequently makes a credible confession before a judge concerning the same offense. This route exists so that a miscoverage of justice can be corrected in either direction, not only toward acquittal. Turkish courts apply the closed-list principle even more strictly here, given how far reopening a completed acquittal can cut against the finality that criminal defendants are ordinarily entitled to rely on.

## Procedure: A Two-Stage Filter Before Any Retrial Is Granted

A retrial petition goes through two distinct stages, both handled by the court that issued the original judgment (or, where the Court of Cassation itself rendered the judgment directly, by that same court that heard the case at first instance). At the first stage, the court examines the petition purely on admissibility, without holding a hearing: if the filing does not meet the formal requirements, or if no ground recognized by law is actually invoked, the petition is dismissed at this point. If it clears that bar, the court may order the collection of evidence relevant to the claimed ground. Once that evidence-gathering is complete, the case moves to the second stage, where the court assesses whether the claimed ground is actually well-founded and would genuinely have affected the original judgment; if it is not, the petition is again rejected without a hearing, but if it is, the court orders the retrial to proceed and a new hearing is opened, at which point the case is examined afresh on the merits. This two-tier filter is designed to prevent the extraordinary remedy from being used to relitigate settled convictions casually, while still leaving room for a genuinely serious claim of judicial error to be heard; decisions issued at either stage can themselves be challenged through an objection (*itiraz*). It is also worth noting that filing a retrial petition does not automatically suspend enforcement of the original judgment — the court has discretion to postpone enforcement, but it is not required to do so.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there a time limit for filing a retrial petition in Turkey?**
As a general rule, no — unlike ordinary appeals, retrial is not subject to a filing deadline, and it can even be pursued after the sentence has been fully served or after the convicted person's death. The single exception is the ground based on a European Court of Human Rights ruling, which must be invoked within one year of that ruling becoming final.

**Can I use retrial simply because I disagree with how the evidence was weighed at trial?**
No. Retrial is not a mechanism for re-arguing the trial court's assessment of evidence it already had before it. It applies only to the closed list of grounds in Article 311 (or, against a defendant, Article 314) — most commonly newly discovered evidence that the original court never had the chance to consider and that is serious enough to plausibly change the outcome.

**Does an unsuccessful appeal to the Court of Cassation rule out a later retrial?**
No. Because retrial and appeal serve entirely different functions, a judgment that was affirmed on appeal remains open to retrial if one of the statutory grounds — for example, later-discovered forgery of a document used at trial, or a qualifying European Court of Human Rights ruling — subsequently comes to light.

**Will filing a retrial petition automatically stop enforcement of my sentence while it is being examined?**
Not automatically. The court reviewing the petition has the discretion to postpone enforcement, but filing the petition by itself does not suspend a sentence that is already being carried out.
