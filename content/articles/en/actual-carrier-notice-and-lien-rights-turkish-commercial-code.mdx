TTK Art. 888-893 close out the carrier-liability chapter of Turkish carriage law with rules that matter most once a shipment has already gone wrong: who else can be sued, how fast a claim must be raised, and how a carrier actually gets paid.

## The Actual Carrier

Where a contracting carrier subcontracts all or part of the carriage to a third party, Art. 888 makes that actual carrier liable on the same terms as the contracting carrier for the portion it performed, entitled to the same defenses, and jointly and severally liable together with the contracting carrier — a claimant can sue either or both for the full amount. Turkish courts have repeatedly struck down lower-court judgments that tried to split liability between the two by fault percentage instead of applying true joint liability.

## Notice Deadlines That Actually Bite

Art. 889 sets a strict notice regime: visible damage must be flagged by the time of delivery or the goods are presumed delivered in conformity; hidden damage must be reported within seven days; and delay claims are barred entirely if not reported within twenty-one days of delivery. These are not soft suggestions — Turkish case law treats the twenty-one-day delay deadline as capable of defeating an otherwise valid claim outright.

## Jurisdiction, Lien, and Priority

Art. 890 adds the place of pickup or intended delivery as an alternative forum, plus cross-jurisdiction options between the contracting and actual carrier's places of business. Art. 891 gives the carrier a lien over the goods (and accompanying documents) for all claims arising from the carriage contract, contingent on the carrier still holding possession or documentary control. In chain carriage, Art. 892 lets a later carrier collect on behalf of earlier ones and preserves their lien through that mechanism, while Art. 893 resolves competing liens by favoring claims directly tied to the carriage over other liens, and — among carriage-related liens — favoring the most recently arisen claim, since the last carrier in the chain typically controls physical delivery.

## Practical Takeaway

A foreign cargo owner should calendar the Art. 889 notice deadlines the moment a shipment problem surfaces — missing the twenty-one-day delay window or the seven-day hidden-damage window can extinguish an otherwise strong claim regardless of its underlying merits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### If a sub-carrier performs the carriage, who can be sued?

Both. Under Article 888/1 TTK, where the carriage is performed wholly or partly by a third party — the <b>actual carrier</b> — that party is liable <b>as if it were the contracting carrier</b> for loss, damage or delay arising <b>during the leg it performed</b>. The contracting carrier retains its contractual liability; the two are jointly and severally liable for that loss and the claimant may proceed against either.

### Within what period must damage to the goods be notified?

Article 889 TTK lays down three periods. Loss or damage that is <b>apparent must be notified at the latest on delivery</b>; if <b>not apparent, within seven days after delivery</b> — failing which the goods are presumed to have been delivered in conformity with the contract. <b>Exceeding the delivery period</b> must be notified within <b>twenty-one days</b> of delivery, otherwise rights arising from the delay are extinguished. Notice given after delivery must be in writing.

### Does the carrier have a lien over the goods?

Yes. Under Article 891/1 TTK the carrier has a lien over the goods under Articles 950-953 of the Turkish Civil Code <b>for all claims arising out of the contract of carriage</b>. Where several liens exist over the same goods, Article 893/1 gives priority to those <b>directly connected with the carriage of the goods</b>. The lien is the carrier's strongest security for collecting the freight.
