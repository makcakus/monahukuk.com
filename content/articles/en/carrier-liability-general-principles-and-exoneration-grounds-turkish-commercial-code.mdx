TTK Art. 1178-1181 open the section of Turkish maritime law that generates the most litigation: when and how far a carrier answers for lost, damaged, or delayed cargo.

## Custody Is the Trigger, Not Fault

Under Art. 1178, a carrier owes the diligence of a prudent carrier throughout performance, but liability itself is triggered by something more mechanical: the loss, damage, or delay must have occurred while the cargo was in the carrier's *custody*. This is the architecture's foundation — liability attaches because the loss happened on the carrier's watch, not because a court first finds abstract fault. Custody begins when the carrier physically receives the cargo and ends at delivery to the consignee, at the point the cargo is held ready for a non-cooperative consignee, or at handover to authorities the destination port requires — whichever happens first.

## Two Powerful Presumptions

Art. 1178/4 presumes delay once cargo isn't delivered within the time the contract specifies (or, absent that, within what a prudent carrier could reasonably need). Art. 1178/5 goes further: if cargo remains undelivered for sixty consecutive days after that deadline, the claimant may treat it as a total loss — sparing them from proving the cargo is actually, physically gone. This is optional, not mandatory: claimants can still pursue a delay claim instead if the cargo eventually turns up.

## Exoneration Grounds

Art. 1179 lets the carrier escape liability for losses not caused by its own or its servants' intent or negligence — but the burden of proving the absence of fault sits with the carrier, making this a reversed-burden fault regime rather than strict liability. "Servants" is defined functionally, covering anyone the carrier actually uses to perform the contract, employed or not. Art. 1180 carves out a much narrower nautical-fault and fire exception: for damage from navigation or technical ship management (or from fire), the carrier answers only for its own personal fault, not its crew's — though measures taken mainly for cargo's benefit don't count as "technical management," and any doubt is resolved against the carrier. Art. 1181 exempts the carrier from damage caused by attempting to save life or property at sea, though a cargo-only salvage attempt must also be reasonable to qualify.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When does the carrier's liability begin and end?

Under Article 1178/3 TTK liability begins <b>at the moment the carrier takes the goods over from the loader or a person acting on its behalf, or from the authorities or third parties to whom the goods must be handed under the laws and regulations applicable at the port of loading</b>, and continues until the goods are delivered to the consignee or to the competent authority. This span is called the carrier's <b>period of custody</b>.

### What is the nautical-fault exception?

Under Article 1180/1 TTK, <b>where the loss results from an act in the navigation or other technical management of the ship, or from fire, the carrier is liable only for its own fault</b>. The carrier is therefore not answerable for loss caused by the crew's error in navigation or technical management. By contrast, liability remains in full for <b>commercial fault</b> relating to the care and custody of the goods; the line between the two is contested in practice.

### When are the goods deemed lost?

Under Article 1178/5 TTK, if the goods are not delivered within <b>sixty days</b> of the expiry of the period for delivery under Article 1178/4, the person entitled may treat them as <b>lost</b> and claim damages for total loss. The period is longer than the twenty days applicable to carriage by land (Article 874) because of the nature of carriage by sea. The presumption is rebuttable: if the goods are later found, the relevant provisions apply.
