Foreign nationals drawn into a Turkish criminal investigation as witnesses, associates or family members of a suspect often face a second, less obvious risk: their own conduct after the fact — helping a friend disappear, staying quiet about where evidence is stored, or discarding an object connected to someone else's case — can itself become a separate crime. The Turkish Penal Code addresses this under a cluster of offenses against the administration of justice, principally Articles 281, 283 and 284, which respectively punish destroying or altering evidence, actively sheltering an offender, and failing to report information about a fugitive or evidence. Each of these offenses is secondary to an underlying crime that must already exist, yet each carries real exposure of its own, softened by narrow but important exceptions for the original suspect and for close family members.

## The Common Thread: Offenses Against the Administration of Justice

Articles 281, 283 and 284 sit together in the part of the Penal Code dealing with crimes against the judiciary, and they share the same underlying concern: conduct that makes it harder for judicial authorities to investigate a crime, preserve its evidence, or bring the person who committed it to justice. What is protected is not a private individual's interest but the state's ability to administer criminal justice and the orderly functioning of the judicial process. All three provisions are parasitic in nature — they presuppose that some other crime has already been committed by someone else, and if no such underlying crime exists, none of these three offenses can be established. Because the legislature positioned the three articles side by side, they protect three distinct functions of the justice system — the integrity of evidence, the capture of the offender, and the flow of relevant information to the authorities — and it is common in practice for more than one of them to be implicated by the same set of facts.

## Destroying, Concealing or Altering Evidence of a Crime (Article 281)

### What the Offense Covers

Article 281(1) punishes destroying, erasing, concealing, altering or otherwise damaging evidence connected to a crime. Any one of these five modes of conduct is sufficient — the law does not require that the underlying investigation actually fail or be measurably harmed, only that the evidence itself be physically or functionally impaired. "Evidence" is read broadly and can include documents, physical objects, traces, records or biological material, whether in physical or digital form; the nature of the medium does not change the analysis. The offense can be committed by anyone, but if it is committed by a public official in connection with that official's duties, the penalty is increased by half.

### Why a Suspect Cannot Be Punished for Destroying Evidence of Their Own Crime

The most distinctive — and most litigated — feature of Article 281 is its second sentence: a person is not punished under this provision for destroying evidence connected to a crime that they themselves committed or in which they participated. This carve-out reflects the broader principle that a person cannot be compelled to produce evidence against themselves or preserve traces that would expose their own wrongdoing (the privilege against self-incrimination, nemo tenetur se ipsum accusare), and it is treated as a natural extension of the right of defense. The exception is narrow: it protects only evidence relating to the person's own conduct. Destroying evidence that belongs to someone else's offense is not covered and remains fully punishable. As a result, where several people are implicated in the same incident and a piece of evidence is shared — pointing to more than one person's involvement — a suspect who destroys it is shielded only to the extent the evidence concerned their own responsibility, not the portion implicating others.

### Voluntary Surrender of Evidence and Its Relationship to Money Laundering

Article 281(3) rewards a change of heart: a person who hands over previously concealed evidence to the court before judgment is rendered has their sentence reduced by four-fifths. Separately, where the evidence in question consists of money or valuables, conduct that goes beyond mere concealment — for example, financial transactions designed to disguise the origin of those assets — may instead fall under the distinct offense of laundering proceeds of crime. The two offenses require different conduct and different intent, so which one applies in a given case depends on exactly what the person did with the asset.

## Harboring or Assisting an Offender (Article 283)

### Elements of the Offense

Article 283(1) punishes anyone who provides a person known to have committed a crime with the means to evade investigation, arrest, detention or the enforcement of a sentence. What this provision protects is not the integrity of evidence but the investigative and prosecutorial process that brings the offender before the courts. The conduct can take many forms — providing a hiding place, helping the person flee, assisting with a change of identity, or misleading the authorities — and the law deliberately avoids an exhaustive list, focusing instead on the practical effect of the assistance. Completion of the offense does not require that the assistance actually succeed; providing the means is enough on its own. The offense requires intent: the person must know that the individual being helped has committed a crime and must act with the specific purpose of providing assistance toward evading justice. Doctrinally, this offense is distinguished from complicity in the underlying crime, since complicity occurs during or before the commission of the original offense, whereas harboring by definition arises only after that offense has already been completed.

### The Family and Accomplice Exception

The most significant feature of Article 283 is its third paragraph, which exempts from punishment conduct committed by an ascendant, descendant, spouse, sibling, or another accomplice in the underlying crime. This reflects the view that it is unrealistic to criminalize the natural instinct of family solidarity, and it mirrors a similar family exemption found elsewhere in the Code for property offenses. What makes this exception distinctive is that it is not limited to family members — it also extends to "another accomplice," meaning that a person who participated in the original crime can, in turn, shelter a fellow participant without incurring liability under this article. That broader scope does not carry over to the separate failure-to-report offense discussed below, which is an important distinction between the two provisions.

## Failing to Report a Fugitive or the Location of Evidence (Article 284)

Article 284 punishes not active assistance but the breach of a duty to disclose. Its first paragraph targets a person who knows the whereabouts of someone subject to an arrest warrant or a convicted person and fails to notify the authorities; its second paragraph applies the same penalty to a person who knows where evidence connected to a crime is being kept by others and stays silent. What matters is that the person actually possesses the information and consciously withholds it — mere lack of knowledge is not punishable. Unlike Article 283, no active assistance of any kind is required; simply failing to share known information is enough to complete the offense. Commission by a public official in connection with official duties is an aggravating factor, while — in contrast to Article 283 — commission by an ascendant, descendant, spouse or sibling results in no punishment, but this narrower family exemption does not extend to accomplices in the underlying crime.

## Attempt, Concurrence and Procedure

All three offenses are, in principle, susceptible to attempt under the general rules of the Penal Code, although the structure of Article 281 (built around alternative acts) and of Article 284 (an offense of omission) tends to limit how often attempt actually arises in practice. General rules on complicity apply, subject to the accomplice exception carved out in Article 283(3). Where the same person both destroys evidence and shelters the offender through genuinely separate acts, the ordinary rules on concurrence of offenses apply; where a person who participated in the underlying crime later destroys evidence of their own involvement, no separate liability arises because of the specific exemption in Article 281(1). These offenses are prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative rather than upon a private complaint, and jurisdiction lies with the criminal court of first instance appropriate to the maximum sentence prescribed for the offense charged.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If I destroy evidence connected only to my own alleged crime, can I be prosecuted under Article 281?**
No, as a general rule. Article 281 expressly exempts a person from punishment for destroying, concealing or altering evidence relating to a crime they themselves committed or participated in. The exemption is limited to that person's own conduct — it does not extend to evidence implicating someone else, even if the evidence is shared.

**Can a family member who helps a relative evade arrest be prosecuted for harboring an offender?**
Generally no. Article 283(3) exempts an ascendant, descendant, spouse, sibling, or another accomplice in the underlying crime from punishment for this type of assistance. This is a broader exemption than the one available under the separate failure-to-report offense in Article 284.

**Is simply staying silent about where a fugitive or evidence is located a crime in Turkey?**
Yes, if the person actually knows the information and fails to report it to the authorities. Article 284 punishes this omission directly; unlike Article 283, no active assistance is required — the failure to disclose known information is itself sufficient.

**Do these offenses require that the underlying crime be proven or finally adjudicated?**
The offenses require that an underlying crime have actually been committed, since all three provisions are dependent on that fact. If it turns out no underlying crime occurred, none of Articles 281, 283 or 284 can be established, regardless of the accused's conduct.
