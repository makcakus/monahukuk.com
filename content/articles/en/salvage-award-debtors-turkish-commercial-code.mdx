Having covered how the salvage award is calculated under Art. 1305, this article addresses who actually has to pay it. Art. 1306 closes out the salvage award sub-section with two rules: who the debtors are, and how liability among them is structured.

## The Debtor Is the Owner at Completion, Not the Operator

Under Art. 1306(1), the debtors of the salvage award are whoever owned the salved vessel and other property at the moment the salvage operation was *completed*. This differs from the multi-point-in-time approach used for general average contribution (covered in the Art. 1274 article), where the ship's contribution attaches to the operator at the moment of the general average act while cargo and freight attach to the owner/claimant at discharge. Salvage does not need that split: unlike a general average act, which is one episode within an ongoing voyage, a salvage operation is a self-contained event with a clear start and end, so a single completion-time snapshot works cleanly for both ship and cargo.

Turkish Court of Cassation case law applying Art. 1306(1) is unambiguous on one point in particular: the debtor is the *owner*, not the *operator*. In one case, a vessel had both a registered owner and a separate operating company as defendants; the Court held that only the registered owner was liable for the salvage award under Art. 1306, and the operator — not being the owner — bore no liability for it, regardless of its role in running the ship. This owner-versus-operator distinction has been applied consistently and without challenge across several rulings.

## Proportional Sharing, No Joint-and-Several Liability

Art. 1306(2) addresses what happens when a salvage operation involves more than one asset (ship, bunkers, cargo) and thus more than one owner: the award is divided among the owners in proportion to the value each of their property retained after salvage — directly tying back to the "post-salvage value" criterion in Art. 1305(1)(a). Critically, the second sentence states there is no joint-and-several liability among these debtors. This mirrors the non-solidary liability model Turkish maritime law uses for collision damage among co-liable shipowners: each owner answers only for its own proportional share, and the salvor cannot recover the full award from a single, more solvent owner and leave that owner to seek reimbursement from the rest.

## Practical Takeaway

For salvors, this means collection is not a single claim — it requires calculating and pursuing each owner's share separately, potentially through separate proceedings, since no owner can be made to answer for another's portion. For cargo and ship owners, exposure is capped at their own share of the salved value from day one.
