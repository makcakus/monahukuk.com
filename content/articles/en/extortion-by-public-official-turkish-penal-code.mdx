Foreign businesses operating in Turkey occasionally encounter a public official who leans on the authority of their post to squeeze out a benefit that was never legally due — a permit officer who hints that paperwork will stall indefinitely unless "helped along," or an inspector who trades on the trust of the uniform to talk a citizen into handing over money under false pretenses. Turkish criminal law treats this as a distinct offense, separate from bribery, because the victim in these cases has not struck a free bargain: their will has been bent or misled by someone wielding public power. Article 250 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) criminalizes this conduct as extortion by a public official, and a closely related provision, Article 251, extends liability to the supervisor who lets it happen. Understanding both is essential for any company navigating licensing, customs, tax, or regulatory contacts with Turkish public officials, and for anyone — Turkish or foreign — who suspects they have been pressured into paying a public official something they did not owe.

## What the Offense Protects and Who Can Commit It

Article 250 sits among the "offenses against the administration" in the Turkish Penal Code, and what it protects is not simply the personal honesty of the individual official but the public's ability to deal with state administration without fear of being manipulated by it. Because of this, the offense can only be committed by someone holding the status of a public official — it is what Turkish criminal doctrine calls a "proper" offense (özgü suç), meaning a private person cannot commit it as a principal. A private individual who assists or instigates such conduct can still be held criminally responsible, but only as an accessory or instigator under the general participation rules, never as the direct perpetrator. This distinguishes extortion under Article 250 from ordinary fraud or threats committed between private parties: what makes the official's conduct unlawful here is not personal strength or deception alone, but the fact that they are trading on the authority the state itself gave them.

The defining feature that separates this offense from other public-office crimes is that the victim's consent is defective. The official does not receive a benefit because the other person freely chose to offer one — the other person's will has been overridden, either by pressure or by deceit, so that what looks superficially like a voluntary payment is in fact something closer to a coerced or induced surrender.

## Three Forms of the Offense, Ranked by Severity

Article 250 does not punish a single fixed behavior. It sets out three distinct variants, each carrying its own penalty range, reflecting how strongly the official's conduct overrides the victim's free will.

**Compulsion (Article 250/1).** This is the most serious form. The official abuses the influence their post carries to compel another person to provide, or promise to provide, a benefit to the official or to a third party. Turkish law defines compulsion broadly for this purpose: it is enough that the victim, faced with the official's unfair conduct, felt obliged to hand over a benefit out of fear that a legitimate matter of theirs would otherwise not be handled properly, or not handled in time. No explicit threat is required — an atmosphere of indirect pressure created by the official's position is sufficient. The offense is also complete once a mere promise of a future benefit has been extracted; actual delivery of the benefit is not required. This variant carries a prison sentence of five to ten years.

**Deceptive persuasion (Article 250/2).** Here the mechanism is trickery rather than pressure. The official abuses the trust their office commands and, through some deceptive conduct, talks the victim into providing or promising a benefit. The victim is not coerced but misled into believing the payment is legitimate or owed. Because manipulating someone's will through deceit is treated as less grave than outright compulsion, the penalty is correspondingly lower: three to five years' imprisonment.

**Exploiting an existing mistake (Article 250/3).** This is a lesser variant of the deceptive-persuasion offense, applying where the official does not actively deceive the victim but simply takes advantage of a mistake the victim had already fallen into on their own. Because the official's role here is passive rather than actively manipulative, the law treats it as the least serious of the three forms, punishable by one to three years' imprisonment.

Article 250 also allows the court, in any of the three variants, to reduce the sentence by up to half, taking into account the value of the benefit extorted and the victim's financial circumstances (Article 250/4). This gives the court a tool to calibrate the punishment to the real-world severity of the harm, rather than applying a flat penalty regardless of how much money changed hands or how significant that sum was for the person who paid it.

## Extortion Versus Bribery: Where the Line Falls

The critical dividing line between extortion under Article 250 and bribery under Article 252 is the position of the person who hands over the benefit. Bribery is, by definition, a two-sided and voluntary transaction: both the official who takes the bribe and the private party who offers it are treated as offenders, because both act out of their own free will in striking the deal. Extortion is different precisely because the payer's will has been compromised — through compulsion, deceit, or exploitation of a mistake — so the person who ends up handing over money is legally the victim, not a co-offender, and is not separately prosecuted for having paid. Getting this classification right matters in practice: investigators must examine whether the person genuinely negotiated and consented to the exchange, rather than simply concluding that a payment reached a public official and treating that fact alone as decisive. Misclassifying a victim of extortion as a bribe-giver can expose an innocent person to investigation for a crime they did not commit.

## Intent, Attempt, and Overlap With Other Offenses

Extortion under Article 250 requires intent: the official must know and want that they are compelling or deceiving the victim by abusing the influence or trust their office provides. Because the conduct typically unfolds in stages, an attempt is conceptually possible — for instance, where the compulsion or deceptive persuasion is set in motion but fails to succeed for reasons outside the official's control. In practice, however, the room for attempt is narrow, because the law treats the offense as complete the moment the victim even verbally agrees to the official's demand, without waiting for the benefit to actually be delivered. Where a private individual participates alongside the official, that person is held liable only as an instigator or accessory under the Penal Code's general participation rules, reflecting the fact that only a public official can be the direct perpetrator of this proper offense. Where the same conduct could also be described as ordinary misconduct in office, Article 250 takes priority as the more specific provision.

## The Supervisor's Liability for Failing to Stop It

A separate provision, Article 251, does not punish the official who personally commits embezzlement or extortion — it addresses the liability of the superior who was responsible for supervising that official. The article distinguishes two degrees of fault. If the supervising official knowingly turned a blind eye to a subordinate's embezzlement or extortion, that supervisor is treated as a joint perpetrator of the underlying offense and faces the same penalty range that applies to the embezzlement or extortion actually committed — Article 251 sets no separate penalty for this first scenario, because the supervisor is, in effect, folded into the primary offense. Where the supervisor instead simply neglected their duty of oversight, without actually knowing what the subordinate was doing, and that neglect made the embezzlement or extortion possible, this is treated as an independent and materially lighter offense, carrying a penalty of three months to three years' imprisonment. That second category is fundamentally a negligence-based form of liability: the supervisor is not complicit in the underlying crime but is punished for the carelessness that allowed it to happen. Because the two scenarios lead to very different charges and penalty ranges, establishing whether the supervisor's state of mind was actual knowledge or mere negligence is one of the most consequential factual questions in any investigation involving Article 251.

## Prosecution and Procedure

Because both extortion and dereliction of supervisory duty are committed by a public official in the course of their duties, prosecutors pursue these cases on their own initiative — a private complaint is not required to trigger an investigation, although the special procedural safeguards that apply to investigating public officials (such as permission or preliminary inquiry requirements under the relevant administrative legislation) remain applicable. The outcome of a prosecution turns heavily on correctly identifying which of the three variants under Article 250 actually occurred — compulsion, deceptive persuasion, or exploitation of a mistake — since that classification fixes both the applicable penalty range and its lower and upper limits. Because the range between the lightest and heaviest variants is so wide, the evidentiary record frequently becomes the battleground on which the defense and the prosecution argue for a different classification of the same underlying facts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How is extortion by a public official different from bribery in Turkish law?**
In bribery, both the official and the person offering the benefit act voluntarily and are both potentially liable. In extortion under Article 250, the person who hands over the benefit does so because their will was overridden by compulsion, deceit, or an exploited mistake — they are the victim, not a co-offender, and are not prosecuted for paying.

**Does the official have to actually receive the money for the offense to be complete?**
No. Under Article 250, the offense is complete once the official has obtained even a promise of a future benefit, or once the victim has agreed to provide one. Actual delivery of money or property is not required for the crime to be considered committed.

**Can a private citizen be charged as the principal offender under Article 250?**
No. Only a public official can be the direct perpetrator, since this is a status-based offense. A private individual who helps or encourages the official can still face criminal liability, but only as an instigator or accessory under the general participation rules.

**When is a supervisor criminally liable for a subordinate's extortion or embezzlement?**
Under Article 251, if the supervisor knowingly overlooked the subordinate's conduct, they are treated as a joint perpetrator of the underlying offense. If they merely failed to exercise proper oversight without actual knowledge, they instead face the lighter, independent offense of dereliction of supervisory duty, punishable by three months to three years' imprisonment.
