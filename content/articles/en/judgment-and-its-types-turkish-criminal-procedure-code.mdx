For a foreign national facing criminal proceedings in Turkey, the moment a court announces its decision at the end of trial can be confusing: Turkish law does not simply produce a "guilty" or "not guilty" verdict, but recognizes six distinct categories of judgment under Article 223 of the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (CPC, "Ceza Muhakemesi Kanunu"). Which category applies determines whether the outcome appears on a criminal record, whether compensation can be claimed, and even which appellate remedy is available. A second mechanism, the deferral of the announcement of the verdict ("Hükmün Açıklanmasının Geri Bırakılması," HAGB, regulated in CPC Article 231), has long allowed first-time, lower-level offenders to avoid a formal conviction altogether. A 2025 Constitutional Court ruling annulled its operative provisions, but Parliament re-enacted them before the annulment took effect, and the mechanism now applies in a rewritten form. This article explains both frameworks for defendants, families and counsel navigating a Turkish criminal case.

## The Six Types of Judgment Recognized by Turkish Law

Article 223 treats the word "judgment" as an umbrella term covering six possible outcomes at the close of trial, rather than a single decision. Acquittal and conviction sit at opposite poles: a court acquits when it finds that the alleged conduct simply does not amount to a crime, that the defendant did not commit it, that the required intent or negligence is absent, that a lawful justification existed (such as self-defense), or that the prosecution has failed to establish the defendant's guilt. Conviction, conversely, follows once the court is satisfied the defendant committed the offense charged. Between these two lies a decision that Turkish practice calls "no penalty imposed" — the court accepts that a crime was committed and that the defendant committed it, but withholds punishment because the defendant lacked criminal culpability (due to age, mental incapacity, duress, an excessive but understandable response in self-defense, or a legally recognized mistake) or because a specific statutory ground for non-punishment applies (such as effective remorse, a personal immunity from punishment, mutual insult, or conduct of minimal wrongfulness). Two further, more procedural outcomes complete the list: dismissal of the case, which applies the double-jeopardy principle where the same defendant has already been tried or convicted for the same act, and discontinuation of the case, which applies when a ground for termination under the Turkish Penal Code arises — such as the statute of limitations expiring, the defendant's death, or a general amnesty — or when a required precondition for prosecution turns out to be unfulfillable.

## Acquittal Versus "No Penalty Imposed": A Practically Important Distinction

Defendants and their families frequently conflate acquittal with the "no penalty imposed" decision, yet the two carry materially different consequences. An acquittal means the court has concluded that there is no punishable wrong at all — either no crime occurred, the defendant was not the one who committed it, there was no culpable intent or negligence, or a justification excused the conduct entirely. A "no penalty imposed" decision, by contrast, starts from the opposite premise: the court accepts that a crime occurred and that the defendant is the one who committed it, but declines to punish because of the defendant's personal circumstances at the time (youth, mental incapacity, and similar factors) or because the legislature has carved out a specific non-punishment ground. This distinction matters in practice because it affects whether a security measure (such as confiscation) can still be ordered, and whether the defendant or a victim retains a basis for a compensation claim arising from the same facts.

## Conviction, Dismissal and Discontinuation

Once a court is satisfied the defendant committed the charged offense, it issues a conviction, which may be accompanied — or in some cases replaced — by a security measure rather than, or alongside, a specific term of imprisonment or fine. Dismissal of the case is a procedural safeguard against double jeopardy: if the same person has already been convicted, or is already facing another prosecution, for the same act, the second case is dismissed without any examination of its merits. Discontinuation applies when circumstances make continuing the prosecution legally pointless — most commonly the running of the statute of limitations, the death of the defendant, or a general amnesty — though the law carves out an exception where prosecution depends on a condition that has not yet been satisfied: in that scenario the court issues a suspension order, subject to objection, and waits for the condition to be met rather than discontinuing the case outright. The law is also explicit that where an acquittal could be granted immediately and outright, courts should not fall back on a suspension, discontinuation or "no penalty imposed" decision instead — the most decisive and fastest outcome favorable to the defendant is meant to take precedence.

## Deferral of the Announcement of the Verdict (HAGB) and Its Uncertain Future

HAGB is a long-standing and heavily used feature of Turkish criminal practice that effectively suspends a conviction for a defined probationary period rather than letting it take immediate legal effect. Under CPC Article 231, it has traditionally applied where the sentence handed down is a prison term or judicial fine at or below a two-year threshold, and required three cumulative conditions: the defendant must have no prior intentional-offense conviction, the court must be satisfied — based on the defendant's character and conduct at trial — that they are unlikely to reoffend, and any harm caused to a victim or the public must have been fully compensated. Where HAGB is granted, the conviction that has been formally reached produces no legal consequences for the defendant (aside from confiscation-related provisions) — it does not appear on a criminal record, and if the defendant completes a five-year supervision period without committing another intentional offense, the case is ultimately discontinued. This makes HAGB, in effect, a "second chance" mechanism, applied most often to first-time offenders facing relatively minor charges.

A significant recent development affects this framework directly. On 10 July 2025, the Turkish Constitutional Court (decision no. E. 2024/98, K. 2025/149, published in the Official Gazette on 31 December 2025) annulled the substantive core of Article 231's HAGB provisions as unconstitutional, postponing the effect of its ruling to 30 September 2026 so as to avoid a legal vacuum. **Parliament acted before that date:** Article 15 of Law No. 7589 of 16 July 2026 comprehensively re-enacted paragraphs (5) to (14) of Article 231. HAGB therefore did not lapse; it continues to apply in its rewritten form. The most important practical consequence of the new text is that a HAGB order is now challenged by **first-instance appeal (“istinaf”), not by objection** (Article 231(12)).

## Contents and Reasoning of the Judgment

Turkish law is precise about what a judgment must contain and how it is expressed (CPC Article 232). Every judgment is issued "in the name of the Turkish Nation" and must open by identifying the court, the presiding judge and public prosecutor, the defendant's full identity, and the time and place of the alleged offense, along with the defendant's custody status. If the full reasoning is not recorded in the trial minutes at the hearing itself, it must be added to the case file within fifteen days of the decision being announced. Critically, the operative part of the judgment must state — without any room for doubt — which of the Article 223 outcomes has been reached, which statutory provisions were applied, the penalty imposed, whether an appeal or compensation claim is available, and if so, the applicable deadline and the competent authority to which it should be directed. This clarity requirement exists precisely so that a defendant can make informed, timely use of appellate rights.

## Appellate Review and the Role of Mediation

Every category of judgment is subject to some form of appellate review — objection, first-instance appeal ("istinaf"), or cassation ("temyiz") — though which remedy applies depends on the type of decision. For certain offenses, Turkish law requires that mediation be attempted before any judgment on the merits is reached; where mediation succeeds, the outcome is typically a decision that no prosecution is warranted or that the case is discontinued, and no judgment on the substance of the charge is ever issued. A frequent point of confusion in practice concerns HAGB decisions specifically: under Article 231(12), as amended by Law No. 7499 of 2 March 2024 and Law No. 7589 of 16 July 2026, a HAGB order is challenged by first-instance appeal (“istinaf”), subject to Article 272(3); before those amendments the remedy was objection. Misidentifying the correct remedy — or relying on the pre-2024 position — can cost a defendant valuable time and, in some cases, the remedy altogether. This is why, at the moment a judgment is pronounced, the defendant must be correctly informed of the applicable remedy, the competent authority, and the deadline for using it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the practical difference between an acquittal and a "no penalty imposed" decision?**
An acquittal means the court found no punishable conduct occurred at all. A "no penalty imposed" decision means the court found that a crime was committed by the defendant, but withheld punishment due to a recognized excuse (such as age or mental incapacity) or a specific non-punishment ground. The distinction can affect whether security measures or compensation claims remain available.

**Is HAGB still available for defendants facing trial in Turkey right now?**
HAGB remains part of Turkish law. The Constitutional Court annulled its core provisions with effect from 30 September 2026, but Law No. 7589 of 16 July 2026 re-enacted them beforehand, so the mechanism continues to operate under a rewritten Article 231 — most notably with appeal, rather than objection, as the applicable remedy.

**Can a HAGB decision be appealed to the Turkish Court of Cassation?**
No. Since the amendments made by Law No. 7499 of 2 March 2024 and Law No. 7589 of 16 July 2026, a HAGB order is challenged by first-instance appeal (“istinaf”) under Article 231(12), subject to Article 272(3), rather than by cassation. The objection procedure that applied before those amendments no longer governs.

**Does dismissal of a case mean the same as an acquittal?**
No. Dismissal applies specifically where the same person has already been convicted or prosecuted for the same act — it is a procedural bar against double jeopardy, decided without the court examining the merits of the second case at all, unlike an acquittal, which follows a substantive review of the evidence.
