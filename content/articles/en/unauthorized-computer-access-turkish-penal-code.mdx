Foreign nationals, expatriates working in Turkey's tech sector, and international companies with Turkish operations are sometimes surprised at how broadly Turkish criminal law defines a computer-related offense. Under the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), simply entering a computer system without authorization — even without causing any damage — is already a crime under Article 243. A separate and considerably more serious offense, Article 244, applies once the conduct moves from mere access to disrupting the system or tampering with the data inside it. Because these two provisions sit next to each other and are frequently charged together or confused in practice, understanding where one ends and the other begins is essential for anyone facing an investigation, advising a company on cybersecurity exposure, or evaluating a criminal complaint filed against an employee, contractor, or third party in Turkey.

## What Article 243 Actually Punishes

Article 243 of the TCK targets the act of entering all or part of a computer system without legal authorization, or remaining inside a system after access has become unauthorized. The provision covers two distinct scenarios: unauthorized entry itself, and the continued, unauthorized presence in a system that a person may have initially accessed lawfully — for example, an employee whose access rights were revoked but who keeps using an old login, or someone whose permission was limited to a specific folder or module but who continues browsing beyond that scope. Turkish legal commentary generally treats this second scenario as an ongoing offense, meaning the violation is treated as continuing for as long as the person remains logged in or connected, which matters for calculating when the statute of limitations begins to run.

Importantly, the base offense under Article 243 does not require that any harm actually occur. Unauthorized entry into even a small part of a system — a single directory, file, or module — is enough to complete the offense; Turkish criminal theory treats it as an abstract-danger crime, meaning the law protects the integrity and confidentiality of the system itself, not any particular outcome. The penalty for a first-tier violation is imprisonment of up to one year or a judicial fine, which places it toward the lower end of the Penal Code's offense scale, but a criminal conviction and record still follow.

The provision carries an internal sliding scale, however. Where the system that is entered is one that is used in exchange for payment — for example, a paid subscription platform or a pay-per-use service — the penalty may be reduced by up to half, reflecting a somewhat different balance of interests when a fee-based service is involved. Conversely, where the unauthorized entry results in the destruction or alteration of data within the system, the applicable sentence rises to a range of six months to two years' imprisonment. Turkish doctrine generally treats this aggravated outcome as a "result-based aggravation" under general principles of the Penal Code, meaning the person's negligence — rather than deliberate intent to destroy data — is enough to trigger the higher penalty; where the person deliberately intended to damage or destroy the data from the outset, the conduct is instead treated as the more specific and more serious offense under Article 244.

A further paragraph, added by Turkey's 2016 Data Protection Law, addresses a related but structurally distinct act: unlawfully monitoring, by technical means and without ever entering the system itself, data transfers that occur within a system or between systems. This form of covert interception of electronic communications carries a heavier penalty of one to three years' imprisonment, precisely because — unlike ordinary unauthorized access — it does not require entering the system at all.

## Article 244: When Access Becomes Disruption

Article 244 addresses conduct that goes beyond mere entry and instead interferes with how a system functions or with the data it holds. A person who blocks or disrupts the operation of a computer system faces imprisonment of one to five years — a substantially higher range than simple unauthorized access. This is the provision typically applied to denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, since the point of such an attack is not to get inside the system but to interrupt its normal operation from the outside.

A second category under Article 244 covers corrupting, destroying, altering, or rendering data inaccessible, planting data into a system, or transmitting existing data elsewhere without authorization, punishable by six months to three years' imprisonment. Ransomware attacks — where data is encrypted and held inaccessible until payment is made — fall squarely within this category. Unlike Article 243, this offense does not require proof that the person entered the system without authorization at all; a person who had legitimate access and then interferes with the system's data or functioning can still be charged under Article 244.

Where either form of this conduct targets a system belonging to a bank, credit institution, or public authority, the penalty is increased by half, reflecting the additional public interest in the continuity of financial services and government operations. A separate and more severe consequence applies where the offender, through any of this conduct, obtains an unjust benefit for themselves or another person — provided the conduct does not already constitute a different, separately punishable offense — carrying imprisonment of two to six years plus a substantial judicial fine of up to five thousand day-fines. This provision is aimed at monetized attacks, such as ransom demands tied to encrypting a victim's data, but it only applies to the extent the conduct is not already captured by fraud or computer-related fraud provisions; where the same act also satisfies the elements of another crime, Turkey's rules on concurrence of offenses determine which charge, or combination of charges, ultimately applies.

## Intent, Attempt, and Procedural Notes

Both offenses can be committed with general intent — it is enough that the person acted knowing their access or interference was unauthorized, without needing to show any further motive, although the "unjust benefit" variant under Article 244 does require a specific intent to profit. An attempt is possible where an effort to access a system is interrupted for reasons beyond the person's control, though the sudden, one-off nature of disrupting a system's operation or its data under Article 244 can narrow the practical scope for an attempted version of that offense. Where the offense takes the form of unlawfully remaining in a system, its continuing nature generally means that, once access is established, every subsequent moment is treated as part of a single completed offense rather than a fresh attempt — a distinction that becomes important when courts determine when the limitation period starts to run.

General rules on complicity apply where more than one person cooperates in gaining unauthorized access or interfering with a system, and Turkey's rules on repeated offenses against the same target over time may come into play depending on the specific facts. Neither Article 243 nor Article 244 requires a formal complaint from the victim to trigger prosecution — Turkish public prosecutors can investigate and pursue these offenses on their own initiative once they become aware of them. Given the sentencing ranges involved, jurisdiction generally lies with the Criminal Court of First Instance (Asliye Ceza Mahkemesi).

## How Turkish Courts Have Approached These Offenses

Turkish appellate practice illustrates several recurring themes in how these provisions are applied. Courts have required prosecutors to actually establish, rather than assume, that a defendant remained connected to a system for a meaningful period — mere momentary access, without evidence of continued unauthorized presence, may not be enough to sustain a conviction under the "remaining in the system" variant of Article 243. Courts have also drawn a sharp line between charges built on unauthorized computer access or data alteration and charges built on separate offenses such as forgery of official documents, insisting that an indictment specify which conduct it actually accuses the defendant of rather than allowing a court to recharacterize the case into an unrelated offense. In cases involving alleged unjust enrichment through system interference, Turkish courts have scrutinized whether a financial benefit was actually obtained — where no payment or benefit in fact flowed to the defendant, the aggravated "unjust benefit" branch of Article 244 has been found not to apply, even where the underlying interference itself was established. Jurisdictional and limitation-period questions also feature prominently, given how the applicable sentencing range determines both the trial court and appellate chamber, as well as how long the state has to prosecute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it a crime under Turkish law to access a computer system without permission, even if nothing was damaged?**
Yes. Article 243 does not require any resulting harm — unauthorized entry into all or part of a system, or remaining in it without authorization, is itself sufficient to constitute the offense, punishable by up to one year's imprisonment or a judicial fine.

**What is the difference between Article 243 and Article 244?**
Article 243 punishes the act of gaining or maintaining unauthorized access to a system, regardless of what the person does once inside. Article 244 punishes disrupting how a system operates or tampering with its data — such as through a DDoS attack or ransomware — and applies whether or not the person's initial access was authorized.

**Does a DDoS attack or ransomware incident in Turkey fall under these provisions?**
Generally yes. A denial-of-service attack that disrupts a system's operation, and a ransomware attack that encrypts or otherwise renders data inaccessible, are both typically prosecuted under Article 244 rather than Article 243, since neither necessarily involves the attacker entering the target system.

**Do these offenses require the victim to file a formal criminal complaint?**
No. Because the Penal Code does not make either offense conditional on a victim complaint, Turkish prosecutors can open and pursue an investigation on their own initiative once they learn of the conduct.
