TTK Art. 1142-1151 govern the process from contract formation to cargo actually being loaded aboard — a cluster with real practical bite in cargo damage litigation.

## Costs and Substitution

Art. 1142 requires the captain to berth where the contract specifies, or in the designated waiting area if only a port/region was agreed. Art. 1143 splits costs by default: getting cargo to the ship is the shipper's cost, loading it aboard is the carrier's — though contract, port regulations, or local custom can override this. Art. 1144 gives the shipper a limited right to substitute cargo bound for the same destination, unless this burdens the carrier or the original cargo was specifically identified in the contract.

## Declaring Cargo Honestly

Art. 1145 requires the shipper and loader to give the carrier a full and accurate account of the cargo — each is directly liable to the carrier for inaccuracy, and liable to other injured parties only if at fault. Art. 1146-1147 extend this to prohibited or secretly loaded cargo: the shipper cannot escape liability just because the captain approved loading, or by pleading the goods were seized, and secretly loaded cargo the captain chooses to keep aboard triggers liability for the *highest* obtainable freight, not the market rate.

## Dangerous Cargo: A Rare Strict-Liability Rule

Art. 1148 breaks from the fault-based pattern: if genuinely dangerous cargo is brought aboard without the captain's knowledge of its dangerous nature, the shipper is liable regardless of fault — an intentional shift addressing the information asymmetry, since only the shipper typically knows what the cargo really is. Conversely, if the captain knowingly approved loading it, the carrier bears no liability if it later causes harm. Art. 1149 attributes the carrier's or its agent's knowledge to the captain, closing an obvious loophole.

## Transshipment and Deck Cargo

Art. 1150 bars moving cargo to another ship without the shipper's consent, with liability nearly impossible to escape. Art. 1151 generally bars deck carriage unless agreed, customary, or legally required — and if agreed, the bill of lading must note it, or the carrier cannot rely on the agreement against a good-faith holder of the bill, including the consignee. Unauthorized deck stowage against an explicit hold-carriage agreement is treated as the carrier's own reckless act, stripping away limited-liability protection.

## Practical Takeaway

For shippers and carriers, the deck-cargo documentation rule is worth special attention: Turkish courts have confirmed that whether deck stowage of a given cargo type (e.g., Ro-Ro trailers) qualifies as "commercial custom" under Art. 1151 is fact-specific and heavily litigated.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who bears the loading costs?

Under TTK Article 1143/1 <b>unless otherwise provided by the contract, by the regulations of the loading port or, failing those, by local custom, the cost of carrying the goods to the ship falls on the charterer and the cost of loading on the carrier.</b> The provision is of a default character; in practice this allocation is frequently altered by trade terms such as FIO and FIOS. The symmetrical rule for discharge is in Article 1167.

### May the carrier carry cargo on deck?

As a rule, no. Under TTK Article 1151/1 <b>the carrier may not carry the goods on deck or hang them on the rail.</b> Under Article 1151/2 there are three exceptions: <b>an agreement with the shipper</b>, <b>conformity with commercial custom</b>, or <b>a requirement imposed by legislation</b>. Where deck carriage takes place, this must be stated in the sea waybill; otherwise the exception to the limitation of liability in Article 1244/1(a) cannot be invoked.

### What follows if dangerous goods are not declared?

Strict liability arises. Under TTK Article 1148/1 <b>if goods regarded as dangerous under the legislation on the protection of life and property at sea are brought on board without the master's knowledge of them or of their dangerous kind or nature, the charterer or the shipper is liable under Article 1145 even if no fault is attributable to them.</b> In addition, the master may at any time and in any place put such goods off the ship and, if necessary, destroy them.
