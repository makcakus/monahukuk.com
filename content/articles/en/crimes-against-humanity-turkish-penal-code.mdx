Turkish criminal law places crimes against humanity in the same part of the Penal Code as genocide, immediately after it, and the two offenses are frequently confused with one another even though they protect different interests and require different mental states. Article 77 of the Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) criminalizes a defined set of violent acts — killing, causing bodily harm, torture, unlawful deprivation of liberty, forced medical experimentation, sexual assault, forced pregnancy, and forced prostitution, among others — when they are carried out as part of an organized plan and in a systematic manner against a segment of the population, motivated by political, philosophical, racial, or religious grounds. Because the underlying acts are already punishable as ordinary offenses under other provisions of the Code, what elevates them into an independent, aggravated offense is precisely this organizational and motivational context. For international counsel advising on Turkish criminal exposure, extradition matters, or historical accountability litigation, understanding exactly where the line between an ordinary offense, crimes against humanity, and genocide falls is essential, since the classification affects everything from the applicable penalty to whether the statute of limitations runs at all.

## The Protected Legal Interest

Article 77 protects more than the individual victims of the acts it lists. At one level, the underlying conduct — killing, injury, torture, sexual violation, unlawful confinement — infringes classic individual interests such as life, physical integrity, and personal liberty. At a second, more fundamental level, the offense exists to protect human dignity and the collective conscience of humanity as such. It is the fact that the conduct is directed, according to a plan, in a systematic way, against a segment of a population that transforms an accumulation of individual crimes into an offense of concern to the international community as a whole. In this sense, the provision also safeguards international peace and legal order, echoing the body of international criminal law that has developed since the Nuremberg trials and that Turkish law has absorbed into its domestic criminal code.

## The Material Elements of the Offense

### Perpetrator

Article 77 does not require the perpetrator to hold any particular status, position, or office. Anyone can, in principle, commit the offense. In practice, however, because the acts must be carried out pursuant to a plan and in a systematic fashion, prosecutions typically involve individuals acting within a state apparatus or another organized structure capable of designing and executing such a plan. An official position is nevertheless not a legal requirement: any person who knowingly participates in the plan is held responsible in proportion to their own contribution, regardless of rank or title.

### Victim

The victim under Article 77 is defined as "a segment of the population." This is the first and most important point of divergence from genocide. Genocide under Article 76 protects a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group defined by a fixed, pre-existing identity. Crimes against humanity, by contrast, target a segment of the population identified by the perpetrator's political, philosophical, racial, or religious motive for attacking it — the targeted group is defined by why the perpetrator singled it out, not by an objectively fixed group membership. Individual victims are members of that targeted segment, but the offense is conceived as being directed against the collectivity as a whole, not merely against the sum of the individuals harmed.

### Conduct and Result

Article 77 is a crime of alternative acts: any one of the listed conducts is sufficient to satisfy this element, provided the contextual requirements below are also met. The acts listed in the first paragraph include intentional killing; intentional injury; torture, cruel treatment or enslavement; unlawful deprivation of personal liberty; subjecting a person to scientific experimentation; sexual assault or the sexual abuse of children; forced pregnancy; and forcing a person into prostitution. None of these acts, taken alone, amounts to a crime against humanity — each already exists as an independent offense elsewhere in the Penal Code. What converts an ordinary act of this kind into the aggravated offense of Article 77 is the simultaneous presence of two contextual conditions: the act must be carried out pursuant to a plan, and it must be carried out systematically against a segment of the population. The "plan" element denotes a pre-arranged organizational scheme, while "systematic" conduct is conduct that is regular and repeated rather than isolated or coincidental. Where this contextual element is absent, the underlying act is punished only under the ordinary provision that would otherwise apply to it.

## The Mental Element and the Discriminatory Motive

Crimes against humanity can only be committed intentionally; the offense has no negligent counterpart. The perpetrator must knowingly and willingly carry out one of the listed acts. Beyond ordinary intent, however, Article 77 requires a further, specific mental element: the act must be committed on political, philosophical, racial, or religious grounds. This discriminatory motive is a constitutive element of the offense, not an aggravating circumstance layered on top of it — without proof that the perpetrator targeted the population segment for one of these reasons, the offense is not made out.

This is where the second key distinction from genocide becomes visible. Genocide requires a special intent (dolus specialis) to destroy the protected group, in whole or in part, as such. Crimes against humanity require no such destructive intent. It is enough that the perpetrator knowingly takes part in the planned and systematic attack while acting on one of the specified discriminatory grounds; there is no additional requirement that the perpetrator intend to annihilate the targeted group.

## The Related Offense of Organizing for These Crimes (Article 78)

Recognizing that crimes against humanity and genocide are typically organizational in nature, the legislature separately criminalized, in Article 78 of the Penal Code, the act of founding, leading, or joining an organization established for the purpose of committing genocide or crimes against humanity. This is an independent offense: it does not require that the organization has actually carried out any underlying crime, and mere founding, leadership, or membership is punishable in itself. Because of its distinct elements and sanctions, Article 78 merits separate treatment and is not addressed further here.

## Attempt, Participation, and Concurrence of Offenses

Because most of the alternative acts listed in Article 77 are acts of commission that can be broken down into discrete stages, the offense is capable of being attempted: where execution has begun but the result does not occur for reasons beyond the perpetrator's control, the general attempt provisions of the Penal Code apply. Given that the offense is typically committed by multiple persons operating within an organized structure, the ordinary rules on participation apply with particular force, allocating responsibility among those who devise the plan, those who give orders, and those who physically carry out the acts, whether as joint principals, instigators, or accessories. On concurrence of offenses, the legislature adopted a specific rule for killing and injury: where either of these acts is committed against multiple victims, the ordinary rule against treating repeated acts as a single continuing offense is displaced, and a separate offense is deemed to arise for each individual victim, with penalties determined victim by victim rather than as a single aggregated crime.

## Sanctions and Procedural Features

Article 77 sets out a two-tier penalty structure. Intentional killing committed within the scope of the offense carries aggravated life imprisonment, while the other listed acts carry imprisonment of not less than eight years. As noted above, killing and injury committed against multiple victims are punished separately for each victim rather than as a single offense. Legal entities can also be made subject to security measures on account of these crimes.

One of the most distinctive features of the offense is that neither the prosecution nor the sentence for it is subject to any statute of limitations, meaning the passage of time provides no bar to prosecution however long it has been since the acts occurred. This is reinforced by a rule of universal jurisdiction: under Article 13 of the Penal Code, these crimes fall within the reach of Turkish law regardless of where they were committed or by whom. As a consequence of these features, crimes against humanity are not treated as political offenses for purposes of extradition law.

Procedurally, the offense falls within the jurisdiction of the heavy penal courts given the severity of the applicable penalty. It is prosecuted ex officio rather than upon complaint, and it falls outside the scope of mediation. It should also be noted candidly that Turkish judicial practice has not yet produced a substantial or settled body of case law on the substance of Article 77; where domestic courts have addressed it, they have drawn heavily on international criminal law standards and comparative practice rather than an established domestic doctrine.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the key difference between crimes against humanity and genocide under Turkish law?**
Genocide requires a special intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, as such — a group defined by a fixed, pre-existing identity. Crimes against humanity require no destructive intent; they require only that the perpetrator knowingly commits one of the listed acts, pursuant to a plan and systematically, against a segment of the population identified by the perpetrator's political, philosophical, racial, or religious motive.

**Can a single act against one victim qualify as a crime against humanity?**
No. The law defines the victim as "a segment of the population," and Turkish courts have confirmed that an act directed against a single individual, without the required plan, systematic pattern, and discriminatory motive, cannot be classified as a crime against humanity, however serious the act itself may be.

**Does the statute of limitations ever run on crimes against humanity in Turkey?**
No. Article 77 expressly excludes both prosecutorial and penal statutes of limitations for these offenses. This non-applicability, however, has been treated by Turkish courts as running only prospectively from the date the provision entered into force, consistent with the constitutional prohibition on retroactive criminal legislation.

**Is founding or joining an organization created to commit these crimes itself a crime, even without an underlying act?**
Yes. Article 78 of the Penal Code separately punishes founding, leading, or joining an organization established for the purpose of committing genocide or crimes against humanity, without requiring that the organization has actually carried out any such crime.
