Foreigners living in Turkey, or Turkish citizens married abroad and then registering a second marriage locally, are sometimes surprised to learn that Turkish criminal law treats certain family-status conduct as a standalone offense rather than a purely civil matter. Two neighboring provisions of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) — Article 230 and Article 231 — sit in the chapter dealing with offenses against family order. Article 230 protects the principle that a person may be validly married to only one spouse at a time, and also punishes marrying under a concealed identity. Article 231 protects a child's right to an accurate legal record of parentage. Although grouped together, the two provisions defend different interests, carry different penalties, and are frequently misunderstood — particularly the outdated reference to "religious ceremony" still appearing in Article 230's title. This article explains both provisions, their elements, and how Turkish courts have applied them.

## The Interest Protected: One Valid Marriage at a Time, and an Accurate Record of Parentage

Article 230 and Article 231 both appear under the heading of offenses against family order, but they safeguard distinct values. Article 230 protects the integrity of Turkey's monogamous marriage system and the formal act of civil marriage itself. Article 231 protects a child's right to know their genuine biological and legal origin, together with the accuracy of the civil registry. Both provisions place the public reliability of the marriage institution and of family registration above private interests — the state has an interest in these records being accurate independent of whether any individual complains. That said, Article 231 also has a clear individual dimension: the child whose parentage is falsified or concealed has a personal right at stake, not merely an abstract public-order interest.

## Article 230(1): Contracting a Second Marriage While Already Married

Under Article 230(1), a person who is already married and goes through with a civil marriage to another person while the first marriage subsists faces a prison sentence of six months to two years. The offense requires that the person actually be married under Turkish civil law procedure at the time of the second ceremony, and that a second civil marriage act be formally completed before a marriage registrar. Only a marriage performed before the official marriage officer counts here — going through a religious ceremony alone, without also completing the civil procedure, does not satisfy this element. Because a bigamous second marriage is void and subject to annulment under Turkish civil law, the criminal provision is not concerned with whether the second marriage is legally valid; it punishes the fact that a married person went through with a second civil marriage act at all. The offense is only complete once the registrar's procedure has actually been finalized — merely filing an application or having banns published, without the ceremony being completed, does not amount to a completed offense.

## Article 230(2): Knowingly Marrying Someone Who Is Already Married

The legislator extends the same penalty — six months to two years' imprisonment — to the other party in the ceremony: someone who is not married themselves, but who knows that the person they are marrying is already married to someone else. This creates an independent offense definition rather than relying on general rules of complicity. The unmarried party is not treated as an accomplice or an aider of the married party's offense; they are directly liable as a principal offender under this paragraph in their own right, purely because of their knowledge of the other party's marital status. As a result, a single bigamous ceremony can produce two separate offenders — the married party under paragraph one, the knowing unmarried party under paragraph two — who are prosecuted independently of one another; a conviction of one is not a precondition for liability of the other. Knowledge is the operative element, and it functions as a specific intent requirement: a party who did not know that the other person was already married cannot be held liable under this paragraph.

## Article 230(3): Marrying Under a Concealed True Identity

The third paragraph addresses a different scenario: a person who conceals their true identity in order to marry someone else faces three months to one year in prison. What this paragraph protects is the other party's ability to consent to the marriage with full knowledge of who they are actually marrying — that is, the integrity of their marital consent. The identity concealment must be serious enough to actually mislead the registrar or the other party; trivial discrepancies in identity information that do not affect the genuineness of the other party's decision to marry fall outside this paragraph.

## The Mismatch Between the Article's Title and Its Current Content

Article 230's title still refers to "religious ceremony," but the paragraphs that once regulated that conduct — paragraphs five and six — were struck down by the Turkish Constitutional Court in a decision dated 27 May 2015 (File No. 2014/36, Decision No. 2015/51). As a result, going through a religious wedding ceremony before completing the civil marriage no longer constitutes an offense under Article 230 today, even though the article's title has not been updated to reflect this. Anyone applying this provision needs to be careful not to infer a criminal offense from the title alone; liability is confined strictly to the conduct actually described in the surviving paragraphs — one, two and three.

## Intent Requirement and the Special Limitation Period Rule

All three offenses defined in Article 230 require direct intent: the offender must knowingly be married themselves, know that the person they are marrying is already married, or knowingly conceal their identity, as the case may be. The legislator has also built in an unusual rule for when the limitation period for prosecution starts running: rather than beginning on the date the offense was committed, it begins only once the decision annulling the marriage becomes final in a separate civil proceeding. This reflects the fact that a bigamous or fraudulent marriage may not come to light until the annulment case concludes, and it makes criminal prosecution effectively contingent on an antecedent civil annulment ruling — in practice, the civil court must annul the marriage before the criminal case can meaningfully proceed.

## Article 231(1): Altering or Concealing a Child's Parentage

Article 231(1) punishes altering or concealing a child's parentage with imprisonment of one to three years. Anyone can commit this offense — a parent or a third party may register a child's true parentage inaccurately in the civil registry, or withhold information about it. The typical scenario is registering another person's child as one's own biological child. The paragraph describes two distinct forms of conduct: "altering" means actively supplying false information to the parentage record, while "concealing" means withholding known true parentage information or preventing the child from later learning it. The victim is the child whose identity is misrepresented or withheld; what the offense protects is the child's ability to learn their own biological and legal origin. This paragraph can only be committed intentionally — the offender must act knowing that the parentage being recorded or maintained is inaccurate.

## Article 231(2): The Negligent Variant — Mixing Up Infants in a Healthcare Facility

The second paragraph departs from the first by resting on a breach of a duty of care rather than intent: a person who negligently causes a child in a healthcare facility to be mixed up with another child faces up to one year in prison. This wording marks the paragraph as a negligence-based offense — no intent is required; the offender simply failed to properly carry out their duties, and that failure resulted in the children being mixed up. The offender is typically healthcare facility personnel, though the text does not formally limit who may be held liable. The absence of a minimum sentence and the one-year maximum show that this paragraph is treated as materially less serious than the intentional offense in paragraph one.

## Attempt, Concurrence and Procedure

Because the Article 230 offenses depend on the marriage act being formally completed before an official, situations where matters stop at an earlier stage can raise attempt-related questions. For Article 231(1), where a civil registry officer knowingly assists in recording a false parentage declaration, questions of complicity can arise; where the facts also overlap with forgery of an official document, Article 231, as the more specific provision, takes priority. None of these offenses is conditioned on a private complaint — they are investigated and prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative, and jurisdiction lies with the ordinary criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi). The comparatively modest statutory maximums for both articles mean these cases are handled under ordinary criminal procedure, without falling within the jurisdiction of the heavy penal courts reserved for more serious offenses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does going through only a religious wedding ceremony while already married amount to bigamy under Turkish law?**
No. Article 230(1) requires a completed civil marriage procedure before an official marriage registrar. A religious ceremony alone, without a civil marriage act, does not satisfy this element, particularly since the paragraphs that once separately punished unauthorized religious ceremonies were annulled by the Constitutional Court in 2015.

**Can the unmarried party in a bigamous marriage also be prosecuted?**
Yes, but only if that person knew the other party was already married. Article 230(2) makes knowing participation in the ceremony by the unmarried party an independent offense, punished the same as the married party's offense, and the two are prosecuted separately from each other.

**When does the statute of limitations start running for these marriage-related offenses?**
Not from the date of the ceremony. Article 230(4) sets a special rule under which the limitation period begins only once the court decision annulling the marriage becomes final, meaning a civil annulment case typically has to conclude before the limitation clock effectively starts.

**Is it a crime if hospital staff negligently mix up two newborns, without any intent to falsify parentage?**
Yes, but as a separate and less severe offense. Article 231(2) punishes negligently causing infants in a healthcare facility to be mixed up with up to one year's imprisonment, distinct from the intentional falsification or concealment of parentage punished under Article 231(1).
