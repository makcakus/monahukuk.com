*Article 10 – (1) Mediators are prohibited from engaging in any attempt or conduct that may be regarded as advertising in order to obtain work, and in particular from using on their signs and printed papers any title other than mediator, lawyer and academic titles.*

*Article 11 – (1) At the beginning of the mediation activity, the mediator is obliged to inform the parties properly about the essentials, the process and the consequences of mediation.*

*Article 12 – (1) Mediators are charged an entry fee upon registration in the registry and an annual fee for each year. (2) Entry fees and annual fees are recorded as revenue of the general budget.*

These three provisions, which close the third chapter of the Law, appear at first sight unrelated: a professional prohibition, a duty to inform and a financial obligation. What links them is that mediation is designed **not as a free commercial activity but as a service subject to public supervision**. A mediator cannot chase work, must inform the person before them, and can remain on the registry only by fulfilling a financial obligation.

Of these three provisions, the one with the gravest practical consequences is undoubtedly Article 11.

## The Advertising Ban: Scope and Limits

Article 10 contains two separate prohibitions. The general prohibition covers **any attempt or conduct that may be regarded as advertising in order to obtain work**. The special prohibition prevents the use, on signs and printed papers, of titles other than mediator, lawyer and academic titles.

The rationale of the ban is the same as that of the advertising ban in Article 55 of the Attorneyship Law: turning mediation into a field of competition could push a mediator to get close to one of the parties in order to obtain work, and would damage impartiality. Consistently with this, Article 7/3 prohibits receiving a fee in return for acting as an intermediary for, or recommending, particular persons, and treats such transactions as void.

The limits of the ban are debated in practice. A mediator maintaining a website containing their name, title and contact details, publishing scholarly work, giving seminars, or having their field of specialisation appear in the registry information published by the Department is not regarded as advertising. By contrast, promotions containing success rates, a "settlement guarantee", client references or promises of fee discounts fall within the ban.

Article 10 provides no direct sanction; a breach triggers the warning and removal-from-registry process under Article 21/2. For lawyer-mediators, the disciplinary provisions of the Attorneyship Law and the Union of Turkish Bar Associations' Advertising Ban Regulation also apply. The Turkish Mediators' Ethical Rules laid down by the Mediation Board elaborate this field; the Council of State has regarded the making of these rules as falling within the Board's statutory competence and characterised the determination of ethical rules as meeting an important need.

## The Duty to Inform: Three Distinct Headings

Article 11 obliges the mediator, **at the beginning** of the activity, to inform the parties "properly about the essentials, the process and the consequences" of mediation. Although short, the provision identifies three distinct headings.

**Essentials.** What mediation is, that the mediator will render no decision, that the parties may withdraw from the process at any moment, that the sessions are confidential, and that what is said at the table cannot be used as evidence in later litigation.

**Process.** How the appointment is made, the three-week period (extendable by one week in compulsory circumstances), the practice of private sessions, who may attend the negotiations, and how the fee is calculated.

**Consequences.** These are the most critical part of the information: that where a settlement is reached no action may be brought again concerning the matters agreed (Art. 18/5); that the settlement document may be deemed a document having the character of a judgment and may be put directly to enforcement (Arts. 18/2 and 18/4); and the sanction that non-attendance at the first meeting produces as to litigation costs and attorney's fees (Art. 18/A/11).

In practice, the duty is discharged through a document signed by the parties known as the "mediation process information minutes". The existence of those minutes creates a strong presumption that the information was given; their absence is assessed against the mediator in later invalidity claims.

## The Effect of Defective Information on the Settlement

A breach of Article 11 is not regulated as a direct ground of invalidity. In practice, however, defective information — assessed not alone but together with other procedural defects — determines the fate of the settlement document.

In a case where no document showing that the parties had been invited to the session was submitted, where the minutes had the character of a release, and where the mediator gave no explanation at all that they were the mediator or as to the legal consequences of mediation, the 9th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation held that this was incompatible with the mediator's independent and impartial position. By contrast, the Chamber also states that where a party who admits having signed the information minutes and the settlement document, and does not contest their signature, fails to support an abstract allegation of fraud with concrete evidence, the allegation cannot be regarded as proven.

The resulting picture is this: where the information minutes have been signed, the burden of proving a claim based on defective information becomes heavier. Attending the session with counsel is therefore the only practical way of ensuring that the party understands the process, independently of whether the information was in fact given — particularly for foreign parties who lack proficiency in the language in which the sessions are conducted.

A high level of education or institutional experience does not remove the need for information either. In one dispute before the Court of Cassation it was argued that the claimant's senior position and experience did not mean that they had prior knowledge of a legal institution such as mediation; that approach shows that the duty to inform cannot be lightened according to the person.

## Dues: The Financial Condition of Remaining on the Registry

Article 12 provides that mediators are charged an **entry fee** upon registration and an **annual fee** for each year, and that these are recorded as revenue of the general budget. The power to determine the amounts belongs to the Mediation Board under Article 32/1(f).

Non-payment of dues amounts to non-fulfilment of an obligation laid down by the Law and may trigger the warning and removal-from-registry process in Article 21/2. That the dues are recorded as general budget revenue is the financial reflection of mediation being designed not as a professional association but as a service organised directly within the structure of the Ministry of Justice — mediators form no union or chamber, and their dues go to the Treasury rather than to a professional body.

## Case Law of the Court of Cassation and the Council of State

The following decisions show how the duty to inform and the professional rules of mediation are supervised in practice.

**1. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2024/8846, Decision No. 2024/11005, dated 09.09.2024**

Assessing together the absence of an invitation document, the release character of the minutes and the witness statements, the Chamber established that the mediator had not declared to the other side that they were the mediator nor what the legal consequences of mediation are, and held that this was incompatible with the mediator's independent and impartial position.

**2. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2025/6508, Decision No. 2025/7765, dated 13.10.2025**

The decision establishes that where a claimant who admits having signed the mediation process information minutes and the settlement document, and does not contest their signature, fails to support an allegation of fraud with concrete witness evidence or other proof, the allegation of defective consent cannot be regarded as proven.

**3. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2022/17651, Decision No. 2023/1140, dated 23.01.2023**

In the dispute it was argued that the claimant's senior position and experience did not mean that they had prior knowledge of the institution of mediation, and that the mediation documents had been prepared before the meeting. The decision is illustrative of the debate that the duty to inform cannot be lightened according to the party's characteristics.

**4. Council of State, Council of Administrative Litigation Chambers, Case No. 2023/1830, Decision No. 2024/1165, dated 23.05.2024**

The Council accepted that under Articles 30 and 32 of Law No. 6325 the Mediation Board is charged with determining the professional rules of mediation, and that the making of the Turkish Mediators' Ethical Rules therefore falls within that competence.

**5. Council of State, 10th Chamber, Case No. 2018/62, Decision No. 2022/6437, dated 22.12.2022**

The Chamber stated that, although the Law and the Regulation contain general provisions on the institution of mediation and on mediators, determining ethical rules for mediators is a necessity in the sense of meeting an important need and is required for the coherence of mediation legislation.
