Foreign companies operating in Turkey typically encounter Turkish data protection rules through the Personal Data Protection Law (KVKK) and its administrative fines. What is less well known outside Turkey is that a separate, older framework runs alongside KVKK: Articles 135 through 140 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) criminalize certain conduct involving personal data, independent of any administrative proceeding. A data controller, an employee who mishandles records, or even an individual who shares someone else's information without authorization can face criminal liability under these provisions, quite apart from whatever fine the Personal Data Protection Board might impose. Understanding where the criminal threshold sits — and how it differs from a routine KVKK compliance failure — matters for any business or individual facing a Turkish criminal investigation or complaint over personal data.

## The Protected Interest and How It Relates to KVKK

Articles 135-140 sit within the part of the Penal Code dealing with offences against private life, and the interest they protect is what Turkish doctrine calls informational self-determination: an individual's ability to know about, control, and object to how their personal data is collected, processed and shared. This is traced back to Article 20 of the Turkish Constitution, which guarantees a right to protection of personal data, including the rights to be informed about one's data, to access it, and to demand its correction or deletion. KVKK gives that constitutional guarantee administrative teeth — fines, orders and other regulatory measures — but it does not exhaust the field. The Penal Code criminalizes only a narrower set of especially serious acts: unlawfully recording personal data, unlawfully transferring or obtaining it, and failing to delete it once a statutory retention period has expired. The practical consequence is important: not every KVKK violation is automatically a crime. Criminal liability requires proof that the specific conduct described in the Penal Code articles actually occurred, not merely that a data processing rule was breached.

## Unlawfully Recording Personal Data (Article 135)

Article 135(1) punishes anyone who unlawfully records personal data with imprisonment of one to three years. The offence has no special requirements on the offender's side — anyone can commit it — while the victim must be a natural person whose data was recorded; corporate or institutional information is not "personal data" for these purposes. The decisive question is lawfulness: recording carried out with the data subject's consent, or under a legal authorization or legitimate basis, does not meet the definition of the offence. It makes no difference whether the recording is electronic, on paper, or audio-visual, so long as the information makes an identified or identifiable person recognizable. This is a "point in time" offence — it is complete the moment the data is recorded, and simply continuing to store it afterward does not create a new, separate offence.

Article 135(2) raises the penalty by half where the recorded data relates to a person's political, philosophical or religious views, ethnic origin, or — when unlawfully processed — moral tendencies, sexual life, health status or union affiliation. This tracks the internationally familiar concept of "special category" or sensitive personal data, and the enhanced penalty reflects the heightened risk of discrimination and harm that recording such data carries.

## Putting Data Into Circulation: The Three Acts Under Article 136

Article 136(1) addresses what happens after data exists: it punishes anyone who unlawfully gives personal data to another person, disseminates it, or obtains it, with imprisonment of two to four years. Any one of these three acts is sufficient on its own. "Giving" means transferring data to a specific person or persons; "disseminating" means making it available to an indeterminate number of people; and "obtaining" means bringing data that was previously under someone else's control into one's own control — for example, through unauthorized access. A notable feature of Article 136 is that it applies regardless of whether the data was originally recorded lawfully or unlawfully under Article 135 — putting data into circulation is treated as an independent wrong. A later amendment (Article 136(2), added by Law No. 7188) increases the penalty by a factor of one where the data in question consists of statements or recordings taken under the Criminal Procedure Code's witness or victim protection provisions, aimed at deterring unlawful disclosure of testimony gathered in criminal proceedings.

## Aggravating Factors: Abuse of Public Authority or Professional Position

Article 137 increases the penalty for offences under Articles 135 and 136 by half where the act is committed either by a public official abusing the authority of their office, or by someone taking advantage of the ease of access afforded by a particular profession or trade. These are two independent alternative grounds — meeting both does not double the increase. The provision targets situations where a relationship of trust, or the reach of public office, made the offence easier to commit, and carries particular weight for professions with heavy exposure to personal data, such as healthcare, banking, law and law enforcement.

## The Failure-to-Delete Offence (Article 138)

Article 138(1) addresses a different kind of wrongdoing: a failure to act. Anyone under a statutory obligation to delete personal data from a system once the legally prescribed retention period has expired, but who fails to do so, faces imprisonment of one to two years. Doctrine classifies this as an offence of omission — what is punished is not an active step by the offender, but the failure to carry out a deletion duty that the law has already imposed once the retention clock runs out. Only a person who is legally charged with that deletion duty can commit this offence, which makes it a special-capacity crime rather than one anyone could commit. Article 138(2) increases the penalty by a factor of one where the data in question is data that should have been eliminated or destroyed under the Criminal Procedure Code.

## Fault Requirement

All of the offences in this group require intent. Turkish law does not provide for a negligent version of any of them, so careless or merely negligent handling of personal data does not trigger criminal liability under these articles — although it may still expose a controller to KVKK administrative sanctions. Intent means the offender knowingly and willingly recorded, transferred, obtained, or failed to delete data understood to be personal and to be handled unlawfully; for the sensitive-data aggravation under Article 135(2), the offender must also have known the data fell into that special category. Where an offender is genuinely and unavoidably mistaken about the unlawfulness of the conduct, general rules on mistake affecting culpability may come into play.

## Prosecution: An Exception to the Usual Complaint Requirement

Article 139 states that offences in this part of the Penal Code are generally prosecuted only upon the victim's complaint — except for unlawfully recording personal data (Article 135), unlawfully transferring or obtaining it (Article 136), and failing to delete it (Article 138). Those three offences are investigated and prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative, without needing a complaint, unlike other offences in the same chapter (such as violations of correspondence or private life). This reflects a policy judgment that personal data security also implicates public order, not just an individual grievance: even if the victim withdraws their complaint, the criminal proceedings for these three offences do not automatically end.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does every violation of KVKK also amount to a criminal offence?**
No. KVKK violations are handled administratively, mainly through fines from the Personal Data Protection Board. The Penal Code criminalizes only specific, more serious conduct — unlawful recording, unlawful transfer or obtaining, and failure to delete data after the statutory retention period. A compliance gap that does not involve one of these specific acts will not, by itself, trigger criminal liability.

**Can a victim end a criminal case by withdrawing their complaint?**
Not for the core offences. Unlawfully recording data (Article 135), unlawfully transferring or obtaining it (Article 136), and failing to delete it (Article 138) are prosecuted by the public prosecutor regardless of complaint, so withdrawing a complaint does not automatically end the case. This differs from other offences in the same part of the Penal Code, which do require a complaint.

**Does an employee's access to data they are already authorized to view count as "obtaining" it under Article 136?**
Not simply by virtue of viewing it. "Obtaining" requires bringing data that was under someone else's control into one's own control, typically through unauthorized access. Querying a system using access credentials that were legitimately issued for one's job is a different situation from that, and Turkish courts have drawn a real distinction between the two depending on the facts.

**Does a company face criminal penalties if an employee commits one of these offences?**
Legal entities cannot be sentenced to imprisonment, but Article 140 provides for the imposition of security measures specific to legal entities where these offences are committed in connection with their activities. This means that a company's own liability can be engaged alongside that of the individual employee who committed the underlying act.
