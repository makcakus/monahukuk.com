*Article 6 – (1) Mediators entered in the registry have the right to use the title of mediator and the powers conferred by that title.*

*(2) The mediator is obliged to state this title during the mediation activity.*

*(3) (Added: 12/10/2017-7036/19) The Department of Mediation is empowered to determine the mediators' fields of specialisation and the procedures and principles relating to specialisation.*

In Turkish law, mediation is tied to a protected title. Article 6/1 establishes this in two directions: those entered in the registry have the **right** to use the title and the powers it confers; those not entered may not use it. The second paragraph turns that right into a **duty** — the mediator must state this capacity while acting. Although at first sight formal, in practice this duty has become a safeguard that affects the validity of the process itself.

## The Source of the Title: Registration

The title of mediator arises not from a training certificate but from entry in the registry kept by the Department of Mediation of the Ministry of Justice. A person who has completed mediation training and even passed the examination is not a mediator until registered; Article 20/3 expressly provides that a mediator may begin practising only from the date of registration.

Admission to the registry does not operate like entry into a liberal profession. The 10th Chamber of the Council of State has consistently held that whether a mediation examination is held at all, and how many mediators are to be brought into the system, lies within the administration's discretion, having regard to criteria such as the number of mediators currently serving, the volume of mediation applications and the widening scope of mandatory mediation. Indeed, the inclusion of employment disputes in 2018 and of commercial actions in 2019 within mandatory mediation is cited in those decisions as the reason for holding an examination.

Article 20/4 also governs the geographical dimension of the registry: the Department lists registered mediators according to the judicial commissions of the first-instance courts in which they wish to serve, and a mediator may be entered on **no more than three commission lists**. In mandatory mediation, appointment is made from those lists under Article 18/A/5.

## The Practical Weight of the Duty to State the Title

Article 6/2 contains one of the most frequently breached obligations in mediation — and one whose breach has the gravest consequences. A party who does not know that the person opposite is a mediator can neither be regarded as informed under Article 11 nor assess the consequences of the document they sign.

The 9th Civil Chamber of the Turkish Court of Cassation, in a case where no document at all was produced showing that the parties had been invited to the session, where the minutes had the character of a release, and where witness statements showed that the mediator had never explained to the other party that they were a mediator or what the legal consequences of mediation were, held that this was incompatible with the mediator's independent and impartial position. In practice this breach appears not on its own but together with other irregularities — minutes in the nature of a release, absence of an invitation document — and leads to the invalidity of the minutes.

It should be noted that for lawyer-mediators the duty has a second layer: because the same person holds both the capacity of lawyer and that of mediator, they must make clear in which capacity they are acting. Article 9/4 prohibits a mediator from later acting as counsel for one of the parties in the dispute in which they served; for that prohibition to work, the capacities must be clear from the outset.

## Fields of Specialisation: The Meaning of the 2017 Amendment

Article 6/3 was added in 2017 by Law No. 7036 and empowers the Department of Mediation to determine mediators' fields of specialisation and the procedures and principles of specialisation. The rationale is practical: as the scope of mandatory mediation widened, appointments had to be made in technically distinct fields such as employee claims, commercial disputes, consumer law and lease relationships, and the inefficiency of distributing every dispute at random to every mediator became apparent.

Specialisation is connected to Article 18/A/3: under that provision the Department notifies the commissions of those wishing to act as mediators, **stating their fields of specialisation where applicable**. However, specialisation is not a binding criterion of appointment; the office takes it into account when appointing from the list, and if the parties agree on any mediator appearing on the list, that mediator is appointed (Article 18/A/5).

The specialisation regime also has its limit: the appointment of a mediator who is not a specialist does not on its own render the process invalid. Specialisation is not a condition of validity but an organisational tool aimed at service quality.

## The Powers Conferred by the Title, and Their Limits

The phrase "the powers conferred by that title" in Article 6/1 refers to the concrete powers the statute grants the mediator: inviting the parties to a meeting (Article 15/1), meeting the parties separately or together (Article 8/1), putting forward a settlement proposal (Article 15/7), drawing up the final minutes and, where necessary, signing them alone (Article 17/2), and requesting an annotation restricting the power of disposition in the land registry in disputes over immovables (Article 17/B/2).

The outer limit of these powers is drawn by Article 15/4: acts which, by their nature, may be performed only by a judge as the exercise of judicial power cannot be performed by a mediator. A mediator cannot force a party into the process despite another party's request, cannot assess evidence and establish facts, and cannot order interim measures or attachment.

Misuse of the title falls under the removal procedure of Article 21/2 and, for lawyer-mediators, additionally under the disciplinary provisions of the Turkish Attorneyship Law.

## Decisions of the Court of Cassation and the Council of State

The following decisions illustrate the practice on acquiring, using and supervising the mediator title.

**1. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, File No. 2024/8846, Decision No. 2024/11005, 09.09.2024**

Assessing together the failure to produce any document showing that the parties had been invited to the session, the fact that the minutes had the character of a release, and the witness statements, the Chamber found that the mediator had not declared to the other party that they were a mediator or explained the legal consequences of mediation, and held that this was incompatible with the mediator's independent and impartial position.

**2. Council of State, 10th Chamber, File No. 2020/6689, Decision No. 2021/6508, 20.12.2021**

The decision records that holding an examination was resolved upon because new mediators had to be brought into the system once employment disputes were included in mandatory mediation in 2018 and commercial actions in 2019; and it emphasises that determining the number of mediators to be admitted lies within the Ministry's discretion.

**3. Council of State, 10th Chamber, File No. 2020/2366, Decision No. 2021/6689, 22.12.2021**

The Chamber set out the statutory framework: registration in the mediator registry requires completion of mediation training and success in the written examination, and it is the Mediation Board's responsibility to determine the basic principles and standards of training and examination.

**4. Council of State, 10th Chamber, File No. 2021/7187, Decision No. 2025/2686, 22.05.2025**

The decision confirmed that it is lawful to take into account the number of mediators currently serving and the volume of mediation applications when setting the number of mediators required, reinforcing the character of the admission regime as dependent on administrative planning.

**5. Council of State, 10th Chamber, File No. 2018/62, Decision No. 2022/6437, 22.12.2022**

The Chamber held that, although both Law No. 6325 and the Regulation contain general provisions on the basic principles, rights and obligations relating to mediation and mediators, laying down a code of ethics for mediators was necessary in order to meet an important need.
