TTK Art. 1096-1102 shift focus from preventing risk to documenting it: they build the evidentiary backbone Turkish courts rely on long after a voyage ends.

## The Ship's Log

Under Art. 1096, every ship (except small port-only vessels) must keep a log recording major events from the start of loading, normally maintained by the first mate under the captain's supervision. Art. 1097 lists nine mandatory entries — weather, route, position, bilge water levels, soundings, pilot times, crew changes, accidents, and births/deaths — signed by both the captain and first mate. This is not a formality: Turkish courts treat the log as primary evidence not only in maritime disputes but even in seafarers' labor claims (overtime, rest days), because it is the closest thing to a contemporaneous, dual-witnessed record of what happened aboard.

## Sea Protest After an Accident

Art. 1098-1101 create a separate, official proceeding: after an accident affecting the ship or cargo, the captain (or anyone with a proven interest) can demand a "sea protest" be recorded before a Turkish court or, abroad, a Turkish consulate — even if the ship is a total loss. Art. 1099 requires the record to cover both what happened and what mitigating steps were taken. Art. 1100 ties this directly to the log: the captain's statement is built on the log's entries, and any inability to produce the log must be explained. Art. 1101 keeps the original filed with the issuing authority, with only certified copies released to interested parties.

## Protecting the Owner Even After Total Loss

Art. 1102 closes this cluster with a strikingly broad rule: the captain must protect the shipowner's interests for as long as necessary, even after the ship is destroyed — gathering insurance documentation, safeguarding recoverable cargo or wreckage, and preserving claims against third parties.

## Practical Takeaway

For insurers and cargo interests, the log and sea protest are often the decisive evidence in a dispute litigated years after the voyage — Turkish courts have reopened cargo-damage findings specifically because a captain's sea-protest statement, recorded in the log, was never properly weighed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the ship's log and why is it important?

Under TTK Article 1096/1, <b>a book called the ship's log is kept on every vessel</b>; the <b>material occurrences</b> taking place during each voyage, from the moment goods or ballast begin to be loaded, are entered in this book. Because the log constitutes a contemporaneous record of the navigation, weather conditions, manoeuvres and accidents, it is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in disputes over collision, general average and carrier liability. Under Article 1097/2, the log is signed by the master and the first mate.

### When is a sea protest drawn up?

Under TTK Article 1098/1, where, during the voyage, <b>an accident occurs that concerns the vessel or the goods carried, or that is likely to give rise to material damage of another kind</b>, the master is <b>authorised</b> to request that a sea protest be drawn up — <b>even if the vessel is lost</b> — and, if requested to do so, is <b>obliged</b> to have it drawn up. The protest ensures that the accident is recorded in an official minute immediately after it occurs, before an official authority.

### From where, and how, is a sea protest obtained?

Under TTK Article 1100/1, in order to have the facts established, the master applies to the <b>court or consulate</b> specified in Article 1098, bringing with them <b>a roll showing the full names of all the crew, the ship's log, and any other documents available relating to the incident</b>. Under Article 1099, the significant occurrences of the voyage — in particular accidents and the measures taken to prevent or reduce damage — are established fully and clearly. Under Article 1101, the original of the minute is retained, and certified copies are given to interested parties who request them.
