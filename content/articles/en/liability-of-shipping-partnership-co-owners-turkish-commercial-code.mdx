TTK Art. 1080-1081 turn to the outward-facing question: how far can a joint shipping ownership's creditors reach into individual co-owners' pockets?

## A Deliberate Middle Ground

Art. 1080 places donatma iştiraki between two familiar extremes. Corporate shareholders are typically shielded entirely from company debts; general-partnership partners are typically jointly and severally liable for the whole debt. This structure lacks a corporate liability shield since it has no separate legal personality — but the Code does not go all the way to joint-and-several liability either. Instead, each co-owner is personally liable to third parties for the venture's debts *only in proportion to its ownership share* — a creditor can pursue a co-owner directly, but only for that co-owner's proportionate slice, never the whole debt. This proportional model tracks the underlying co-ownership structure itself: rights and obligations both track the share, with none of the "all for one" logic of joint tenancy. The shipowner's separate right to limit liability under international maritime conventions is expressly preserved alongside this rule.

Turkish courts have confirmed the practical consequence directly: because the entity has no legal personality, creditors can sue or enforce against co-owners individually — but only for their proportionate share, never more.

## Liability on Transfer of a Co-Ownership Share

Art. 1081 governs what happens when a share changes hands. A transferring co-owner remains treated as a co-owner — and stays liable for debts arising before the transfer — until the transfer is actually *notified* to the other co-owners or the ship manager. The buyer, meanwhile, becomes liable from the moment of acquisition onward, regardless of notice. This creates a deliberate overlap period protecting the venture's other members, who need certainty about who they are dealing with. The buyer also steps into the seller's existing contractual bindings (agreements, resolutions, undertakings already made), while retaining warranty claims against the seller if the share turns out more burdened than expected — and other co-owners may even set off the seller's outstanding debts against the buyer. The same rules apply to shares acquired through forced (enforcement) sale.

## Practical Takeaway

Anyone buying into an existing joint shipping ownership should confirm the transfer has actually been notified to the other co-owners and the manager — an unnotified transfer leaves the seller still on the hook, which can complicate later recourse arrangements between buyer and seller.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are the co-owning shipowners jointly and severally liable for the venture's debts?

No. Under TTK Article 1080/1 the co-owning shipowners are liable to third parties for the venture's debts <b>personally and in proportion to their shares in the venture</b>. The liability is not joint and several: a creditor may claim from each co-owning shipowner only the part corresponding to that owner's share, and bears the risk of a co-owner's insolvency. This is one of the fundamental differences between a joint shipping venture and a general partnership.

### When does the liability of a co-owner who transfers their share end?

Under TTK Article 1081/1 a co-owning shipowner who transfers their share in the venture is, <b>until the transfer is notified to the other shipowners or to the ship's manager by the transferee together with the transferor</b>, still regarded as a co-owning shipowner in relations with them and remains liable to them in that capacity <b>for all debts arising before that notification</b>. The notification thus cuts off liability for the future; debts arising in the past continue.

### Does limitation of liability apply to co-owning shipowners?

Yes. TTK Article 1080/1 opens with the words <b>'subject to the provisions on limitation of liability for maritime claims'</b>. The regime of limitation of liability laid down in Articles 1328 et seq. therefore also applies to the co-owning shipowners; each of them benefits from the right to limit in proportion to their share in the venture. This reflects the basic risk-sharing mechanism of maritime trade in the joint shipping venture as well.
