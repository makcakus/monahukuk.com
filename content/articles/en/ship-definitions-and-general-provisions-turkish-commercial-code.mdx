TTK Art. 931-937 open Book Five, "Maritime Commerce" — roughly 470 articles governing everything from ship registration to collision and general average. These first seven articles build the conceptual scaffolding everything else rests on.

## What Counts as a Ship

Art. 931 defines a "ship" broadly: any craft intended for water-based movement, capable of floating, and not "too small" — self-propulsion is not required, so barges and unpowered platforms qualify. A "merchant ship" is any ship devoted to or actually used for an economic purpose in water, regardless of who owns or operates it. This economic-use test is what triggers most of Book Five's provisions.

## Three Independent Tests of Fitness

Art. 932 carefully separates three concepts often confused in practice: seaworthiness (structural soundness against normal sea hazards), voyage-worthiness (whether the specific, planned voyage is adequately supported by crew, fuel, and supplies), and cargo-worthiness (whether the ship's holds suit the particular cargo). Courts apply this distinction in concrete disputes — including whether a seized ship may be released from an execution freeze, which turns on voyage-worthiness specifically.

## Constructive Total Loss

Art. 933 defines two related but distinct concepts: a ship "beyond repair" (repair is physically or logistically impossible) and a ship "not worth repairing" (repair cost exceeds three-quarters of its prior value, valued as of the start of the relevant voyage). Turkish courts require this to be proven with technical repair reports and valuation evidence — a bare assertion does not suffice, and deregistration cannot rest on an unproven claim.

## Ships as a Hybrid Property Category

Under Art. 936, every ship — registered or not — counts as movable property. But Art. 937 carves out important exceptions: ships subject to enforcement rules for immovables (mortgage foreclosure follows real-estate procedure, not chattel-pledge procedure) and select Civil Code provisions on guardianship, family homes, and estate liquidation that extend "immovable" treatment to ships. Only ships on the Turkish registry benefit from this hybrid status — a foreign-flagged vessel remains a pure movable under Turkish law.

## Practical Takeaway

For anyone financing, buying, or enforcing against a Turkish-flagged vessel, getting the seaworthiness/voyage-worthiness distinction and the movable/immovable hybrid status right at the outset avoids procedural missteps that Turkish courts treat as grounds for reversal.
