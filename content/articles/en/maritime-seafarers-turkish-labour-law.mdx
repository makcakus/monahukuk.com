Turkey's general labour statute, Law No. 4857, expressly excludes maritime and air transport work from its scope (Art. 4/1-a). This is not a gap in protection: seafarers working on ships fall under a dedicated statute, the Maritime Labour Law No. 854, dated 20 April 1967, built specifically around the operational realities of shipping — voyage-based work, crews that may be at sea for extended periods, and vessels that can be sold or transferred mid-employment. For shipowners, ship management companies and HR teams handling crew based in or operating out of Turkish ports such as Antalya, understanding where Law No. 854 applies — and how it departs from the general labour regime on matters like contract form, working time and severance — is essential to avoiding disputes and miscalculated payouts. This article sets out who qualifies as a seafarer under the law, the formal requirements for a seafarer's employment contract, and the principal ways severance and termination rules differ from ordinary employment law.

## Who Falls Within the Scope of Law No. 854

Article 1 of Law No. 854 sets two cumulative conditions for its application: the vessel must fly the Turkish flag, and it must have a gross tonnage of 100 or more. Gross tonnage is simply a measure of a ship's total internal volume, and the legislator chose this threshold to carve out small boats and fishing vessels, which are left to a different legal regime. The same article contains an anti-avoidance rule: even where individual vessels fall below the 100-tonnage threshold, the law still applies if the combined tonnage of all vessels belonging to the same employer reaches 100 or more, or if that employer employs five or more seafarers in total. This prevents an owner who operates a fleet of small boats from structuring around the law simply by keeping each vessel under the threshold. The definition of "vessel" is deliberately broad and extends to smaller craft such as rowboats, barges, lighters and similar vessels.

Turkish case law has repeatedly clarified that simply working in a role that resembles seafaring is not enough to trigger Law No. 854. The Court of Cassation has consistently held that two conditions must be met together: the person must actually work as a seafarer, and that work must take place on a Turkish-flagged vessel operated by, or belonging to, an employer engaged in maritime transport. If either condition is missing, the relationship does not fall under Law No. 854 — it will instead be assessed under Law No. 4857 or the general provisions of the Turkish Code of Obligations, depending on the facts of the case.

## Who Counts as a Seafarer, Employer, Captain and Employer's Representative

Article 2 of the law defines four roles that reflect the hierarchical structure of ship operations. The term "seafarer" is drawn broadly to cover the captain, officers, and crew members — anyone working on board under an employment contract. The "employer" may be the vessel's owner, but it may equally be a person who operates a vessel they do not own, on their own account — a definition that captures charter arrangements, where the operator rather than the registered owner bears employer status. The "captain" is the person responsible for commanding and managing the vessel, or their substitute where the captain is unavailable for compelling reasons. Finally, the law defines an "employer's representative" as anyone authorized to act on behalf of the captain or employer — and makes clear that the employer remains directly responsible for that representative's dealings and undertakings toward the crew. This last rule ensures that day-to-day management carried out through intermediaries on board does not weaken the chain of responsibility that protects the seafarer.

## Formal Requirements for the Seafarer's Employment Contract

Unlike the general labour law, Law No. 854 requires that a seafarer's employment contract be made in writing under certain conditions and contain a defined set of minimum elements, set out in Article 6. These include the employer's name and address, the seafarer's identifying details and registration number, the name, registration number, tonnage and registry office of the vessel on which the seafarer will work, the place and date the contract was concluded, the seafarer's duties, the date and place work is to begin, whether the contract is for a fixed term, the agreed wage basis and amount, and the payment terms, place and advance conditions. The strictness of this formal requirement reflects the nature of maritime work: long voyages, multinational crews and work carried out far from any court or authority make written proof of the parties' agreed terms far more important than in a typical shore-based employment relationship. Failing to conclude the contract in writing does not, by itself, invalidate the employment relationship, but it can shift the burden of proof against the employer in a subsequent dispute.

## Severance Pay: Where It Differs From the General Labour Law

Article 20 of Law No. 854 mirrors, in its general structure, the severance regime that once applied under the now-repealed Law No. 1475: 30 days' wages for each full year of service, payable on termination by the employer for reasons other than the seafarer's misconduct, on the seafarer's own resignation for compulsory military service or to draw an old-age, retirement or disability pension, or on the seafarer's death. Several features, however, set the maritime regime apart. Seniority is calculated on the total time the seafarer has worked for the same employer, across one or several vessels, even where the employment contract was renewed at intervals rather than continuing uninterrupted. Where a vessel is transferred to a new owner, both the transferring and the acquiring employer may, under certain conditions, be held liable for severance that accrued before the transfer. In addition, where wages are not fixed — as is common in maritime employment paid by voyage, piece rate, lump sum or percentage — severance is calculated on the seafarer's average wage over the preceding year rather than a single fixed figure. The Court of Cassation has also clarified a procedural point worth noting: because Law No. 854 contains no special provision on the applicable interest rate for severance claims, statutory interest applies rather than the higher deposit interest rate used for ordinary severance claims — a distinction that is easy to overlook when comparing outcomes with claims brought under the general severance regime.

## Working Time and Termination Rules

On working time, Law No. 854 broadly follows the general labour law's structure of an eight-hour day and forty-eight-hour week (Art. 26), but defines working time as time spent actually on duty or standing watch — a definition necessarily shaped by the fact that a vessel must be operated continuously at sea. Case law draws a distinction, for purposes of the weekly rest day, between port and short-hop coastal vessels on one hand and vessels making short, near or long voyages on the other; on voyaging vessels, a seafarer may be required to work extra hours during the voyage without their separate consent being required for each instance. On termination, Article 14 gives both the seafarer and the employer the right to terminate for just cause; where a seafarer terminates for just cause because the employer or its representative breached the law, the employment contract or other working conditions, the seafarer is entitled to severance pay under Article 20. The law's administrative penalty and inspection provisions (Arts. 49-50) mirror the general labour law's oversight structure, though the enforcement body and procedural details are specific to maritime regulation.

## Where Maritime Labour Disputes Are Heard

Disputes arising under Law No. 854 are heard in the labour courts, like other employment disputes. Article 5/1-a of the Labour Courts Law No. 7036 expressly places seafarers covered by Law No. 854 within the jurisdiction of the labour courts, and the same law's mandatory mediation provision (Art. 3/20) confirms that a seafarer under Law No. 854 is treated as an employee for mediation purposes — meaning that claims for wages, severance or other maritime labour entitlements must go through mandatory mediation before a lawsuit can be filed. Where, however, it turns out that the vessel or employer does not meet the tonnage and flag conditions required by Law No. 854, the seafarer benefits from neither that law nor Law No. 4857 (due to the maritime exclusion in Art. 4/1-a); the relationship then falls under the general service-contract provisions of the Turkish Code of Obligations, and jurisdiction may shift to the general civil courts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Law No. 854 apply to every ship's crew member in Turkey?**
No. It applies only where the vessel flies the Turkish flag and has a gross tonnage of 100 or more (or meets the fleet-tonnage or five-seafarer alternative test in Art. 1). Crew on smaller vessels that do not meet either test fall outside the law and may be governed by the Turkish Code of Obligations instead.

**Does a charter operator who does not own the vessel count as the employer?**
Yes. Article 2 defines "employer" to include a person who operates a vessel belonging to someone else, on their own account and for their own benefit — a definition designed to capture charter and operating arrangements, not just registered ownership.

**Is an oral seafarer employment contract void?**
No. The absence of a written contract meeting the requirements of Article 6 does not invalidate the employment relationship, but it weakens the employer's position on proof of the agreed terms if a dispute arises later.

**How is severance calculated if a seafarer is paid per voyage rather than a fixed monthly wage?**
Where the wage is not fixed — as with voyage-based, piece-rate, lump-sum or percentage pay — severance is calculated using the seafarer's average wage over the preceding year rather than a single wage figure at the date of termination.
