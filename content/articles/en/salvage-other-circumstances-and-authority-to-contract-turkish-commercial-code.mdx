This article covers the two provisions immediately following Art. 1298's [definition of salvage activity](/articles/salvage-activity-definition-and-scope-turkish-commercial-code): Art. 1299, which extends the chapter to two additional circumstances, and Art. 1300, which sets out who may sign a salvage contract and on whose behalf.

## Two Extra Situations Still Count as Salvage (Art. 1299)

Art. 1299(1)(a) confirms that the chapter still applies even where the salvor already had a statutory duty to render assistance — the classic example being a public coast-guard body performing its legal rescue function. This is a deliberate asymmetry with Art. 1298(4)(c): a pre-existing *contractual* duty takes a service outside salvage (because the parties already priced that risk into their agreement), but a pre-existing *statutory* duty does not, because a public rescue obligation carries no such pre-agreed economic offset. The effort, skill, and risk expended still deserve a reward.

Art. 1299(1)(b) confirms the chapter applies even where the salvor and the salved vessel share the same owner. A vessel deployed to rescue a sister vessel still incurs real crew time, equipment wear, and risk — the analysis is operation-based, not ownership-based, and in practice the two vessels are often covered by different insurers in any event.

## Authority to Sign a Salvage Contract (Art. 1300)

Art. 1300(1) authorizes the master to conclude a salvage contract on behalf of the vessel's owner, including agreeing on the competent court or arbitration. Art. 1300(2) extends this: both the owner and the master may sign a salvage contract on behalf of the owners of cargo and other property aboard, again including forum or arbitration clauses.

The reason for this broad agency is practical necessity: a vessel in danger cannot wait for a distant owner — let alone dozens of cargo owners scattered across time zones — to approve contract terms while the danger grows. Placing that authority with the person on the scene who can act immediately, the master, lets a binding salvage contract be concluded in minutes rather than days.

## Practical Takeaway

For owners, cargo interests, and their insurers, this means a master's signature on a salvage contract — including its jurisdiction or arbitration clause — genuinely binds the owner and cargo interests; it is not a formality that can later be disowned. Turkish case law confirms this squarely: the Court of Cassation has overturned a lower court that treated a master-signed jurisdiction clause as non-binding, and separately noted that this broad contracting power is balanced by the equity-review mechanism available under Art. 1301, which allows a court to adapt or annul salvage terms reached under duress or grossly disproportionate to the service rendered.
