Most employment relationships in Turkey are protected by the mandatory rules of Labour Code No. 4857, but that coverage is not universal. Certain categories of work — because of the nature of the job, the size of the workplace, or the status of the worker — are deliberately carved out of the Labour Code and pushed instead into the general service contract regime found in Articles 393 and following of the Turkish Code of Obligations (TBK) No. 6098. This does not leave the workers in question without legal protection: the Code of Obligations has its own protective rules for the party performing the work, but it lacks the Labour Code's detailed and largely mandatory package of severance pay, job security and overtime entitlements. For foreign investors and HR teams operating in Turkey, correctly classifying a working relationship as "Labour Code" or "Code of Obligations" is often the single most consequential legal question in a labour dispute, since it determines which rights exist at all.

## The Service Contract Under Article 393 of the Code of Obligations

Article 393 of the Code of Obligations builds its definition of the service contract around the same three building blocks used elsewhere in Turkish labour law: a subordinate working relationship, an open-ended or fixed duration, and pay set either by time worked or by output produced. Read that way, the provision tracks the Labour Code's own conception of an employment relationship almost feature for feature, which is precisely why the two regimes overlap so closely in substance while differing sharply in the level of protection they afford. The second paragraph of Article 393 extends the definition to part-time arrangements, confirming that regularly repeated part-time work also qualifies as a service contract. The third paragraph adds an important cross-reference: the general service contract provisions apply by analogy to apprenticeship contracts, without prejudice to any special legislation governing apprenticeships (such as Vocational Education Law No. 3308). In practice, the Code of Obligations' service contract rules do not apply to every relationship simply because the Labour Code is silent — they apply specifically to relationships that display the subordination structure of employment but that Article 4 of Labour Code No. 4857 has expressly excluded from that Code's scope.

## The Excluded Categories Listed in Article 4 of the Labour Code

Article 4 of Labour Code No. 4857 works through a fixed list of jobs and working relationships that sit entirely outside the Labour Code's reach. Transport by sea or air is one; so is farm and forestry work carried out at a small enough scale (50 workers or fewer); building work tied to a family's own farming operation is another, alongside handicraft work done at home among family members and relatives within three degrees of kinship, with no outsider involved. The list continues with domestic staff, apprentices, athletes and people undergoing rehabilitation, and closes with small workplaces — three employees or fewer — that qualify as tradesmen or craftsmen under the applicable special law. Some of these exclusions point toward other special statutes — maritime and air transport work, for instance, falls under the Maritime Labour Code and related legislation — while others fall directly into the Code of Obligations' service contract framework. The agricultural and forestry threshold is notable because it is expressed as a hard numerical line: once a workplace's headcount exceeds 50 employees, the Labour Code applies in full; at 50 or fewer, the relationship is governed by the Code of Obligations instead. This particular boundary is frequently litigated, because when an employer's "agricultural worker" defence conflicts with the employee's version of events, courts are required to investigate both the true nature of the work performed and the actual headcount of the business. The family-economy construction work and family handicraft exclusions rest on a similar rationale: the legislature did not want to burden non-commercial, family-based cooperation with the Labour Code's full weight of obligations.

## Domestic Workers: The Most Frequently Litigated Exclusion

Among all the excluded categories, the one that arises most often in practice is the "domestic services" exclusion under Article 4/1(e). According to the case law of the Court of Cassation, domestic services mean work performed to meet the everyday needs of a household — cooking, valet duties, cleaning, gardening and similar tasks. Workers in these roles are not considered "employees" for Labour Code purposes, and any claims they may have — for example, regarding severance pay, notice pay or overtime — must be pursued on the basis of the Code of Obligations' service contract provisions, in the ordinary courts of general jurisdiction rather than under the Labour Code framework. However, the exclusion is narrow: the Court of Cassation has consistently held that a nurse caring for a patient at home, or a person providing structured childcare requiring a distinct professional qualification, falls outside the concept of "domestic service" and must be assessed under the Labour Code instead. The distinguishing factor is not the abstract fact that the work takes place inside a home, but whether the work in question serves the household's ordinary day-to-day needs or amounts to a separate professional service.

## Apprentices and Athletes: A Partial, Not Absolute, Exclusion

The exclusion for apprentices and athletes is not as absolute as the domestic services exclusion. Article 4/1(f) removes apprentices from the Labour Code's general provisions, but the same Code preserves the application of its occupational health and safety rules to apprentices — recognising that, despite the primarily educational nature of the apprenticeship relationship, a person actually working on-site still needs their physical safety protected. The substance of the apprenticeship contract itself is governed, by analogy under Article 393(3) of the Code of Obligations, by the general service contract rules, subject to the special provisions of Vocational Education Law No. 3308. A comparable model applies to athletes: the employment relationship of professional athletes generally falls outside the Labour Code, and matters such as non-competition undertakings are addressed instead under the Code of Obligations' general provisions; an athlete's professional rights and status are largely shaped by the relevant federation's regulations together with the Code of Obligations.

## The Level of Protection Available Under the Code of Obligations

Although the Code of Obligations' service contract provisions do not create as detailed a protective framework as the Labour Code, they do not leave the worker unprotected either. Article 396 imposes a duty of diligence and loyalty on the worker, while Article 417 places a mirroring obligation on the employer to protect the worker's personality: the employer must take the measures necessary to prevent the worker from suffering psychological or sexual harassment and to ensure occupational health and safety, and where a breach of law or contract causes death, bodily harm or a violation of personality rights, the ordinary rules on contractual liability apply to any resulting compensation claim. The Code of Obligations' general rules on notice periods for termination, termination for just cause, and limitation periods also apply to workers governed by this regime. What is missing is specifically the Labour Code's own machinery: the statutory severance pay formula, notice pay, and the job security (reinstatement) mechanism. For this reason, determining at the outset whether a working relationship falls under the Labour Code or the Code of Obligations is the first and most decisive step in shaping both litigation strategy and the scope of the rights that can realistically be claimed.

## Which Court Has Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction is a separate and important question for disputes arising from service contracts governed by the Code of Obligations. Article 5/1(a) of Labour Courts Law No. 7036 defines the jurisdiction of labour courts to include disputes between employers and workers governed either by Labour Code No. 4857 or by the service contract provisions of the Code of Obligations. As a result, workers who are excluded from the Labour Code under Article 4 but who fall under the Code of Obligations can generally still bring their case before the labour courts, absent a contrary jurisdictional rule. That said, in some of the excluded categories — domestic workers being a recurring example in older case law — courts of general jurisdiction have at times been found competent instead, so the correct forum needs to be assessed carefully on the facts of each case.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If I employ a live-in housekeeper or gardener, does the Labour Code apply to them?**
Generally not. Work performed to meet a household's everyday needs — cooking, cleaning, gardening and similar tasks — typically falls under the "domestic services" exclusion in Article 4/1(e) of the Labour Code, meaning the relationship is instead governed by the Code of Obligations' service contract rules.

**Does that exclusion also cover a home nurse or a private tutor/childcare provider?**
No. Turkish case law treats roles that require a distinct professional qualification — such as a home-care nurse or a structured childcare educator — as falling outside the "domestic services" concept, meaning the Labour Code applies to them in full.

**If my agricultural business grows and exceeds 50 employees, does anything change?**
Yes. The Article 4/1(b) exclusion for agricultural and forestry workplaces only applies up to and including 50 employees. Once the headcount exceeds that threshold, Labour Code No. 4857 applies to the workplace in full, including severance pay and job security protections.

**Can a worker excluded from the Labour Code still sue in the labour courts?**
In most cases, yes. Labour Courts Law No. 7036 gives labour courts jurisdiction over disputes involving workers governed by the Code of Obligations' service contract provisions as well as those governed by the Labour Code, though the specific facts of certain excluded categories should still be checked.
