Having established when a freight contract can be terminated, TTK Art. 1224-1227 close out this part of the Code by settling how termination must be communicated and what each party owes afterward.

## Termination Must Be in Writing

Art. 1224 requires termination notice to be in writing, though fax, email, or similar technical means satisfy this — a familiar formula that recognizes maritime trade parties are often scattered across different countries or even at sea, where a strict "wet signature" requirement would be impractical.

## Termination Before the Voyage Starts: Clean and Cost-Free

Where termination happens before the voyage begins, Art. 1225 keeps things simple: neither party owes the other damages, and each simply settles obligations already accrued. This mirrors the no-fault lapse rule that applies when a ship is lost pre-voyage — procedurally different (an elected termination versus automatic lapse), but financially identical, because at this early stage neither side has made any irreversible commitment.

## Termination After the Voyage Starts: Distance Freight and Discharge Rules

Once underway, more is at stake. Under Art. 1226, the carrier is entitled not only to claims already accrued, but also to "distance freight" calculated under the same equity-based formula used for a lost ship — even if the cargo ends up back at the loading port. Absent agreement otherwise, cargo is discharged at or near the ship's location when termination occurs; for partial charter and general cargo (kırkambar) contracts, discharge that would delay the voyage or require transshipment needs the other shippers' consent, with the terminating shipper covering resulting costs and damage. The captain's emergency duty to protect cargo interests — including forwarding, warehousing, or selling cargo — applies equally here.

## Ships Chartered for Multiple Voyages

Art. 1227 confirms that the whole Art. 1209-1226 framework applies to multi-voyage charters only to the extent the contract's own nature permits — since concepts like "the voyage" or "loading port" don't always translate cleanly to a multi-voyage arrangement. Where a ship must first complete a positioning voyage to the loading port before cargo voyages begin, and it actually reaches that port, the carrier is separately owed a proportional "distance compensation" for that leg under the same Art. 1210 formula.

## Practical Takeaway

For parties structuring or exiting a freight contract, the financial stakes of termination timing are significant — pre-voyage termination is essentially cost-free, while post-voyage termination can trigger a real distance-freight bill. Reported Turkish case law on these exact mechanics remains limited, with disputes usually resolved on procedural grounds instead.
