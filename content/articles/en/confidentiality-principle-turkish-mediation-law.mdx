*Article 4 – (1) Unless otherwise agreed by the parties, the mediator is obliged to keep confidential the information and documents submitted to them or otherwise obtained within the framework of the mediation activity, as well as all other records.*

*(2) Unless otherwise agreed, the parties and the other persons attending the sessions are likewise bound to observe this confidentiality.*

Mediation works because parties can say things there that they would never say in litigation. An employer may offer a figure at mediation that it would never concede in court; a tenant may openly acknowledge a delay at the table that they would not admit before a judge. The sole guarantee of that openness is confidentiality. The legislature has made its standing clear by regulating confidentiality not merely as a professional rule but as an offence whose breach carries imprisonment of up to six months (Art. 33).

Confidentiality has two distinct layers that are frequently confused. Article 4 imposes a **prohibition on disclosure**: information learned during mediation may not be told to outsiders. [Article 5](/articles/inadmissibility-of-mediation-statements-turkish-law) imposes a **prohibition on use**: such information may not be advanced as evidence in subsequent civil litigation or arbitration. A statement may be inadmissible under Article 5 while also having been disclosed contrary to Article 4; the two provisions operate together but separately.

## The Scope of Confidentiality as to Persons

Article 4/1 binds the mediator, while Article 4/2 binds "the parties and the other persons attending the sessions". In practice this second group is broader than assumed: the parties' lawyers, statutory representatives, the expert persons kept present during negotiations under Article 15/6, commission members representing a public administration and interpreters all fall within it. The 5th Criminal Chamber of the Court of Cassation has expressly held that information obtained during the mediation process must be kept confidential "by the parties and by the lawyers representing them" — counsel's confidentiality duty is independent of, and no lighter than, that of the client.

## Scope as to Subject Matter: "Information, Documents and Other Records"

The provision covers every kind of information, document and record "submitted to the mediator or otherwise obtained" by them. The phrase "otherwise obtained" matters: confidentiality is not limited to formally produced documents; information learned from one party in a private session (caucus), oral statements made during the sessions and the mediator's own notes are equally covered.

Private sessions also generate an additional "internal confidentiality" layer. Article 8/1 empowers the mediator to meet the parties separately; if a party does not wish information shared in a private session to be conveyed to the other side, the mediator is bound to respect that wish. While not a direct consequence of Article 4, this is a settled requirement of mediation practice and of the Turkish Mediators' Ethical Rules.

## "Unless Otherwise Agreed": Waiver of Confidentiality

Both paragraphs carry the qualification "unless otherwise agreed by the parties". Confidentiality is a protection the parties may dispose of; acting together, they can lift it. In practice this arises in three situations: where the settlement document must be produced to a third party, where the process must be reported to an institutional approval mechanism, and where the parties wish to establish a particular point by joint declaration in future litigation.

It must not be overlooked that waiver **cannot be unilateral**. One party's consent to disclosure does not render lawful the disclosure of the other party's statements. Equally, the mediator cannot lift confidentiality with the permission of only one party.

## The Limit of Confidentiality: Documents That Must Be Filed with the Court

In mediation as a condition of action, the claimant must attach to the statement of claim the original of the final minutes recording that no settlement was reached, or a copy certified by the mediator (Art. 18/A/2; for labour disputes, Art. 3/2 of Law No. 7036). This production, commanded by statute, is not a breach of Article 4; indeed Article 5/3 permits disclosure to the extent that it is "required by a provision of law".

That exception is, however, narrow and confined to the final minutes themselves. In one judgment of the 5th Criminal Chamber, a lawyer who not only attached the final minutes to the statement of claim but also wrote in it the **monetary offer** made by the employer during mediation and the opposing party's **telephone number** was held to be subject to sufficient evidence for the opening of the final investigation. The judgment draws the line clearly: producing the minutes is compulsory, disclosing the offer is prohibited.

By contrast, the 7th Criminal Chamber upheld an acquittal that had been founded on the view that submitting the minutes as an annex to a pleading did not fall within the scope of confidentiality, finding that the proceedings had been conducted in accordance with procedure and the evidence was sufficient. Read together, the two judgments yield this criterion: what is decisive is not that the document entered the case file, but whether **content going beyond the scope commanded by law** was disclosed.

## Confidentiality, Audio Recordings and Personal Data

Recording mediation sessions without the knowledge and consent of one of the parties directly conflicts with the confidentiality principle and, in addition, raises the offence of listening to and recording conversations between persons under Article 133 of the Turkish Criminal Code. A judgment of the 12th Criminal Chamber concerning the recording, without consent, of a conversation about the possibility of mediation demonstrates the criminal-law dimension of this field. In practice such recordings carry both the risk of criminal liability and the risk of being inadmissible as evidence under Article 5.

Likewise, sharing data such as the other party's telephone number, address and identity information in a pleading or with third parties where this is not required gives rise, in addition to a breach of confidentiality, to a debate on the unlawful processing of personal data.

## Sanctions for Breach

A breach of confidentiality may produce three distinct consequences. **Criminal**: under Article 33/1, a person who acts contrary to the obligation in Article 4 and thereby causes damage to a legally protected interest of another is punished with imprisonment of up to six months; investigation and prosecution depend on complaint. **Disciplinary/registry**: a mediator found not to have complied with the obligations laid down by law is given a written warning under Article 21/2 and, if the warning is not heeded, their defence is taken and removal from the registry may be requested from the Board; for lawyer-mediators the disciplinary provisions of the Attorneyship Law also apply. **Civil**: a person harmed by the breach may claim damages under the general provisions.

## Case Law

The following judgments show how the boundaries of the confidentiality obligation are drawn in practice.

**1. Court of Cassation, 5th Criminal Chamber, Case No. 2023/12472, Decision No. 2024/814, dated 25.01.2024**

The Chamber emphasised that information obtained during the mediation process must be kept confidential by the parties and by the lawyers representing them. In the case at hand the accused, a lawyer, expressly wrote in the statement of claim the offer made by the employer side during the mediation stage and the complainant's telephone number. According to the judgment, although attaching the final minutes to the statement of claim is a condition of action, disclosing an offer made within the scope of mediation is prohibited and cannot be assessed within that exception; the existing evidence was sufficient for the opening of the final investigation.

**2. Court of Cassation, 7th Criminal Chamber, Case No. 2024/6804, Decision No. 2026/7678, dated 29.06.2026**

The file concerned a public prosecution for the offence of breach of confidentiality under Article 33/1 of Law No. 6325. The first-instance court acquitted on the assessment that the minutes annexed to the statement of defence did not fall within the scope of confidentiality, and the appeal on the merits was dismissed. After establishing that the decision was subject to cassation under the final paragraph of Article 59 of the Attorneyship Law, the Chamber concluded that there was no unlawfulness in the judgment and upheld it.

**3. Court of Cassation, 12th Criminal Chamber, Case No. 2020/1083, Decision No. 2023/769, dated 13.03.2023**

The decision concerns the recording by one party, without the knowledge and consent of the other, of a conversation about the possibility of mediation between them. The dispute is significant in revealing the criminal-law dimension of unauthorised recording of discussions in a mediation context.

**4. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2022/7271, Decision No. 2022/8056, dated 20.06.2022**

In this dispute the mediator refused a request to include a third company in the process on the grounds that the applicant's counsel had not consented and that the request would constitute a breach of confidentiality, whereupon the invalidity of the final minutes was asserted. The local court dismissed the action, holding that there is no compulsory joinder among employers in employee-claim disputes, that the employee may go to mediation against whomever they choose, and that the mediator is under no obligation to accept a request to add a party.

**5. Council of State, 10th Chamber, Case No. 2018/3449, Decision No. 2021/1714, dated 08.04.2021**

Within the framework of an action for annulment of the Regulation on the Law on Mediation in Civil Disputes, the decision addresses the principles of confidentiality (Art. 4), inadmissibility of statements and documents (Art. 5) and meeting with the parties (Art. 8) as a whole, and sets out the statutory framework of the confidentiality regime.
