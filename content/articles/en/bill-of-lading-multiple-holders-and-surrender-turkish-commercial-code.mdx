A negotiable bill of lading can be issued in several original copies, all representing the same cargo. TTK Art. 1235-1236 resolve the priority problem this creates and close the loop on how cargo is actually released.

## Priority Among Competing Holders

Under Art. 1235(1), once one holder has already validly collected the cargo from the captain against one original, a different holder still carrying another original cannot claim the delivery-equivalent effect of Art. 1234 against that person — actual delivery exhausts the representative function of the remaining copies for that cargo. Art. 1235(2) addresses the scenario where the captain has not yet delivered and multiple holders assert conflicting claims: priority goes to whichever holder received the *first* endorsement and delivery from the common endorser (typically the exporter or its agent). Where a copy was sent onward by post or courier rather than handed over in person, the dispatch date counts as the delivery date to that holder — a practical evidentiary rule, since proving the exact moment a distant bank or buyer physically received a document is often impossible.

## Surrender Against a Delivery Endorsement

Art. 1236 closes the system with a simple but essential mechanical rule: cargo may only be released against surrender of the bill of lading copy bearing a notation that the cargo has been received. This serves two functions — it creates a permanent record of what was delivered to whom, and it prevents the same copy from being used a second time, whether by mistake or by a bad-faith third party, to claim cargo that has already gone out.

## Why This Matters

For carriers, banks, and buyers dealing in bills issued in multiple originals, these rules determine who wins when paper claims collide. A bank financing a shipment against one original should understand that a rival original endorsed and handed over earlier in the chain will defeat its claim to the goods, regardless of which copy it happens to hold. Carriers, in turn, protect themselves by insisting on the notation-and-surrender procedure for every release — it is their best evidence against a later priority dispute.

Turkish courts have not yet published decisions interpreting Art. 1235-1236 by number; multi-original priority disputes appear to be resolved in banking and shipping document-control practice well before reaching litigation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where a bill of lading is issued in several originals, which one prevails?

The one under which delivery was actually taken. Under Article 1235/1 TTK, where an order bill of lading has been issued in several originals, <b>the holder of one original cannot assert against a person who has already taken the goods from the master under Article 1230 on the strength of another original — before that holder itself demanded delivery — the consequences attached to surrender of the bill by Article 1234</b>. Priority therefore lies with the holder who acted first and took the goods.

### Is the bill of lading taken back when the goods are delivered?

Yes, necessarily. Under Article 1236/1 TTK, <b>the goods are delivered only against return of the original of the bill of lading, endorsed to acknowledge that the goods have been received</b>. This is the maritime application of the general principle of negotiable instruments — performance against surrender of the instrument (Article 646 TTK). Returning the instrument without the endorsement, or delivering without it, exposes the carrier to the risk of paying twice.

### May cargo be delivered without the bill of lading?

In law, no. Under Article 1228/1 TTK the carrier is bound to deliver the goods <b>only against presentation of the bill of lading</b>, and under Article 1236 delivery depends on return of the endorsed original. Delivery against a letter of indemnity, used in practice when documents are delayed, does not release the carrier from that statutory duty: if the true holder appears, the carrier remains liable to them.
