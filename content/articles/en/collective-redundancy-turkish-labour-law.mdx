When a business restructures, downsizes a production line, or exits a market in Turkey, dismissing a large group of employees at once triggers a separate legal regime from an ordinary individual termination. Article 29 of Labour Code No. 4857 subjects "collective redundancy" to its own procedural framework: numeric thresholds that decide whether a wave of dismissals counts as collective at all, an advance notice and consultation duty toward the union and the state employment authorities, and a distinct set of consequences if the employer gets the procedure wrong. For foreign investors and HR teams managing a Turkish workforce, understanding where this procedure ends and the ordinary test for a valid dismissal begins is essential, because the two are frequently — and incorrectly — treated as the same question.

## When Does a Round of Dismissals Become a "Collective Redundancy"?

Article 29 applies only when two conditions are met together. First, the dismissals must be driven by economic, technological, structural or similar requirements of the business, the workplace or the work itself — in other words, an operational cause rather than reasons tied to individual employees' conduct or performance. Second, the number of employees affected within a one-month period must cross a headcount-linked threshold: in a workplace with 20 to 100 employees, at least 10 dismissals; with 101 to 300 employees, at least 10 percent of the workforce; and with more than 300 employees, at least 30 dismissals. These dismissals can occur on the same date or on different dates within that one-month window and still be aggregated for the purpose of the threshold.

A practical risk for employers is trying to stay under the threshold by spreading terminations out. Turkish courts have consistently looked at the substance of a series of dismissals rather than their formal timing, and treat an employer's attempt to break up what is, in reality, a single collective redundancy into smaller batches as an evasion of Article 29 — with the consequence that the full collective procedure is deemed to apply regardless of how the terminations were staged.

## The Notification and Union Consultation Duty

Once a collective redundancy is contemplated, the employer must notify — in writing and at least 30 days in advance — the workplace union representatives, the relevant regional labour directorate, and the Turkish Employment Agency (İŞKUR). This notice is not a bare announcement: it must set out the reasons for the redundancy, the number and categories of employees who will be affected, and the timeframe over which the terminations will take place.

The notice opens a consultation phase rather than closing the matter. The employer and the union representatives are required to discuss ways to avoid the redundancy altogether, reduce the number of employees affected, or mitigate its adverse effects on those who are let go, and a written record confirming that this consultation took place must be produced. Substantively, the actual termination notices to the affected employees only become effective 30 days after the employer's notification reaches the regional labour directorate — a waiting period designed to give the authorities and the union a genuine window to intervene before dismissals take legal effect.

## Breaching the Procedure Does Not, By Itself, Invalidate the Dismissal

A point that is often misunderstood in practice is what happens when the employer skips or botches the notification and consultation steps. The settled position of the Turkish Court of Cassation treats the Article 29 procedure as analytically separate from the question of whether the dismissal rests on valid grounds under the job-security provisions of the Labour Code. Failing to give the required notice or to hold consultations does not automatically render the terminations invalid; the sanction the law attaches to a procedural breach is an administrative fine under Article 100, not reinstatement.

In the same vein, the general requirement of a separate written notice of termination to each employee, which normally applies to individual dismissals, is not additionally imposed in the collective redundancy context — the law instead relies on the 30-day period running from notification to the regional directorate as the trigger for the dismissals taking effect. That said, the closing provision of Article 29 makes clear that the collective redundancy rules may not be used to sidestep the job-security protections found in Articles 18 to 21. Where the numeric or causal conditions for a genuine collective redundancy are not actually present, an affected employee can still have the dismissal reviewed under the ordinary rules on valid cause for termination.

## Collective Redundancy Still Needs a Valid, Proportionate Business Reason

Complying with the notice and consultation formalities does not, on its own, make a collective redundancy lawful. Courts additionally examine whether the underlying dismissals rest on a genuine operational decision and a valid reason in the sense used for individual job-security cases, whether the employer applied that decision consistently rather than selectively, whether the employer acted without arbitrariness, and — critically — whether the dismissal was truly unavoidable, in line with the principle that termination must be a last resort. An employer that had a realistic alternative short of dismissal, such as placing staff on unpaid leave, but proceeded directly to collective redundancy instead, can be found to have breached the last-resort principle — and in that case the dismissals may still be held invalid even though the Article 29 notice and consultation steps were followed to the letter. This dual-layer scrutiny shows that Article 29 is not merely a formal notice provision; the substantive validity of a collective redundancy remains fully open to judicial review.

## Full Workplace Closure and the Re-Hiring Preference

Where a workplace shuts down entirely, permanently and definitively, the employer is relieved of the detailed notification-content and consultation duties described above. The obligation is narrower: notify the regional labour directorate and İŞKUR at least 30 days beforehand and post the closure at the workplace. In exchange for this lighter procedure, the law imposes a compensating safeguard — if the employer wishes to hire again for the same type of work within six months of the collective redundancy becoming final, it must give preference to re-engaging the qualified former employees who were let go. This preferential re-hiring duty exists precisely to prevent the closure route from being used as a way to quietly replace a workforce. Separately, the collective redundancy rules do not apply at all to dismissals of employees in seasonal or campaign-based work, where the termination follows from the inherent nature of that kind of work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does every large layoff in Turkey count as a "collective redundancy" under Article 29?**
No. Two things must both be present: the dismissals must be based on economic, technological or structural operational grounds, and the number of employees affected within one month must reach the applicable headcount threshold (10 in workplaces of 20–100 employees, 10 percent in workplaces of 101–300 employees, or 30 in workplaces above 300 employees). Terminations for individual performance or conduct reasons do not fall under this regime even if several happen at once.

**If we forget to notify the union or the regional directorate, are the dismissals automatically void?**
No. Turkish case law treats a breach of the Article 29 notice and consultation duties as separate from the validity of the dismissal itself; the primary consequence of a procedural breach is an administrative fine, not reinstatement. However, the dismissals can still be challenged on other grounds, including whether they truly rested on a valid operational reason.

**Can we avoid the collective redundancy procedure by spreading dismissals out over several weeks?**
Not reliably. Where dismissals that are, in substance, part of a single restructuring are staged in smaller batches to stay under the numeric threshold, this can be treated as an evasion of Article 29, and the full collective procedure — and its scrutiny — may still be applied.

**We closed the workplace completely — do we still owe anything to the dismissed employees?**
Yes, potentially. Even after a full and permanent closure, if you want to hire for the same type of work again within six months, you are required to give preference to qualified employees from the group that was let go, ahead of hiring externally.
