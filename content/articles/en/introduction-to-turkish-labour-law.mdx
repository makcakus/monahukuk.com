Foreign employers setting up in Turkey often assume that a single, uniform labour statute governs every working relationship. In practice, Turkish Labour Code No. 4857 is deliberately narrower than that: it protects most employment relationships, but it carves out a defined list of exceptions, and knowing which side of that line a given role falls on changes almost everything — from severance entitlement to which court hears a dispute. This article explains what the Labour Code is designed to do, who it covers, which relationships it deliberately leaves out, and what happens to those excluded relationships instead.

## The Purpose of the Labour Code

Turkish labour law exists because the ordinary rules of contract law assume two parties bargaining on roughly equal footing — an assumption that breaks down in an employment relationship, where the employer typically holds the stronger economic position. Article 1 of Labour Code No. 4857 states the Code's purpose in exactly these terms: to regulate the rights and obligations of employers and employees who work under an employment contract, covering both their working conditions and their working environment.

That phrasing matters. The Code is not limited to economic terms such as wages and working hours; it extends to the working environment as a whole, including occupational health and safety. Because of this protective purpose, most of the Code's substantive rules are what Turkish legal doctrine calls "relatively mandatory": an employment contract cannot lawfully set terms below the floor the Code establishes, but it is free to grant the employee more favourable terms. This is the doctrinal backbone of Turkish labour law and explains why individual employment contracts in Turkey tend to track statutory minimums closely rather than depart from them.

## Who and What the Code Covers

Article 1 also sets out the Code's general scope: subject to the exceptions listed in Article 4, the Code applies to every workplace, and to every employer, employer's representative, and employee, regardless of the type of business activity involved. The reference to "regardless of business activity" is a deliberate design choice — coverage does not turn on the employer's sector or industry, but on the nature of the relationship between the parties: is there an employment contract, and is the work performed under the employer's subordination and direction. Where those two elements are present, the Code applies by operation of law.

This last point deserves emphasis because it surprises some foreign investors: registering a workplace with the Ministry of Labour and Social Security is an administrative formality, not a precondition for coverage. Whether or not that notification has been filed, the workplace, the employer, and the employees are bound by the Code's provisions from the moment the underlying conditions are met. Coverage is automatic; it does not depend on paperwork. The Code's own definitions of "employee," "employer" and "workplace" — found in Article 2 — are what ultimately settle borderline cases about who counts as covered.

## The Article 4 Exceptions: What Falls Outside the Labour Code

Article 4 lists a defined set of work and working relationships that Labour Code No. 4857 does not govern. Broadly, these exceptions fall into two categories: relationships where the ordinary protective mechanisms of the Code — job security, severance pay, statutory working-hour limits — are difficult to apply given the nature of the work, and relationships that are already regulated by a dedicated separate statute, such as the Maritime Labour Code or the Press Labour Code.

The main categories excluded under Article 4 include: sea and air transport work; agricultural and forestry workplaces employing 50 or fewer workers; construction work connected to agriculture that stays within the scope of a family's own economic activity; work carried out in private homes and in handicrafts among family members and relatives up to the third degree, without outside participation; domestic services; apprentices; athletes; persons undergoing rehabilitation; and small workplaces employing three or fewer people within the meaning of the (now repealed) Tradesmen and Craftsmen Code No. 507.

Turkish courts have consistently read these exceptions narrowly rather than broadly. In the agricultural context, for instance, the exception is understood to cover only work directly tied to plant and animal production, care and cultivation — administrative work, marketing, or processing carried out on the same agricultural enterprise is treated as ordinary labour falling back under the Code. Similarly for domestic service, the dividing line courts apply is whether the worker serves the household's personal needs (cleaning, cooking, childcare) as opposed to work connected to the employer's own commercial activity, which pulls the relationship back into the Code's scope. The practical lesson for employers is that job titles and superficial descriptions do not control; what the worker actually does, and for what purpose, does.

## What Happens to Relationships the Code Excludes

Falling outside Labour Code coverage does not leave a worker without any legal protection. Relationships excluded under Article 4 are instead governed by the general service-contract provisions of the Turkish Code of Obligations No. 6098 (from Article 393 onward). That shift has real practical consequences: workers under the Code of Obligations regime cannot rely on the Labour Code's job-security remedies (such as a reinstatement claim), statutory severance pay, or collective bargaining protections. In exchange, the Code of Obligations' general contract rules and its provisions on compensation for unjust termination apply instead.

This distinction also determines which court has jurisdiction. Disputes falling under the Labour Code are heard in labour courts, while disputes arising from a service relationship governed by the Code of Obligations go to the general civil courts of first instance. Correctly identifying whether a relationship falls under Article 4 is therefore not just a question of which substantive rights apply — it also decides where a claim must be filed and, in effect, what procedure it follows.

## The Exception to the Exception

Article 4's second paragraph adds a further layer: it pulls certain activities back under the Labour Code even though, on their face, they would appear to fall within one of the excluded categories in the first paragraph. Loading and unloading work between ships and shore at coasts, ports and piers; work at all onshore aviation facilities; work in workshops and factories that manufacture agricultural vehicles and machinery; construction work within agricultural enterprises; park and garden work open to public use; and aquaculture work not covered by the Maritime Labour Code are all treated as falling back under Labour Code No. 4857, despite outwardly resembling maritime or agricultural activity.

The takeaway for employers is that the exception system in Article 4 is not a mechanical, activity-based checklist. It is built around the actual character of the work being performed, and a single business — an agricultural enterprise that also runs a workshop, for example — can have some roles inside the Code's coverage and others outside it at the same time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does registering a workplace with the authorities determine whether the Labour Code applies?**
No. Coverage under the Labour Code is automatic once an employment contract and a subordinate work relationship exist; it does not depend on whether the workplace has been formally notified to the Ministry of Labour and Social Security.

**If my business is agricultural, are all of my staff automatically outside the Labour Code?**
Not necessarily. The agricultural exception is interpreted narrowly to cover only work directly related to production, care and cultivation. Administrative, marketing, or processing roles within the same agricultural enterprise typically remain subject to the Code.

**If a worker falls under one of the Article 4 exceptions, do they have no legal protection at all?**
They still have protection, but under a different legal regime — the general service-contract rules of the Turkish Code of Obligations rather than the Labour Code. This means no statutory severance pay or job-security remedy, but the Code of Obligations' general contract and unjust-termination rules still apply.

**Why does it matter which regime applies, beyond the substantive rights involved?**
It also determines jurisdiction. Labour Code disputes go to labour courts; disputes under the Code of Obligations service-contract regime go to the general civil courts. Getting the classification wrong can mean filing in the wrong court entirely.
