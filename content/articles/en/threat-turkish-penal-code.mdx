Most criminal offenses require an actual harm to materialize before liability attaches. Threat works differently: under Article 106 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), the offense is complete the moment a threatening statement reaches its target, regardless of whether the announced harm is ever carried out. What is punished is not a future event but the present disruption of another person's peace of mind and freedom to decide and act without fear. Because the underlying evil is, by definition, still hypothetical, Turkish courts and practitioners spend most of their attention on a single recurring question: was the statement, in its actual context, genuinely capable of producing fear in the person who heard it? This article explains how Article 106 is structured, what the courts look for when assessing seriousness, and how the offense interacts with related crimes such as blackmail and coercion.

## The Interest Protected: Peace of Mind and Freedom of Decision

Article 106 sits among the offenses against personal liberty, and that placement is deliberate. What the law protects is not physical safety as such — that is covered by other provisions — but a person's inner peace and their freedom to make decisions and act without being pressured by fear of an announced evil. A person who is made to live under constant apprehension about what might happen to them will, sooner or later, start shaping their choices — where they go, what job they keep, who they associate with — around that fear rather than around their own free will. For this reason, threat is classified as an offense of endangerment rather than an offense of actual harm: prosecutors do not need to prove that the victim was in fact frightened. It is enough that the statement was, viewed objectively, capable of instilling fear in a reasonable person in the victim's position.

## Two Tiers of Threatened Harm

Article 106 distinguishes between two categories of announced evil, each carrying a different penalty and a different procedural regime. The first and more serious tier covers threats to attack the victim's own life, physical integrity, or sexual inviolability, or that of someone close to the victim. This tier carries imprisonment of six months to two years, and where the victim is a woman the minimum sentence cannot be less than nine months. The second, lighter tier covers threats of substantial damage to a person's property or of some other unspecified "evil." Here the penalty drops to imprisonment of two to six months or a judicial fine, and — critically — prosecution proceeds only on the victim's own complaint.

The open-ended phrase covering an unspecified "other evil" is intentionally broad, but it is not limitless. Whatever evil is threatened must be both serious and unlawful in nature. Announcing that one intends to exercise a right one is legally entitled to exercise — for instance, warning a debtor that enforcement proceedings will be initiated, or that a criminal complaint will be filed — does not, as a rule, amount to a threat. The unlawfulness element is precisely what is missing in such cases: the law itself sanctions the announced conduct, so there is no wrongful pressure being applied.

## The Suitability and Seriousness Test

For a statement to be punishable as a threat, it must be shown that, given the concrete circumstances, it was genuinely capable of generating fear in the victim. This is not a dictionary-level reading of the words used. Courts weigh the relationship between the parties, the setting in which the statement was made, whether the speaker had any realistic capacity to carry out what was said, and the tone and manner used. Statements that describe something physically impossible, purely fanciful, or plainly exaggerated will not meet this threshold.

At the same time, requiring genuine seriousness does not mean every angry outburst escapes liability. In practice, courts draw a careful line between coarse language exchanged in the heat of an argument — which does not create real fear — and statements that, in context, exert a genuine pressure capable of restricting the listener's freedom of action. The threat also does not need to be explicit or direct: an implied threat, conveyed through insinuation or through conduct rather than words, can still satisfy the offense, provided the recipient could reasonably understand that some form of harm was being communicated to them.

## Who Can Commit the Offense and Who Counts as a Victim

Threat is not a specialized offense limited to a particular category of offender — anyone can commit it. The victim, however, must be a specific or at least identifiable natural person. One notable feature of Article 106 is that the threatened harm does not need to be directed at the listener personally: a statement announcing an attack on someone close to the victim — a family member, for example — is still sufficient to constitute the offense against that victim, because a person's peace of mind is disturbed not only by threats to their own safety but also by threats to people they care about. The conduct can be committed verbally, in writing, by telephone or electronic communication, or through a gesture with an unambiguous meaning; the offense is complete once the threatening statement reaches the person it is aimed at.

## Intent Requirement

Threat can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent version of the offense. It suffices that the perpetrator knowingly and willingly makes a statement capable of instilling fear in the listener. The law does not require any particular motive or purpose, so the reason behind the statement does not, on its own, determine whether the offense exists. That said, whether the words were spoken with a genuine intention to intimidate, as opposed to being a mere venting of anger, remains highly relevant to how a court assesses intent on the facts of a given case.

## Distinguishing Threat From Neighboring Offenses

Threat is frequently confused with related offenses that share surface similarities. Insult targets a person's honor and dignity in the present, whereas threat works by announcing a future evil in order to generate fear. Blackmail, regulated separately under Article 107 of the Penal Code, is a specific variant in which a threat is used as leverage to obtain a benefit or to force someone to do or refrain from doing something. Coercion, under Article 108, involves the actual, present-tense use of force, while threat by contrast defers the harm to the future. Where a threat operates merely as an element of, or an aggravating factor for, another offense — robbery being the clearest example — no separate conviction for threat is entered on top of the other offense.

## Aggravated Forms

The second paragraph of Article 106 increases the penalty where the threat is made in a way that heightens its intimidating effect or allows the perpetrator to conceal their identity, leaving the victim more exposed. These aggravated circumstances include threats made with a weapon; threats made while disguising one's identity, through an anonymous letter, or through special signs; threats made jointly by more than one person; and threats made by drawing on the intimidating power of an existing or merely presumed criminal organization. In these situations, the penalty rises to imprisonment of two to seven years. Notably, where the aggravating factor is the intimidating power of a criminal organization, the organization does not actually need to exist — it is enough that the victim was made to believe such a power was behind the threat.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence

Because threat is what Turkish doctrine calls a "mere conduct" offense, it is generally considered complete the moment the statement reaches its target, leaving little room for an attempted version of the crime. An attempt becomes conceptually possible only where the course of conduct can be split into distinct stages — for example, where a threatening letter is sent but never actually reaches the addressee. Ordinary rules on participation apply without restriction, and the involvement of more than one person acting jointly is treated not merely as participation but as a distinct aggravating circumstance in its own right.

On concurrence of offenses, the third paragraph of Article 106 sets out a special rule: where intentional killing, intentional injury, or damage to property is actually carried out in order to make good on the threat, a separate conviction is entered for that additional offense on top of the threat conviction. Where the same victim is threatened repeatedly at different times pursuant to a single ongoing criminal design, the repeated conduct is assessed under the rules on continuing offenses rather than being charged as separate, unrelated crimes.

## Penalties, Complaint Requirements and Mediation

The complaint regime is not uniform across the whole of Article 106. Threats involving substantial property damage or another unspecified evil are prosecuted only upon the victim's complaint. By contrast, threats to life, physical integrity or sexual inviolability, along with all of the aggravated forms under the second paragraph, are prosecuted automatically by the public prosecutor without requiring a complaint. Threats falling under the first tier are, under the Turkish Code of Criminal Procedure, eligible for mediation: where mediation succeeds, the case results in a decision not to prosecute or is otherwise discontinued. The aggravated forms are not eligible for mediation. Given the applicable penalty ranges, these cases are tried before the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the threatened harm actually need to happen for the offense to be committed?**
No. The offense is complete once the threatening statement reaches the victim and is objectively capable of creating fear. Whether the announced harm is ever carried out is irrelevant to liability, although actually carrying it out can trigger a separate, additional conviction under the third paragraph of Article 106.

**Is telling someone I will sue them or start enforcement proceedings a criminal threat?**
As a rule, no. Announcing an intention to exercise a right the law itself allows — such as filing a lawsuit or a criminal complaint, or pursuing a debt through enforcement proceedings — lacks the unlawfulness element required for a threat, provided the right is genuinely held and not being used as a cover for other pressure.

**Can a threat be prosecuted without the victim filing a complaint?**
It depends on which tier of Article 106 applies. Threats to life, physical integrity or sexual inviolability, and all aggravated forms under the second paragraph, are prosecuted automatically. Threats involving property damage or another unspecified evil require the victim's own complaint.

**Does an implied or indirect threat count, or must the statement be explicit?**
An implied threat can be sufficient. The statement does not need to be direct or explicit; what matters is whether, in context, the recipient could reasonably understand that a specific evil was being communicated to them.
