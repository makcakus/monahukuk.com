Two offences under the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) are frequently confused because both sit in the chapter protecting the integrity of public administration, yet they describe very different conduct and involve very different actors. Influence peddling, regulated in Article 255, criminalizes a person — who need not be a public official at all — obtaining a benefit by claiming to have sway over a public official and offering to use that sway to get an unlawful matter handled. Exceeding the limits of the authority to use force, regulated in Article 256, criminalizes a public official who is lawfully entitled to use force — typically a law enforcement officer — but who applies more force than the task actually required. This article explains how each offence is structured under Turkish law, who can commit it, and why the two are treated so differently at sentencing.

## The Interest Each Offence Protects

Although both provisions appear under the same heading of offences against the reliability and proper functioning of public administration, they safeguard different interests. Influence peddling protects public confidence in the impartial and merit-based operation of public services — regardless of whether the public official named by the offender was in fact influenced or even aware of what was going on. The mere claim of pull over a public office, made in exchange for a benefit, is treated as damaging to that confidence in itself. Exceeding the limits of the use of force protects two things simultaneously: the physical integrity of the person subjected to the force, and the principle that state power must be exercised proportionately and within legal bounds. When an officer equipped with coercive powers stretches those powers beyond what the situation calls for, both the individual victim and the broader trust underlying the relationship between the state and the citizen are affected.

## Influence Peddling: Profiting From the Appearance of Pull

The defining feature of Article 255 is that its perpetrator does not need to be a public official. The person who commits the offence is anyone who — directly or through intermediaries — claims influence over a public official and, on the strength of that claim, obtains a benefit for themselves or for someone else in exchange for undertaking to get an unlawful matter handled. Turkish law does not require the claimed influence to be genuine: it is enough that the offender creates the impression of having such influence and monetizes that impression. This is precisely what separates influence peddling from bribery (Article 252) and from extortion by a public official (Article 250): in bribery, the public official is a direct party to the illicit bargain, whereas in influence peddling the offender is a third party standing between the person seeking the favor and the public official, who may well be entirely unaware that their name is being used.

The statute draws a further distinction between the two sides of the transaction. Someone who obtains a benefit by offering to exploit alleged influence, or who acts as an intermediary in securing that benefit, faces a heavier sentencing range than someone who simply pays for the promised favor or in the expectation of receiving it — a "seller" versus "buyer" distinction, in effect. Where the person making the claim of influence happens to actually be a public official, the applicable prison sentence is increased, reflecting the view that a public office lends particular credibility — and therefore particular gravity — to a false or exaggerated claim of influence.

## Exceeding the Authority to Use Force: A Departure From Otherwise Lawful Conduct

Article 256 addresses a public official who, unlike the offender in influence peddling, does possess a genuine legal authority — the power to use force in the course of official duties, such as during an arrest, a crowd-control operation, or another law enforcement intervention. What distinguishes this offence from ordinary intentional injury is precisely that the official began acting under a lawful power. Using the degree of force that a given task reasonably requires is lawful; the offence arises only from the point at which the official uses force against a person beyond what the task required. Rather than fixing an independent penalty, the legislator opted for a direct referral technique, providing that the sentencing provisions for intentional injury apply to the disproportionate excess. In practice, this means the actual sentence handed down depends on the severity of the harm caused, assessed under the general injury provisions. The starting point of lawful authority nevertheless remains a distinct element that shapes how the conduct is legally characterized, distinguishing it from a bare case of intentional injury and requiring an assessment of just how disproportionate the force used actually was.

## The Mental Element Required for Each Offence

Both offences require direct intent; neither can be committed negligently. In influence peddling, the offender must knowingly create the impression of having influence over a public official and must obtain or secure the benefit precisely for that purpose. In the excess-of-force offence, intent attaches to the officer's awareness that the force applied went beyond what the duty required; believing, at the outset, that one is acting within a legitimate power does not remove intent from the picture — it simply supplies the special legal backdrop that separates this offence from ordinary intentional injury.

## Aggravating Circumstances, Complicity and Attempt

Influence peddling is drafted so broadly that conduct which would normally only qualify as an attempt is instead punished as if the offence were complete: reaching a mere agreement on the benefit to be provided is treated as a completed offence. Conversely, where a benefit is demanded but refused, or offered/promised but not accepted, the sentence is reduced by half — a bespoke reduction mechanism that effectively substitutes for the ordinary rules on attempt. Anyone who acts as an intermediary in the scheme is treated as a joint principal and faces the same sentencing range as the primary offender; the same joint-principal treatment extends to any officer of a third-party individual or legal entity who accepts a benefit that indirectly flows to that third party through the influence-peddling relationship — a special extension of the ordinary rules on complicity found elsewhere in the Code. Where the attempt to get the matter handled itself amounts to a separate, independent offence, the offender is prosecuted for that offence as well, under the ordinary rules on the concurrence of offences. The article's reach also extends to influence peddling carried out over foreign public officials and staff of international organizations who fall within the scope of the bribery provisions; such cases are prosecuted and investigated automatically whenever the persons concerned are present in Turkey, regardless of their nationality. Exceeding the authority to use force does not carry a comparable set of aggravating variants of its own — the applicable sentence instead tracks the aggravating factors already built into the intentional injury provisions it refers to, such as permanent disfigurement, loss of a bodily function, or the existence of a family relationship between offender and victim.

## Prosecution, Jurisdiction and Procedure

Neither offence is subject to a complaint requirement, since both concern the reliability of public administration as a whole; investigation and prosecution proceed automatically, on the authorities' own initiative. For influence peddling, depending on the statutory maximum applicable to the underlying conduct, jurisdiction may lie with either the criminal court of first instance or the heavy penal court, and the offence's extension to foreign public officials gives it an unusually broad international reach. For exceeding the limits of the use of force, the competent court follows from whichever intentional-injury provision is referenced given the severity of harm caused. In practice, what unites the two offences is a shared concern: preventing state power and the image of public office from being converted into tools of private gain or arbitrary coercion. That concern continues to drive how Turkish courts interpret both provisions, with proportionality and public trust remaining the guiding criteria.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a private individual with no real connections still be convicted of influence peddling?**
Yes. Turkish law does not require the claimed influence to be genuine. What matters is that the offender creates the impression of having sway over a public official and obtains a benefit on the strength of that claim; whether the influence actually exists is not the deciding factor for whether the offence is made out, although its absence can affect how the conduct is ultimately classified.

**How is exceeding the use-of-force limit different from an ordinary assault by a police officer?**
The key difference is the starting point. An officer who begins an intervention under a genuine legal authority to use force, but who then applies more force than the task required, is prosecuted under Article 256 by reference to the intentional injury provisions. An officer acting without any such authority from the outset is simply prosecuted for intentional injury on ordinary terms, without the benefit of that special legal framework.

**Is merely agreeing to pay for influence already a completed crime, even if no money changes hands yet?**
Yes. Under Article 255, reaching an agreement on the benefit to be provided is treated as a completed offence in itself. If a benefit is instead only demanded, offered, or promised without being accepted, the sentence for the completed offence is reduced by half rather than the case being treated as a mere attempt.

**Does it matter whether the public official named in an influence-peddling scheme knew anything about it?**
No. Because the offence protects public confidence in the impartial operation of public administration rather than the official's own conduct, the named official's awareness — or complete ignorance — of the scheme does not prevent the offence from being committed by the person making the claim.
