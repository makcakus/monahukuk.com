Foreign companies bidding on Turkish public contracts, and international counsel advising them, often assume that irregularities in a state tender are purely an administrative matter to be resolved through objections under the Public Procurement Law. In Turkey, however, certain forms of tender manipulation are also crimes in their own right, prosecuted independently of whatever the contracting authority decides to do about the tender itself. Article 235 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) criminalizes bid rigging in public procurement, and the neighboring Article 236 extends criminal liability into the contract-performance stage once the tender has been won. Understanding how these two offenses are defined, how they differ from ordinary contractual disputes, and how they interact with the administrative procurement process is essential for any business or lawyer dealing with a Turkish public tender that has gone wrong.

## The Interest Protected and Where This Offense Sits in the Penal Code

Articles 235 through 237 of the Turkish Penal Code form part of the chapter on offenses against the economy, industry and trade. Article 235 and Article 236 are functionally tied to Law No. 4734, the Public Procurement Law, but the criminal provisions operate independently of that administrative framework: their purpose is to protect the integrity of the tender and contracting process itself, the equal competitive footing of participants, and the state's financial interests, regardless of what happens administratively to the underlying tender. Article 237, discussed briefly below for context, addresses a related but distinct concern — manipulation of market prices generally, not limited to public procurement. What unites the three provisions is that each punishes deception or coercion that distorts an economic process from within.

## What Counts as Bid Rigging Under Article 235

Article 235 does not require any particular relationship between the offender and the tender — the person committing the offense can be a bidder, a third party assisting a bidder, or an official administering the tender on the state's side. The victim is the contracting public entity and, indirectly, the public interest in fair procurement; the fifth paragraph of the article extends this protection beyond core public institutions to professional bodies with public-institution status, companies formed with their participation, foundations operating within such companies, and public-benefit associations and cooperatives.

The law defines bid rigging through a closed list of alternative acts, any one of which is sufficient to constitute the offense:

- Using deceptive conduct to block a qualified bidder from participating in the tender or in the tender process, or conversely, enabling an unqualified party to participate
- Deceptively disqualifying a bid that in fact meets the tender specifications, or accepting a bid that does not meet them as if it did
- Disclosing confidential bid-related information — information that tender rules or specifications require to be kept secret — to unauthorized parties
- Using force, threats, or other unlawful means to prevent a qualified bidder from participating
- Bidders reaching an open or secret agreement among themselves intended to influence the tender's terms, and in particular its pricing

This list is exhaustive, but the common thread running through it is deception, or force and threats used as an alternative to deception. Critically, the offense does not require that the public authority actually suffer a loss. The deceptive or coercive act itself is punished, independent of whether it produces measurable financial harm — which is why this offense is classified as one of abstract danger rather than one requiring a concrete result. The law's own structure confirms this: where no damage to the relevant public body actually materializes, a reduced penalty applies rather than an acquittal, showing that the absence of loss affects only sentencing, not whether the core offense has occurred.

Because deception is baked into the definition of every alternative act, the offense can only be committed intentionally — it cannot be committed through mere negligence or carelessness. A profit motive is not a formal element of the crime, but in practice the presence of a motive is often what makes intent easier to prove.

## Why the Criminal Case Runs Independently of the Administrative Tender Process

A recurring question for foreign bidders is whether a criminal bid-rigging case depends on the tender being annulled or the resulting contract being cancelled by the administration. It does not. The prevailing view is that whether the offense has been committed is assessed independently of any decision the contracting authority makes about the tender's validity, and criminal proceedings are not bound by the administrative objection and review mechanisms available under the Public Procurement Law. A later decision by the administration to cancel the tender, or to recover the resulting loss, does not retroactively change whether the deceptive conduct met the legal definition of the offense at the time it occurred. This separation is consistent with treating the offense as one of abstract danger: what matters is the act, not its administrative aftermath.

## Article 236: Fraud During Contract Performance, and How It Differs From Article 235

Article 236 addresses a different stage of the same relationship. Where Article 235 covers the tender process itself — everything that happens before a contract is formed — Article 236 punishes deceptive conduct during the performance of an obligation already undertaken toward a public entity, that is, during the execution phase after the contract has been signed. The typical offender is the contractor or the private party to the contract, though a public official can also be implicated in the same scheme.

The conduct that triggers liability under Article 236 includes delivering or accepting goods that differ from, or fall short of, what the tender decision or contract specifies; accepting an obligation as performed when it was not performed on time; accepting completed works or materials in construction contracts despite non-compliance with the specifications; and treating a service that was performed incompletely or defectively as if it had been fully and properly rendered. The decisive qualifier is that each of these acts must be carried out deceptively. A simple breach of contract — a delay, a quality shortfall, a documentation error — remains a civil or administrative matter unless deception is present; it is the element of deception that elevates a performance failure into a crime.

This distinction matters most in practice for inspection and acceptance committees. If committee members knowingly turn a blind eye to a specification breach, their conduct can fall within Article 236. If, on the other hand, an inspection is simply negligent or careless without rising to the level of deception, the consequence is administrative responsibility, not criminal liability.

## Aggravating Circumstances, Participation, and Concurrence With Other Offenses

When bid rigging is committed through force or threats, the minimum sentence is increased, and if the force or threat itself amounts to a separately punishable offense — such as aggravated bodily harm or a qualified form of threat — the offender is punished for that offense as well, in addition to the bid-rigging conviction. Similarly, where a public official or other person obtains a benefit through the bid-rigging scheme, that person is separately punished under whatever other offense the benefit corresponds to (for example, an offense related to bribery), rather than having that conduct absorbed into the bid-rigging sentence alone. General principles of complicity apply, and because coordinated bid manipulation through agreement necessarily requires at least two participating wills, that particular form of the offense is inherently a multi-party offense by its nature, even though other forms can be committed by a single actor. Attempt is possible for the offense given its structure of alternative acts — for instance, where an attempt to pass on confidential information fails for reasons outside the offender's control — but once the deceptive conduct is completed, the offense is treated as complete without any need to show that a loss actually resulted.

## Procedural Notes

Bid rigging and performance-stage fraud are not offenses subject to private complaint; they are investigated and prosecuted by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative. This reflects the fact that what is being protected is the integrity of the public procurement system and the broader economic order, not a private interest that only the injured party can choose to pursue. Prosecution can proceed regardless of whether the relevant public institution or market has, in fact, suffered a loss.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a bid-rigging prosecution proceed even if the tender was never cancelled by the contracting authority?**
Yes. The criminal case is assessed independently of the administrative process. A decision by the contracting authority not to annul the tender, or to resolve the matter through the Public Procurement Law's own remedies, does not prevent or resolve a parallel criminal case.

**Does a foreign company need to prove the state actually lost money for a bid-rigging charge to succeed?**
No. Article 235 punishes the deceptive or coercive act itself, without requiring proof that the public entity suffered actual financial harm. The absence of demonstrated loss can reduce the applicable sentence, but it does not by itself defeat the charge.

**If a contractor delivers goods that fall short of the contract specifications, is that automatically a crime under Article 236?**
No. A performance shortfall by itself is ordinarily a contractual or administrative issue. It only becomes a criminal matter under Article 236 if the shortfall was accepted or concealed through deception — for example, if an inspection committee knowingly certified non-conforming work as compliant.

**Who can be held liable for disclosing confidential bid information?**
Anyone who discloses information that the tender rules require to be kept confidential to an unauthorized party can be liable under Article 235, regardless of whether the party who received the information ultimately participated in, or benefited from, the tender.
