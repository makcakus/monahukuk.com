Turkish criminal law contains a distinct family of offenses that protects not property or physical safety, but the actual exercise of constitutional freedoms. Articles 112 through 115 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) criminalize obstructing four separate freedoms — the right to education, access to public services, political participation, and freedom of belief and conscience — largely through the same legal mechanism: preventing someone from exercising a protected right by force, threat, or another unlawful act. Because these provisions share a common structure, they are often confused with one another in practice, even though the interest each one protects, and the conduct each one targets, differs in important respects. This article explains how the shared framework operates, what distinguishes each of the four offenses, and how aggravating circumstances and procedural rules apply across the group.

## The Shared Structure: What Counts as Obstruction

At the core of all four offenses lies the concept of "obstruction" — rendering the exercise of a protected right impossible, or making it so difficult that continuing to exercise it becomes practically unbearable. Mere inconvenience, delay, criticism, or a temporary difficulty does not rise to the level of obstruction under these provisions. The law recognizes three distinct means by which obstruction can occur: force, threat, and — the most contested category — "another unlawful act." The first two are relatively well-defined concepts in Turkish criminal law. The third catches conduct that falls short of force or threat but is nevertheless not permitted by the legal order — for example, physically blocking a school entrance, occupying space in a way that prevents access to a place of worship, or disabling a public service counter so that it cannot function. The decisive test is the unlawfulness of the conduct itself: a legitimate criticism, protest, or administrative measure does not satisfy this element merely because it happens to make exercising a right more difficult.

As a rule, these are offenses of endangerment through obstruction rather than offenses requiring actual harm: what matters is that the perpetrator's conduct actually cut off the exercise of the freedom in question, not that the victim suffered damage beyond that. None of the four offenses requires the perpetrator to hold any special status — they are general, not qualified, offenses. Where the perpetrator happens to be a public official, that fact does not create a separate crime but can trigger the aggravating regime under Article 119, discussed below.

## Four Freedoms, Four Distinct Protections

### Obstructing the right to education (Article 112)

Article 112 covers three separate forms of conduct in a single provision: obstructing any education or instruction activity that is either state-run or conducted under a permit from a public authority; obstructing a person's exercise of their own right to education; and preventing students from entering, or forcing them out of, buildings and their annexes where they reside collectively — in practice, student dormitories. This third branch is a distinctive feature of the article, since it extends protection of the right to education into the housing dimension by directly shielding student accommodation. The penalty is imprisonment from two to five years.

### Obstructing access to public services (Article 113)

This provision protects two overlapping interests at once: the uninterrupted operation of public activity, and the individual's access to the service itself. Obstructing services provided by public institutions, by professional bodies with public-institution status, or under the permit of a public authority carries imprisonment from two to five years. A second paragraph, added in 2022, increases the penalty by up to one-sixth when the obstructed service is a healthcare service — a targeted response to violence directed at healthcare workers.

### Obstructing the exercise of political rights (Article 114)

Article 114 deliberately departs from the shared pattern in its first paragraph. There, the prohibited conduct is limited to force or threat used with the specific purpose of compelling a person to join or refrain from joining a political party, to participate or not participate in party activities, to resign from a party or from a position within it, or to withdraw from candidacy for, or resign from, an elected public office. Unlike the other offenses in this group, "another unlawful act" is not an available means here, and no particular outcome needs to be shown — the offense is complete once force or threat is used for the stated purpose, regardless of whether the intended result is actually achieved. The penalty is imprisonment from one to three years. The second paragraph has a different structure: obstructing the activities of a political party through force, threat, or another unlawful act is a result-based offense, and it carries imprisonment from two to five years.

### Obstructing freedom of belief, thought and conviction (Article 115)

Article 115 addresses three distinct scenarios across three paragraphs. The first paragraph punishes using force or threat to compel a person to declare or change their religious, political, social, or philosophical beliefs, thoughts, or convictions, or to prevent them from declaring or disseminating those views; the penalty is imprisonment from one to three years. The second paragraph attaches the same penalty to obstructing the performance of religious obligations or the individual or collective practice of religious worship or rites. The third paragraph criminalizes interfering with, or forcing a change to, a person's lifestyle choices that stem from their beliefs, thoughts, or convictions. A notable asymmetry runs through the article: the first paragraph requires force or threat specifically, while the second and third paragraphs also allow "another unlawful act" as a means of commission.

## Intent: A Purpose Beyond Ordinary Knowledge and Will

All four offenses can only be committed intentionally; there is no punishable negligent form. The perpetrator must know that their conduct is obstructing the exercise of a constitutional freedom and must want that result. Beyond this general intent, Article 114(1) and Article 115(1) and (3) require something more: a specific purpose to compel the victim toward a particular political choice, to declare or change a conviction, or to change a lifestyle. Where that specific purpose cannot be proven, the conduct may still fall under general offenses such as threat or intentional bodily harm, if their conditions are otherwise met.

## Shared Aggravating Circumstances and the Treatment of Force (Article 119)

Turkish law applies a single aggravating catalogue to this entire group of offenses — obstruction of education, obstruction of activities of public institutions or professional bodies with public-institution status, obstruction of political rights, obstruction of freedom of belief and conviction, violation of the inviolability of residence, and violation of the freedom to work. Under Article 119, the penalty is increased by half again (a one-fold increase) where the offense is committed: with a weapon; by disguising one's identity, or through anonymous letters or special signs; by more than one person acting together; by drawing on the intimidating power of an actual or presumed criminal organization; or by abusing the influence conferred by a public office.

The second paragraph of Article 119 is decisive for how these cases combine with other offenses: if, during the commission of any of these crimes, the aggravated consequences of intentional bodily harm occur, the provisions on intentional bodily harm are additionally applied. Read the other way around, this confirms that the ordinary, low-level force that is simply an element of the obstruction itself is absorbed into the penalty for the obstruction offense — the same logic applies to threat used as an element of the offense, which is likewise not punished separately.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence of Offenses

For the result-based versions of these offenses — Article 112, Article 113, Article 114(2) and Article 115(2) — an attempt becomes relevant where the conduct has begun but the exercise of the freedom has not actually been cut off. By contrast, purpose-based offenses such as Article 114(1) are already complete once force or threat is used for the stated aim, so an attempt is only conceivable where the conduct itself can be meaningfully divided into stages. All ordinary forms of participation are available; however, where the offense is committed by several people acting together, this triggers the specific aggravating factor under Article 119(1)(c) rather than being analyzed solely through the general participation rules. Repeated obstruction of the same freedom over time can constitute a continuing (chain) offense, while a single act that obstructs the rights of several different people at once is analyzed under the rules on concurrence of offenses of the same kind.

## Sanctions and Procedural Framework

Given the maximum sentences involved, these cases are tried before the courts of first instance for ordinary criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi); the increase applied under Article 119 is not itself taken into account when determining jurisdiction. None of the offenses in this group is contingent on a private complaint — investigation and prosecution proceed automatically (ex officio), and a victim's withdrawal of a complaint does not terminate the case. These offenses also fall outside the categories eligible for mediation under Turkish law. In practice, the central difficulty in these cases usually lies in distinguishing conduct that is genuinely unlawful obstruction from the legitimate exercise of a right recognized by the legal order, and in establishing the perpetrator's specific purpose through concrete evidence rather than inference.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does criticizing or protesting an educational institution or a public service count as obstruction under these articles?**
Not on its own. The law requires that access to the right be rendered impossible or practically unbearable through force, threat, or another unlawful act. A legitimate protest, criticism, or administrative measure does not satisfy this element even if it incidentally makes exercising the right more difficult.

**Can a single incident of workplace or facility disruption be prosecuted under more than one of these articles at once?**
It depends on the facts. If one act obstructs the rights of multiple people, the case is generally analyzed under the rules on concurrence of offenses of the same kind rather than as separate, unrelated crimes. Which specific article applies turns on which protected freedom — education, public service, political participation, or belief — was actually obstructed.

**Is a public official who commits one of these offenses charged with a separate crime?**
No separate offense exists for public officials as such. Instead, if the public official abused the influence of their office to commit the obstruction, Article 119 increases the penalty for the underlying offense by half again, rather than creating a distinct charge.

**Can the victim end the case by withdrawing their complaint?**
No. None of these offenses is subject to private complaint; prosecution proceeds ex officio regardless of the victim's wishes, and these crimes are not included among the offenses eligible for mediation.
