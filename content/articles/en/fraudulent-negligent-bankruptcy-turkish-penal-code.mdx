When a Turkish trading company collapses into bankruptcy, creditors quickly discover that recovering their money is only half the problem — the other half is establishing why the company failed and whether anyone should answer for it criminally. Turkish criminal law addresses this through a cluster of offenses in Articles 161 through 164 of the Penal Code (Law No. 5237), which distinguish between a trader who deliberately manipulated the company's assets to escape its debts and a trader who simply managed the business with less care than the law expects of a merchant. For foreign businesses operating in Turkey, or foreign creditors of a Turkish counterparty that has gone under, understanding this distinction is essential to assessing both the exposure of company insiders and the practical value of filing a criminal complaint alongside civil bankruptcy proceedings.

## Why a Bankruptcy Ruling Has to Exist First

Unlike most criminal offenses, fraudulent and negligent bankruptcy cannot be prosecuted on the strength of the underlying conduct alone. Article 161 makes clear that the asset-stripping acts it describes are punishable only "where a bankruptcy ruling has been issued" — regardless of whether those acts occurred before or after that ruling. The same structural link applies to the negligent form of the offense in Article 162. Turkish courts and doctrine treat this bankruptcy ruling not as an element of the crime itself but as an objective precondition to punishability: the underlying conduct may already be complete, yet the criminal case cannot proceed until a commercial court has issued a bankruptcy ruling and that ruling has become final under the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law. In practice, this means a criminal court facing a bankruptcy prosecution where the underlying commercial ruling is not yet final will typically suspend the criminal case and wait for the commercial proceeding to conclude, since the very possibility of conviction depends on that outcome.

This dependency also shapes what the offense is designed to protect. The immediate interest at stake is the property rights of the creditors affected by the trader's insolvency, but the provisions also serve a broader purpose: preserving confidence in commercial credit and the reliability of the trading system as a whole. The injured party in any given case is the specific creditor or group of creditors who suffered the loss.

## The Four Ways Fraudulent Bankruptcy Can Be Committed

Article 161 does not treat "fraudulent bankruptcy" as an open-ended concept — it lists four specific categories of conduct, and Turkish legal commentary generally treats this list as exhaustive rather than illustrative. Any one of the four suffices; the prosecution does not need to prove all of them together. In substance, they are: removing, concealing or reducing the value of assets that served as security for creditors' claims; hiding or destroying commercial books, records or documents in order to prevent the discovery of such asset-stripping; creating documents that falsely inflate the debtor's liabilities by suggesting a debt relationship that does not actually exist; and misstating the company's assets as smaller than they really are through false accounting entries or a fabricated balance sheet. What unites these four scenarios is that each distorts the true picture of the trader's assets to the detriment of creditors. As a practical matter, an indictment needs to specify which of these four categories the alleged conduct falls under — a defendant cannot properly defend against a charge that never identifies the specific act relied upon.

## The Element That Separates Fraud from Mere Business Failure

What turns ordinary business failure into a criminal fraudulent bankruptcy is intent. Article 161 speaks of a person who "engages in fraudulent dispositions aimed at reducing assets," and Turkish courts read the word "fraudulent" as requiring that the trader acted with the specific awareness and will to cause loss to creditors — not merely to benefit personally, and not merely to make a poor commercial decision. Because this intent is embedded in the concept of fraud itself rather than spelled out as a separate mental element in the statute's text, proving it is inherently difficult, and in practice courts infer it from objective indicators: how close in time the disposition was to the bankruptcy, how far it departed from the company's ordinary course of business, and how systematic any tampering with records or accounts appears to be. A trader's simple pursuit of unfair personal advantage is not, by itself, enough — what matters is whether the disposition was consciously carried out in a way designed to harm creditors. Because these disputed transactions frequently surface while a postponement-of-bankruptcy request or a debt-restructuring (concordat) negotiation is underway, the criminal case is often effectively tethered to the expert findings developed in those parallel commercial files.

## Negligent Bankruptcy: When Carelessness Replaces Intent

Article 162 rests on an entirely different footing. It punishes a trader for causing their own bankruptcy through a failure to exercise "the care and diligence a trader is required to show" — there is no fraud, and no intent to harm creditors need be shown at all. The offense consists simply of falling below the objective standard of care that comes with being a merchant, where that shortfall results in bankruptcy. This distinction between deliberate manipulation and negligent mismanagement carries through directly into sentencing — three to eight years' imprisonment for fraudulent bankruptcy against two months to one year for the negligent form — and into what the prosecution must prove: in a negligent bankruptcy case, the prosecution does not need to show bad faith, only that the defendant departed from the standard of a prudent trader. This difference also drives defense strategy: a defense against fraudulent bankruptcy typically focuses on disproving intent, while a defense against negligent bankruptcy focuses on showing that the precautions actually taken met the prudent-trader standard.

## Corporate Misrepresentation Under Article 164

Article 164 protects a different interest altogether: the transparency of corporate and cooperative disclosures. It is a special-subject offense that can only be committed by a founder, partner, director, manager, representative, board member, auditor, or liquidator of the entity — an outsider cannot be a principal offender under this provision, though they may still be liable as an instigator or accomplice. The conduct consists of providing, or causing to be provided, materially false information in public statements or in reports and proposals submitted to a general assembly, where that information is capable of causing loss to those concerned. This provision has to be read alongside the financial reporting and disclosure obligations set out in the Turkish Commercial Code (Law No. 6102), since the meaning of "material information" and "falsity" is largely defined by reference to those commercial-law standards. Notably, Article 164 does not require that any loss actually materialize — it is an offense of endangerment, satisfied once the false information is capable of causing loss, regardless of whether a loss is ultimately proven.

## Attempt, Complicity and Penalties

Attempt is conceptually possible in fraudulent bankruptcy where the asset-stripping act has been started but not completed, though the requirement of a final bankruptcy ruling as an objective precondition effectively defers any such analysis until that ruling becomes final. Third parties who knowingly assist in stripping assets — for example, a person who accepts a sham transfer of property — may face separate liability depending on the nature of their contribution. Because Article 164 is limited to specific corporate roles, anyone outside that group can only be liable as an instigator or accomplice, never as a principal. Penalties range from three to eight years' imprisonment for fraudulent bankruptcy (Article 161), two months to one year for negligent bankruptcy (Article 162), and six months to three years' imprisonment or a judicial fine of up to one thousand days for the corporate misrepresentation offense (Article 164). None of these offenses is classified as requiring a formal complaint by the victim, so prosecutors may investigate on their own initiative — but in practice, the pace of the criminal case remains closely tied to findings made in the parallel bankruptcy-postponement or concordat files, meaning criminal proceedings routinely follow in the shadow of the commercial litigation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can someone be prosecuted for fraudulent bankruptcy before a bankruptcy ruling is final?**
No. A final bankruptcy ruling from the commercial court is treated as an objective precondition to punishability under both Article 161 and Article 162. Even if the underlying conduct is complete, the criminal case cannot proceed to conviction without it, and criminal courts often suspend proceedings to await the outcome of the commercial case.

**Is failing to hand over company books enough, by itself, to prove fraudulent or negligent bankruptcy?**
No. Turkish appellate practice has repeatedly held that the mere non-production of commercial books or records does not, on its own, establish either offense. For fraudulent bankruptcy, the prosecution must show an underlying asset-stripping disposition that the concealment of records was meant to hide. For negligent bankruptcy, a causal link between the trader's carelessness and the bankruptcy itself must be established, typically through expert examination.

**What is the practical difference between fraudulent and negligent bankruptcy for a creditor?**
Fraudulent bankruptcy requires proof that the trader intentionally acted to harm creditors and carries a substantially harsher sentence (three to eight years). Negligent bankruptcy requires only a failure to meet the standard of a prudent trader and carries a much lighter sentence (two months to one year). For creditors, the distinction affects both the strength of the case they can support and the leverage a criminal complaint may add to a civil recovery strategy.

**Can a company director be prosecuted under Article 164 even if the company never actually loses money?**
Yes. Article 164 is framed as an offense of endangerment: what matters is that the false information given in public statements or general-assembly materials was capable of causing loss to those concerned, not that a loss was actually suffered.
