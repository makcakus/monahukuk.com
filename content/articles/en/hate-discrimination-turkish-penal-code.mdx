Most legal systems address discrimination through administrative fines, labour compensation, or civil liability. Turkish criminal law takes a different, more exceptional path: Article 122 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") makes certain discriminatory acts a standalone criminal offense punishable by imprisonment. What makes this provision genuinely difficult to apply is not identifying the conduct on its face, but proving what was happening inside the perpetrator's mind. A landlord's refusal to rent an apartment, or an employer's rejection of a job applicant, can just as easily be an ordinary exercise of contractual freedom as it can be a criminal act of discrimination. The line between the two is the discriminatory motive the statute requires, and that line is where most Article 122 cases are actually won or lost.

## How the 2014 Amendment Reshaped the Offense

The provision reached its current form through Law No. 6529 of 2 March 2014, which rewrote the article under the heading "Hate and Discrimination" and, critically, inserted the requirement that the obstruction be carried out "because of hate" arising from one of a defined set of differences. This was a narrowing amendment, but it also gave the offense a much clearer identity: not every discriminatory act falls within the statute, only obstruction that is the product of hate rooted in the specific grounds the law enumerates. This reflects the principle that criminal law should be a last resort — forms of discrimination that can be adequately addressed through administrative or private law are deliberately left outside the criminal sphere.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Article 122 protects more than a victim's property or employment prospects in the narrow sense. It protects a person's right to participate in social and economic life on equal footing with others. Discriminatory obstruction does not merely deprive the victim of one transaction; it communicates that the victim is worth less because of the group they belong to. The interest at stake is therefore twofold: individual dignity and the right to equal treatment on one side, and the social peace that discrimination erodes on the other.

## A Closed, Not Illustrative, List of Protected Grounds

The statute enumerates the differences that can underlie the required hate: language, race, nationality, colour, sex, disability, political opinion, philosophical belief, religion, or sect. This list is exhaustive, not illustrative. Under the principle of legality in criminal law, courts cannot extend these grounds by analogy — obstruction based on a characteristic outside this list, however unjust it may be, simply does not fall within Article 122, no matter how serious the underlying wrong. In practice, disputes tend to cluster around the outer edge of the listed terms: whether conduct targeting a person's ethnic background can be captured under "race" or "nationality" is a recurring interpretive question. By contrast, stretching the listed concepts to cover grounds the legislature did not include — sexual orientation or age are commonly discussed examples — pushes against the limits the legality principle sets. Interpretation may clarify the ordinary meaning of a listed term, but it may not expand the list itself.

## Who Can Commit the Offense, Who Can Be a Victim, and What Conduct Is Covered

Article 122 is not a special-status offense — anyone can be a perpetrator. Where a public official commits the act by exploiting the opportunities their office provides, this raises a separate question of overlap with offenses relating to abuse of public duty. The victim, by contrast, must be an identifiable natural person: the statute speaks of obstruction directed against "a person," which means a diffuse or general discriminatory statement aimed at an undefined group does not by itself satisfy the offense unless it crystallizes into an obstruction of a specific individual.

The law defines four alternative forms of conduct:

- Preventing a person from buying, acquiring, or renting movable or immovable property that has been offered to the public,
- Preventing a person from benefiting from a particular service offered to the public,
- Preventing a person from being hired for employment,
- Preventing a person from carrying on an ordinary economic activity.

There is a structural distinction worth noting among these four grounds. The first two expressly require that the property or service have been "offered to the public" — property that was never placed on the market, or a private service never opened to general use, falls outside the offense. No equivalent limitation applies to hiring or to ordinary economic activity. The offense also requires an actual result, not merely an act: the statute is framed around a person who "obstructs," so the victim must actually have been prevented from acquiring the property, using the service, being hired, or carrying on the economic activity in question.

## How "Because of Hate" Shapes the Mental Element

The offense can only be committed intentionally; negligence is not sufficient. But ordinary intent alone does not satisfy the statute either. Because the law requires that the obstruction be carried out because of hate stemming from one of the listed differences, the mental element carries an additional, specific motive requirement on top of general intent. The perpetrator must know and want the obstruction to occur, and must be doing so out of hatred directed at the difference the victim embodies. This significantly raises the evidentiary bar: a refusal that looks like an economic decision, a commercial preference, or an arbitrary but neutral choice does not amount to the offense unless the underlying motive is actually established. In practice, courts assess motive through indirect evidence — the perpetrator's statements, advertisements, correspondence, a repeated pattern of behaviour toward members of the same group, and inconsistencies in the justification the perpetrator offers for the refusal.

## Distinguishing Discrimination from Incitement to Hatred

Article 122 is frequently confused with the separate offense of inciting the public to hatred or hostility. The distinguishing criterion is straightforward: incitement punishes public speech that endangers social peace, while the hate and discrimination offense punishes the actual obstruction of a concrete right belonging to a specific person. In one, speech is the essence of the wrong; in the other, an act is. This distinction has real practical consequences, because it determines which provision governs the investigation and who qualifies as the victim. Where a single course of conduct combines public incitement with the obstruction of a specific person, the requirements of both offenses are assessed independently.

## Attempt, Participation, and Concurrence of Offenses

Because the offense requires a result, an attempt is possible: if the perpetrator has begun acts aimed at obstruction but the result does not materialize for reasons beyond their control, the rules on attempted offenses apply. General principles of criminal participation govern liability where more than one person is involved — a person who directs a discriminatory practice through an institutional instruction may be liable as an instigator, while the person who actually carries out the instruction is liable as the principal offender. Where concurrence is concerned, the number of victims is decisive: acts committed against different individuals ordinarily constitute separate offenses, while repeated acts of obstruction against the same victim, carried out at different times pursuant to a single criminal design, are treated under the rules on continuing offenses. If independent offenses such as insult or threat are committed in the course of the obstruction, the rules on genuine concurrence of offenses apply.

## Sanctions and Procedural Features

The statute prescribes a single penalty for all four alternative forms of conduct: imprisonment of one to three years. It does not provide for a judicial fine as an alternative sanction, though whether the general provisions on short-term imprisonment can apply in a given case is assessed separately. Given that the upper limit does not exceed three years, mechanisms such as suspension of the announcement of the verdict or deferral of the sentence may become available where their other conditions are met. Because of the sentencing range, jurisdiction lies with the courts of first instance for ordinary criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi). The offense is not subject to a complaint requirement — the prosecution proceeds automatically once the authorities learn of it, and withdrawal of a complaint does not terminate the case. Whether mediation is available depends on whether this non-complaint-based offense is expressly listed among mediable offenses under the Turkish Code of Criminal Procedure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is every refusal to rent, sell, or hire someone a criminal offense under Article 122?**
No. The obstruction must be shown to have been motivated by hate connected to one of the statute's listed grounds — language, race, nationality, colour, sex, disability, political opinion, philosophical belief, religion, or sect. A refusal based on ordinary commercial, economic, or personal reasons is not criminal even if it disadvantages the person concerned.

**Can grounds not listed in the statute, such as sexual orientation or age, support a charge under Article 122?**
Under the principle of legality, the list of protected grounds is exhaustive and cannot be extended by analogy. Obstruction motivated by a characteristic outside the enumerated list falls outside this offense, whatever other legal remedies may exist for it.

**What is the difference between this offense and incitement to hatred or hostility?**
Incitement punishes public speech that threatens social peace and does not require an identifiable victim. The hate and discrimination offense under Article 122 requires that a specific, identifiable person actually be obstructed from exercising a concrete right — the offense centres on an act, not a statement.

**How is discriminatory motive actually proven in these cases?**
Since general intent is not enough, motive is typically established through indirect evidence: the perpetrator's own statements, advertisements or written communications, a repeated pattern of similar treatment toward people from the same group, and inconsistencies between the stated justification for the refusal and the surrounding facts.
