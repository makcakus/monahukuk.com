Foreign nationals living in Turkey who are exploring fertility treatment, or who already have a child born through surrogacy or donor conception abroad, often assume Turkey's rules will look similar to those back home. They do not. Assisted reproduction and surrogacy in Turkey are governed by some of the strictest rules in Europe, and getting the legal side wrong can affect a child's registration, residence status, and even legal parentage for years to come. This guide explains what the law actually allows, what it bans outright, and what foreign families should think about before they start treatment or move to Turkey with a child already born.

## Assisted Reproduction Is Legal, But Only for Married Couples

Fertility treatment using in-vitro fertilisation and similar techniques is legal in Turkey and available at licensed treatment centres, including in Antalya. The regulation governing these centres restricts the treatment to married couples who cannot conceive naturally and who are found medically suitable. Before treatment begins, the clinic must confirm the couple's marriage documents and take a joint, written consent from both spouses. A foreign couple can access treatment in Turkey on the same basis as Turkish nationals, provided their marriage is legally valid and documented.

What the regulation does not allow is just as important. Treatment centres may only use the wife's own egg and the husband's own sperm; there is no route for an unmarried couple, a single person, or a same-sex couple to access licensed fertility treatment in Turkey under current rules.

## The Bright Line: No Donor Gametes, No Surrogacy

Since a 2018 amendment to Turkey's law on organ and tissue procurement, the ban on third-party reproduction has statutory force, not just regulatory guidance. The law is explicit on two points. First, using either spouse's reproductive cells, or an embryo created from them, to have someone else carry the pregnancy is prohibited outright, meaning surrogacy in any form is not legally available in Turkey. Second, donating, selling, possessing, storing, transporting, importing, exporting, or arranging the use of another person's egg, sperm, or embryo is equally banned. In short, Turkish clinics can only help a married couple use their own genetic material, carried by the wife herself.

The legislature's stated reason for the ban is to preserve certainty over a child's parentage and to reflect prevailing social values. Whatever view one takes of the policy, the practical consequence for foreign clients is unambiguous: donor-egg or donor-sperm treatment, and any surrogacy arrangement, must take place outside Turkey if it is to happen at all.

## If Your Child Was Born Through Surrogacy or Donor Conception Abroad

Many foreign nationals arrive in Turkey — for work, retirement, or property ownership — with a child already born through surrogacy or donor conception in a country where that was legal. Turkey does not have a settled, published body of case law specifically addressing how such a child's foreign birth certificate is treated once the family is here, which itself is a reason to seek advice early rather than assume the foreign paperwork will be accepted automatically.

The starting point in Turkish civil law is that legal motherhood follows the birth itself, not genetics or intention (see the next section). That default rule was written with domestic births in mind, but it can complicate how a Turkish authority looks at a foreign birth certificate that lists an intended parent who did not give birth. This does not mean the child's status in Turkey is at risk, but it does mean the family should not assume the matter is automatically settled and should plan the paperwork — birth registration, residence permit, and any future adoption or acknowledgment steps — with a lawyer familiar with both the foreign order and Turkish practice.

## How Turkish Law Decides Who the Legal Parents Are

Under the Turkish Civil Code, parentage between a child and the mother is established by the birth itself — the woman who gives birth is the child's legal mother. Parentage with the father is established through the mother's marriage, a formal acknowledgment, or a court judgment. Read together with the ban on surrogacy described above, this shows how tightly Turkish law ties legal parenthood to the act of giving birth within a marriage, rather than to genetic contribution or the parents' original intention. For foreign families formed through modern reproductive technology, this is precisely the kind of mismatch worth resolving with proper legal advice, whether that means a stepparent [adoption](/en/articles/adoption-by-foreigners-turkey-guide) for the parent who is not the birth mother, or a [paternity](/en/articles/paternity-denial-establishment-lawsuit-turkey) action to formally confirm the biological father's legal status.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Can a foreign married couple do IVF in Turkey using their own eggs and sperm?

Yes. Foreign nationals can access licensed fertility treatment in Turkey on the same terms as Turkish citizens, provided they are legally married and the treatment uses only their own reproductive cells.

### Q: Can I use an egg donor or a surrogate if I travel to Turkey specifically for that?

No. Egg, sperm, and embryo donation are banned for use in Turkey, as is any surrogacy arrangement, regardless of where the intended parents come from.

### Q: Is our marriage abroad enough to qualify for treatment in Turkey?

Generally yes, provided the marriage is properly documented and recognised, but the clinic will require the original or certified marriage documents before starting treatment, so confirm the paperwork in advance.

### Q: Our child was born through surrogacy in another country. Will Turkey recognise us both as legal parents?

Not automatically, and the outcome depends on the specifics of your case. Because Turkish courts have not published a settled approach to this exact situation, it is worth getting advice before you rely on the foreign paperwork alone.

### Q: Does Turkish law recognise same-sex couples or single parents for fertility treatment?

No. Licensed treatment is limited to married different-sex couples, and Turkey does not recognise same-sex marriage for this purpose.
