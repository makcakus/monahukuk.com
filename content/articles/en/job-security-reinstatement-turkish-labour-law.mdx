Turkish labour law does not let an employer end an open-ended employment contract simply because it wishes to. Where a workplace and employee meet certain thresholds, the employer must have a legally recognised "valid reason" to terminate, follow a defined procedure, and be prepared to defend that reason before a court if challenged. This body of rules — generally called job security (iş güvencesi) and set out in Articles 18 through 21 of Labour Code No. 4857 — is one of the features of the Turkish employment system that most surprises foreign employers used to more flexible at-will regimes. Understanding who is covered, what counts as a valid reason, and what happens if a court finds the termination unjustified is essential for any business that employs staff in Turkey.

## Who Is Covered: The Thirty-Employee and Six-Month Thresholds

Job security protection does not apply to every employment relationship. Three conditions must be met together: the workplace must employ thirty or more people, the employee must have at least six months of seniority, and the contract must be open-ended (indefinite-term) rather than fixed-term. For the thirty-employee count, all employees across every workplace the employer operates in the same line of business are added together — an employer cannot escape the threshold by artificially splitting a single business into smaller formal workplaces. Seniority is calculated using the deemed-worked periods defined in Article 66 of the Labour Code, and time spent with the same employer across different workplaces is combined. Employees working underground are exempted from the six-month seniority requirement altogether, reflecting the particularly harsh conditions of mining work. On the other side, senior representatives of the employer — those who manage the entire business or an entire workplace and who hold the authority to hire and dismiss staff — are treated as standing in the employer's shoes and fall outside job security protection entirely: no valid reason is required to end their contracts, and they cannot bring a reinstatement claim.

## What Counts as a Valid Reason

The law groups valid reasons into three categories: reasons arising from the employee's incapacity (such as persistently low performance or an inability to adapt to the role), reasons arising from the employee's conduct (persistent conflict with colleagues or other behaviour that disrupts the workplace), and reasons arising from the requirements of the business, the workplace or the work itself (economic difficulty, technological change, downsizing and similar operational needs). The underlying test is whether continuing the employment relationship can reasonably be expected of the employer; minor, everyday friction does not rise to the level of a valid reason. The statute also lists specific grounds that can never serve as a valid reason for termination, including union membership or lawful union activity, acting as a workplace union representative, pursuing rights or claims against the employer through administrative or judicial channels, discriminatory grounds such as race, sex, marital status, family responsibilities, pregnancy, religion or political opinion, absence during the periods when female employees are legally barred from working around childbirth, and absence during the waiting period added to the notice period because of illness or accident. This list exists precisely to stop an employer from citing a superficially valid reason while actually dismissing an employee for a prohibited motive: if the employee proves that the real reason for termination falls within this list, the termination is invalid regardless of the reason the employer formally cited.

## Procedural Requirements: Written Notice and the Right to Respond

Article 19 layers a procedural safeguard on top of the valid-reason requirement. The termination notice must be in writing, and the reason must be stated clearly and definitively — a vague or general statement cannot later be supplemented with a different justification. Separately, where the termination is based on the employee's conduct or performance, the employer must obtain the employee's response to the allegations before terminating; this is the practical expression of the employee's right of defence, and a termination carried out without it is procedurally defective even if the underlying reason would otherwise have been valid. This defence requirement applies only to conduct- and performance-based dismissals, not to terminations grounded in business or operational needs. It also does not apply to the employer's right of immediate termination for just cause under Article 25 of the Labour Code — where those conditions are met, the employer may rely on that route instead.

## Challenging a Dismissal: Mandatory Mediation Before Suit

An employee who believes no reason was given, or that the reason given was not valid, must apply to a mediator within one month of receiving the termination notice before a reinstatement claim can be brought — mediation is a mandatory precondition to filing suit under Turkey's Labour Courts framework. If mediation does not produce an agreement, the employee has two weeks from the date of the final mediation record to file a lawsuit with the labour court, or, if both parties agree, to submit the dispute to private arbitration instead. If a case is filed directly with the court without first going through mediation, it will be dismissed on procedural grounds and the parties notified automatically; a further two-week window to apply to a mediator then opens once that dismissal becomes final. Missing the initial one-month deadline is fatal — the termination becomes final and a reinstatement claim can no longer be pursued. On the substance, the burden of proving that the termination rested on a valid reason lies with the employer; the employee bears the burden only if alleging that the true reason for dismissal was something else, such as a union-related or discriminatory motive. This allocation of the burden of proof, which favours the structurally weaker party to the relationship, tends to shape how these cases are actually litigated and decided.

## Consequences of an Invalid Termination: Reinstatement and Compensation

If a court or arbitrator finds that no valid reason was shown, or that the stated reason was not valid, the termination is declared invalid and the employer must reinstate the employee within one month. A favourable ruling does not put the employee back to work automatically, however: the employee must apply to the employer within ten working days of the final court decision being served, or the termination is deemed to have been valid after all, with the employer liable only for its ordinary legal consequences. Where the employee does apply in time but the employer fails to reinstate within one month, the employer must pay reinstatement (non-engagement) compensation equal to between four and eight months' wages, the exact figure to be fixed by the court or arbitrator itself, together with up to four months of back pay and other entitlements accrued for the period the employee was kept out of work pending the final decision. The precise amount of the reinstatement compensation is set within that four-to-eight-month range according to factors such as the employee's seniority and the severity of the underlying dismissal; where the dismissal is found to be union-related, Law No. 6356 sets a floor of at least one year's wages instead. If the employee is in fact reinstated, notice pay and severance already paid at termination are deducted from these sums.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does job security protection apply to every employee in Turkey?**
No. It applies only where the workplace has thirty or more employees (counted across all workplaces of the employer in the same line of business), the employee has at least six months' seniority, and the contract is open-ended. Senior managerial representatives with hiring-and-firing authority are excluded regardless of these thresholds.

**What happens if the employee misses the one-month mediation deadline?**
The deadline to apply to a mediator, running from receipt of the termination notice, is strict. If it is missed, the termination becomes final and a reinstatement claim can no longer be brought, regardless of how weak the employer's stated reason may have been.

**Who has to prove the termination was justified?**
The employer bears the burden of proving the termination rested on a valid reason. The employee only carries a burden of proof if claiming the real motive was something else entirely, such as a discriminatory or union-related reason.

**If a court orders reinstatement, does the employee go back to work automatically?**
No. The employee must apply to the employer within ten working days of the final decision. If the employer then fails to reinstate within a month, it owes reinstatement compensation of four to eight months' wages plus limited back pay, rather than being compelled to physically reinstate the employee.
