*Article 3 – (1) The parties are free as to applying to a mediator, continuing the process, concluding it or abandoning it. (Sentence added: 6/12/2018-7155/22) Provided that the provision of Article 18/A on mediation as a condition of action is reserved.*

*(2) The parties have equal rights both when applying to a mediator and throughout the process.*

The load-bearing principle of the mediation system is voluntariness: the parties produce their own solution, and no one can be forced into a settlement. Article 3/1 recognises that freedom at four separate moments — applying, continuing the process, concluding it and abandoning it. Yet the second sentence, added in 2018, also shows the principle's present limit: the provision of Article 18/A on mediation as a condition of action is reserved. That addition is the most concise expression of the ten-year evolution of Turkish mediation law, and it calls for a distinction frequently confused in practice: **the compulsory nature of the application is not the same as a compulsory settlement.**

## Voluntariness Divided: Compulsory Application, Voluntary Outcome

In mediation as a condition of action, the only compulsory elements are applying to the mediation bureau and concluding the process with final minutes. After attending the first meeting, a party may withdraw from the process by saying "I do not wish to settle" (Art. 17/1-c), and this produces no substantive-law sanction. A record of non-settlement is entered in the final minutes and the judicial route opens.

The only genuine sanction attaching to voluntariness concerns the attendance obligation in Article 18/A/11: a party who, without showing a valid excuse, **does not attend the first meeting at all** is identified in the final minutes and, even if wholly or partly successful in the action to be brought, is held liable for half of the other side's litigation costs; in addition, half of the attorney's fee determined under the Minimum Attorney's Fee Tariff is awarded in favour of the other side. The point to note is that the sanction attaches not to failing to settle but to **failing to attend**. A party who attends the meeting and accepts no offer at all does not face this sanction.

The result of this balance is that mediation as a condition of action does not abolish voluntariness; it limits it by an "obligation to sit at the table". This distinction underlies the view — of the Constitutional Court and of scholarship alike — that the arrangement is proportionate in terms of the freedom to seek justice: the judicial route is not closed, merely made subject to a short preliminary stage.

## The Equality Principle and the Mediator's Duty to Maintain Balance

Article 3/2 states that the parties have equal rights "both when applying to a mediator and throughout the process". This becomes an active duty for the mediator under Article 9/3: the mediator is obliged to observe equality between the parties.

Equality here is a functional rather than a formal criterion. An equal amount of speaking time, or the same number of private sessions, does not by itself show that equality has been achieved. In relationships marked by pronounced informational asymmetry — an employee and a corporate employer, a consumer and a company, a foreign tenant and a Turkish landlord — the mediator's duty to inform (Art. 11) becomes the practical guarantee of the equality principle. The 9th Civil Chamber's finding, in a case where the mediator gave no explanation at all that they were the mediator or as to the legal consequences of the process, that such conduct was incompatible with the mediator's independent and impartial position, reflects precisely this functional understanding of equality.

## Defective Consent: The Sanction for Breach of Equality

A mediation settlement document is, in the words of the 9th Civil Chamber, "a substantive-law contract in the sense of the law of obligations". The consequence of that characterisation is that the general provisions on contracts — mistake, fraud, duress and unconscionable exploitation — may be applied to the settlement document as well. This is the route to be taken where voluntariness and equality have been breached.

Three points require attention in practice.

**Time.** Under Article 39 of the Turkish Code of Obligations, the right to annul on the ground of defective consent is subject to a one-year forfeiture period running from the moment the mistake or fraud is discovered or the effect of the duress ceases. The 9th Civil Chamber holds that, in respect of a voluntary mediation document, a request for annulment must be dismissed where that one-year period has expired.

**Proof.** Asserting defective consent in the abstract is not enough. The Chamber consistently states that where a claimant who does not deny their signature and has also signed the information minutes fails to support an allegation of fraud with concrete witness evidence or other proof, the allegation is to be regarded as unproven.

**Alternative characterisation.** Even where defective consent cannot be proved, the document may be held invalid on grounds of mediability or procedure. The Court of Cassation holds that texts which do not qualify as a valid mediation settlement document must be assessed as a **release** or as **performance** within the framework of the Labour Law and Article 420 of the Turkish Code of Obligations. This produces an important consequence in the employee's favour: the document cannot benefit from the rule in Article 18/5 that "no action may be brought concerning the matters agreed upon", and results only in the set-off of the amount paid.

## The Right to Withdraw at Every Stage

The words "abandoning the process" in Article 3/1 give the parties the possibility of withdrawing without giving any reason. Withdrawal terminates the activity under Article 17/1-c; notice to the other party or to the mediator suffices. The right to withdraw cannot be abolished even by a mediation agreement; even if the parties stipulate a clause such as "the party leaving the mediation process shall pay a penalty", that clause must be regarded as invalid in the face of the mandatory character of Article 3/1.

By contrast, Article 18/A/18 sets an important limit: where special laws impose an obligation to resort to arbitration or another alternative dispute-resolution method, or where the parties have made an arbitration agreement, the provisions on mediation as a condition of action do not apply. In a case where the contract provided for mediation first and arbitration afterwards, the 3rd Civil Chamber found it lawful that, in accordance with the parties' intention, mediation was pursued first and, no settlement being reached, arbitration was then invoked.

## Case Law of the Court of Cassation

The following decisions show how allegations of breach of voluntariness and equality are assessed.

**1. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2026/1306, Decision No. 2026/3216, dated 15.04.2026**

The Chamber characterised the dispute as a request for a declaration that a voluntary mediation settlement document was not a valid settlement document within Article 18/5 of Law No. 6325, and emphasised that a mediation settlement document is a substantive-law contract in the sense of the law of obligations. That characterisation opens the door to applying the invalidity provisions on contracts to the document.

**2. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2025/4863, Decision No. 2025/5550, dated 30.06.2025**

The decision establishes that, as regards a voluntary mediation document, claims based on defective consent are subject to a one-year forfeiture period and that, where that period has expired as at the date of the action, the request for invalidity must be dismissed.

**3. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2022/16194, Decision No. 2023/279, dated 12.01.2023**

The Chamber found it correct that the voluntary mediation settlement document submitted as an annex to the defendant's statement of defence was dealt with as a preliminary issue during the proceedings, and endorsed the assessment dismissing the allegation on the ground that it could not be proved by the evidence on file that the document had been signed under conditions of defective consent such as mistake, fraud and duress.

**4. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2025/5789, Decision No. 2025/7613, dated 09.10.2025**

In the dispute it was established that the mediator had acted against one of the parties and that the text drawn up did not qualify as a valid mediation settlement document. The Chamber stated that in such a case the document must be assessed under the conditions in Article 420 of the Turkish Code of Obligations No. 6098 or the provisions on performance.

**5. Court of Cassation, 9th Civil Chamber, Case No. 2025/6508, Decision No. 2025/7765, dated 13.10.2025**

The Chamber held that where a claimant who admits having signed the mediation information minutes and the settlement document, and does not contest their signature, fails to support an allegation of fraud with concrete witness evidence or other proof, the allegation of defective consent cannot be regarded as proven.
