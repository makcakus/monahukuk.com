Most crimes under Turkish law are completed in a single moment. Unlawful deprivation of liberty, defined in Article 109 of the Turkish Penal Code, is different: it keeps happening for as long as the victim's freedom of movement remains restrained. The offense is complete the instant a person is locked in or physically prevented from leaving, but the illegality does not stop there — it renews itself for every minute the restraint continues, until the person is released or otherwise regains their freedom. This "continuing offense" quality is not a mere academic label. It drives when the limitation period starts to run, whether an attempt is even legally possible, how late-arriving accomplices are treated, and how far into the episode a victim can still lawfully use force in self-defense. Anyone assessing a Turkish deprivation-of-liberty case has to start from this feature, because nearly every contested point in practice traces back to it.

## The Interest the Law Protects: Freedom of Movement

Article 109 does not protect personal liberty as an abstract concept; it protects one concrete expression of it — a person's ability to leave a place they are in, or to go where they choose. What matters is not what the offender said or threatened, but the plain physical fact of whether the victim could leave when they wanted to. Because the protected interest is the legal entitlement to move, not the victim's subjective sense of safety or calm, awareness of the restraint is not required. A sleeping person, an intoxicated person, or someone unconscious can still be a victim of this offense, since the law is concerned with the restriction of their freedom of movement, not with whether they perceived it.

## What Counts as the Act: Preventing Departure or Forcing a Stay

The statute frames the conduct in two mirror-image ways: stopping the victim from going somewhere, or forcing the victim to remain somewhere against their will. It is what Turkish criminal doctrine calls a "freely-shaped" offense, meaning the law does not list specific methods — any conduct producing the same practical result qualifies. Locking a door, refusing to stop a moving vehicle, removing a ladder or a set of stairs, confiscating someone's identity documents and phone so that leaving becomes practically impossible, or even wheeling away a sick person's wheelchair can all amount to the offense. It can also be committed by omission: if someone has a duty to unlock a door and simply fails to do so, that inaction is treated the same as an active restraint.

For criminal liability to attach, the restriction must last long enough to be legally significant — a restraint of a few seconds does not reach that threshold. But once that minimum bar is crossed, the length of the confinement affects only the severity of the sentence, not whether the offense exists at all; the Code sets no fixed minimum duration.

## Consequences of Being a Continuing Offense

Because the offense keeps being committed while the restraint lasts, four practical consequences follow. First, the criminal limitation period does not start on the day the confinement began — it starts on the day the confinement ends. Second, a person can still join in as a principal offender, not merely as an accessory, at any point before the victim regains their freedom, because the underlying conduct is still ongoing. Third, every location where the restraint occurred or continued can count as a place where the offense was committed, which creates alternative venues for jurisdiction. Fourth — and most significant in practice — the conditions for self-defense remain available to the victim throughout the entire episode: if a victim uses force against the offender while trying to escape, that use of force is treated as occurring during an ongoing attack, not after it has ended.

Because the offense is only complete once the victim's freedom of movement has actually been eliminated, an attempt is legally possible only for conduct that precedes that moment — for example, someone who tries to force a victim into a car but is stopped before succeeding can be held liable for an attempted offense.

## Intent and the Special Case of a Sexual Motive

The offense can only be committed intentionally; there is no punishable negligent version. In its basic form, general intent is enough, and the offender's underlying motive is normally irrelevant to liability. The one express exception concerns a sexual motive: where the restraint is carried out for that purpose, the penalty is increased by half. What matters here is not whether any sexual act actually took place, but whether the liberty of the victim was restricted for that purpose; if the intended act is in fact carried out, the offender additionally faces liability for the separate sexual offense.

A victim's genuine consent negates the offense — but only for as long as that consent continues throughout the restraint. If someone who initially agreed to stay later wants to leave and is prevented from doing so, the offense comes into existence from that later moment onward, regardless of how the situation began.

## Aggravating Circumstances and Concurrent Injury

The Code builds up its aggravating factors in layers. Where force, threats or deceit are used to carry out the restraint or during it, this is treated as an independent aggravated form, raising the sentence to a range of two to seven years' imprisonment. A further set of aggravating circumstances turns on the identity of the offender or victim, the means used, or the number of offenders acting together: committing the offense with a weapon, by more than one person acting jointly, because of a public duty the victim performs, by abusing the influence conferred by a public office, against an ascendant, descendant, spouse or former spouse, or against a child or a person unable to defend themselves physically or mentally — any of these doubles the sentence otherwise applicable.

A separate aggravating factor applies where the restraint causes the victim significant economic loss, which adds a judicial fine of up to one thousand days on top of the prison sentence. The Code also expressly resolves the overlap with intentional bodily harm: if, in the course of or for the purpose of committing this offense, an aggravated form of intentional bodily injury occurs, the provisions on intentional bodily harm apply in addition.

A related and frequently litigated question arises where the restraint is merely the vehicle for another crime, such as robbery or sexual assault. If the victim is held beyond the time genuinely needed to carry out that other offense, a separate liability for unlawful deprivation of liberty arises alongside it — and whether that extra margin of time actually existed is often the single most contested factual issue in these files.

## Voluntary Release: The Conditions for a Reduced Sentence

Turkish law gives an offender an incentive to end the confinement voluntarily. Under Article 110 of the Penal Code, a person who commits this offense can have their sentence reduced by up to two-thirds if, before any investigation into the offense has begun, they voluntarily release the victim in a safe location without having caused the victim any personal harm. Three conditions must all be satisfied: the release must occur before an investigation has started, the victim must not have suffered personal harm, and the release must be spontaneous and take place somewhere safe. A "safe location" excludes releasing the victim somewhere isolated or exposed to a new danger, while "spontaneous" excludes a release that is really a response to imminent capture or the intervention of law enforcement.

## Penalties, Procedure and Corporate Liability

In its basic form, the offense carries a prison sentence of one to five years. Where force, threats or deceit are used, the range rises to two to seven years; where one of the aggravating circumstances listed above applies, the applicable sentence is doubled; and where a sexual motive is present, it is increased by half instead.

The offense is not subject to a victim's complaint requirement — investigation and prosecution proceed on the authorities' own initiative, and a victim's withdrawal of a complaint does not end the case. The basic form of the offense falls within the scope of mediation, while its aggravated forms are excluded from mediation. Because sentencing enhancements and reductions are not taken into account when allocating jurisdiction, these cases are tried before the criminal court of first instance. A related provision, Article 111, allows security measures specific to legal entities to be imposed on any corporate body that derived an unjust benefit from the commission of this offense.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the victim need to have been aware of being confined for the offense to apply?**
No. Because the law protects the entitlement to freedom of movement rather than the victim's subjective experience, a person who is asleep, intoxicated, or unconscious can still be a victim, since what matters is the objective restriction of their ability to move.

**When does the statute of limitations begin to run?**
It begins on the day the restraint ends, not the day it began. Because this is treated as a continuing offense, the limitation clock does not start until the victim actually regains their freedom of movement.

**Can someone who joins in after the confinement has already started still be treated as a principal offender?**
Yes. Because the offense continues to be committed for as long as the restraint lasts, a person who joins in before the victim is released can be held liable as a co-principal rather than merely as an accessory.

**What does it take to qualify for the reduced sentence under Article 110?**
Three conditions must all be met: the release must happen before any investigation has begun, the victim must not have been personally harmed, and the release must be voluntary and take place in a location that is genuinely safe. Turkish appellate practice reads "safe location" strictly, excluding places where the victim would still face difficulty returning to safety.
