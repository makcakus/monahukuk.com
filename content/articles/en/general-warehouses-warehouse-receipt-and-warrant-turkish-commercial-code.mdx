TTK Art. 832-840 regulate a financing tool still used in Turkish agricultural commodity trading: a licensed warehouse issues a paired set of documents — a warehouse receipt and a warrant — that let the depositor sell or pledge stored goods without physically removing them.

## Licensing and Form

Under Art. 832, only warehouses licensed by the Ministry can issue these documents; ordinary storage arrangements without licensed paper fall outside this regime entirely and are governed by general bailment rules instead (Art. 833). Both documents must be torn from a stub-book kept on file at the warehouse (Art. 836) and must carry identical mandatory content — depositor identity, warehouse identity, description and value of the goods, applicable taxes and insurance status, and whether the instrument runs to order or to a named person (Art. 834-835). Missing any of these elements, or issuance by an unlicensed warehouse, strips the document of its status as a negotiable instrument, reducing it to a mere receipt.

## The Ownership/Pledge Split

The commercial heart of the system is Art. 839: endorsing both documents together transfers full ownership; endorsing the warrant alone creates a pledge right over the goods in favor of the transferee; and endorsing the receipt alone transfers ownership subject to whatever pledge the warrant holder holds. In practice, a depositor can endorse the warrant to a bank as loan collateral while separately selling the receipt to a buyer — the same physical goods simultaneously secure financing and change ownership, with the buyer taking subject to the bank's recorded lien. The warrant's first endorsement must record the secured debt, interest rate, and maturity (Art. 840), and those same particulars are copied onto the receipt so a later receipt-holder can see exactly what encumbrance they are acquiring the goods subject to.

## Practical Takeaway

For a foreign lender or trader dealing in Turkish agricultural commodities, the key distinction is that holding the receipt means owning the goods, while holding the warrant means holding a pledge over them — and a title check on either document alone tells only half the story.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What purpose do the warehouse receipt and the warrant serve?

Under TTK Article 832/1, general warehouses accept goods and grain under a contract of storage <b>against the issue of a warehouse receipt and a warrant</b>, thereby giving depositors <b>the ability to sell or pledge the deposited goods by means of these instruments</b>. The goods remain in the warehouse while being sold or used as credit security through the instruments. The <b>warehouse receipt</b> represents ownership and the <b>warrant</b> the right of pledge.

### What is the effect of endorsing the instruments separately?

Article 839/1 of the TTK lays down three consequences: <b>endorsement of the warehouse receipt and the warrant together transfers ownership of the deposited goods</b>; <b>endorsement of the warrant alone</b> gives the endorsee a right of pledge over the goods; and <b>endorsement of the warehouse receipt alone</b> transfers ownership of the goods encumbered with the warrant holder's right of pledge. This structure allows ownership of the goods and the security right over them to circulate independently of one another.

### Is every warehouse a general warehouse?

No. Under TTK Article 833/1, <b>the provisions on general warehouses do not apply to other institutions and places established to accept goods and grain solely under a contract of storage, without issuing the instruments referred to in Article 832.</b> What is decisive is not the storage activity but <b>the power to issue a warehouse receipt and a warrant</b>. An ordinary bonded warehouse or depot cannot issue these instruments and does not benefit from the prohibition of attachment in Article 841.
