Turkish employment law imposes a continuous duty on employers to treat employees equally, from the moment a position is offered until the employment relationship ends. This duty, set out in Article 5 of Labour Code No. 4857, is one of the principal counterweights to the structural imbalance of power between employer and employee, limiting how far an employer's freedom of contract and managerial authority may go. For foreign investors and HR teams managing a Turkish workforce, understanding what counts as prohibited discrimination — and how much easier Turkish procedural law makes it for an employee to prove it — is essential to managing legal exposure around pay, promotion and termination decisions.

## What the Equal Treatment Principle Actually Requires

Equal treatment under Article 5 does not mean employers must treat every employee identically. Turkish courts and legal doctrine consistently recognize that an employer may treat employees differently where the difference rests on an objective, justifiable basis — seniority, performance, qualifications, or the nature of the job itself. What the law prohibits is arbitrary differentiation, and in particular, differentiation based on a closed list of protected grounds: language, race, colour, sex, disability, political opinion, philosophical belief, religion and sect, among comparable characteristics.

This distinction matters in practice. A pay gap between two employees is not automatically unlawful discrimination — the employer remains free to justify it by reference to experience, output or role. But where a difference in treatment tracks one of the protected grounds listed in the statute, Turkish courts treat it as a distinct and more serious category of claim, separate from an ordinary breach of the general duty to treat staff fairly.

## Protection for Part-Time and Fixed-Term Employees

Article 5 separately addresses a form of discrimination that has become increasingly relevant as flexible work arrangements spread: an employer may not, without an essential reason, treat a part-time employee less favourably than a comparable full-time employee, or a fixed-term employee less favourably than a comparable permanent employee. This provision protects employees whose only distinguishing feature is the structure of their contract, not the substance of their work, and is particularly relevant for businesses that rely on seasonal or project-based staffing — a common pattern in Antalya's tourism and construction sectors.

## Sex and Pregnancy Discrimination, and Equal Pay for Equal Value

The statute gives sex and pregnancy discrimination especially detailed treatment. Unless a genuine biological reason or a requirement inherent to the job dictates otherwise, an employer may not treat an employee differently, directly or indirectly, because of sex or pregnancy — at any stage from formation of the employment contract through to its termination. The reference to *indirect* discrimination is significant: a policy that appears neutral on its face can still breach Article 5 if, in practice, it disadvantages employees of one sex.

The law also addresses pay specifically: a lower wage may not be set for the same work, or work of equal value, because of the employee's sex. This rule reaches beyond identical job titles — it also covers different roles that are, in substance, of comparable value once skill, responsibility, effort and working conditions are weighed against each other. Wage discrimination claims are, in practice, among the most common discrimination disputes employers face, which is why pay-setting decisions deserve particular care. Nor can an employer justify a lower wage by pointing to protective measures that apply because of an employee's sex, such as restrictions on night work or maternity leave — the statute expressly rules this out as a defence.

## The Shifted Burden of Proof

Perhaps the most consequential feature of Article 5, from a risk-management perspective, is its departure from ordinary evidentiary rules. As a general matter, an employee alleging a breach of the equal treatment duty carries the burden of proving it. Turkish law relaxes this considerably in discrimination cases: once the employee presents facts that strongly suggest discrimination has occurred — a prima facie case — the burden shifts to the employer, who must then prove that no such violation took place.

This shift exists precisely because discrimination is inherently difficult for an employee to prove from the outside; employees ordinarily have no access to an employer's internal decision-making or to other staff members' personnel records. In practice, showing that comparable colleagues with the same role and seniority received a pay increase while the claimant did not can be enough to shift the burden. From that point, the employer must show that the difference in treatment rested on an objective reason unrelated to any protected ground. This relaxed evidentiary regime operates alongside — but separately from — the rules under Article 20 governing proof of a valid reason for dismissal, so where a discrimination claim is raised together with a wrongful dismissal claim, both evidentiary frameworks apply in parallel.

## Discrimination Compensation and Its Relationship to Other Claims

Where the equal treatment duty is breached, during the employment relationship or upon its termination, the employee may claim appropriate compensation of up to four months' wages, in addition to being made whole for any rights they were denied. This is not primarily designed to compensate the employee's exact financial loss; Turkish courts treat it as having a preventive and deterrent function, with the amount left to the judge's discretion based on the severity of the case.

Discrimination compensation is a separate and independent claim from severance pay, notice pay, or the compensation due for an employer's failure to reinstate a dismissed employee. Turkish case law allows these claims to be pursued together in the same lawsuit, but each must still be assessed on its own terms — the same underlying conduct does not automatically justify every possible head of compensation. This distinction is particularly relevant in reinstatement disputes where a dismissal is alleged to be discriminatory: the compensation for non-reinstatement and the discrimination compensation are evaluated independently of one another, a point that frequently arises in practice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does paying two employees differently automatically count as discrimination?**
No. A pay difference is lawful if it is based on an objective factor such as seniority, performance or qualifications. It becomes a discrimination claim under Article 5 specifically when the difference tracks a protected characteristic such as sex, and it becomes an equal-pay claim when it concerns the same or equally valuable work.

**If an employee simply alleges discrimination, does the employer have to disprove it?**
Only once the employee first establishes facts that make discrimination appear likely. At that point, yes — the burden shifts, and the employer must show the treatment rested on an objective, non-discriminatory reason.

**Can an employee claim both reinstatement compensation and discrimination compensation for the same dismissal?**
Yes, Turkish courts allow both claims to be pursued together, but each is assessed independently on its own conditions — receiving one does not automatically entitle the employee to the other.

**Is indirect discrimination covered, or only explicit differential treatment?**
Article 5 explicitly covers indirect discrimination in the context of sex and pregnancy — meaning a seemingly neutral policy that in practice disadvantages one sex can still breach the statute.
