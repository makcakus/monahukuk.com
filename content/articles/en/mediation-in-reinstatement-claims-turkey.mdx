Reinstatement is the narrowest and least forgiving field in terms of mediation time limits. Two separate forfeiture periods run one after the other, and both are measured in days. A single day's delay can wholly extinguish the right to benefit from the job-security provisions.

## Two Periods, One After the Other

Article 20/1 of Labour Law No. 4857 sets the periods out expressly:

**First period — one month.** An employee whose employment contract has been terminated must, alleging that no reason was given in the notice of termination or that the reason given is not valid, apply to a mediator with a reinstatement claim **within one month of the date the notice of termination was served**.

**Second period — two weeks.** Where no settlement is reached at the end of the mediation activity, an action may be brought before the labour court **within two weeks of the date the final minutes were drawn up**. If the parties agree, the dispute may be taken within the same period to a private arbitrator instead of the labour court.

Both periods are forfeiture periods; they are taken into account by the judge of their own motion and cannot be extended.

## The Critical Detail: "The Date Drawn Up"

The second period starts from the date the final minutes were **drawn up** — not the date they were served on you, uploaded to the UYAP system or learned of by your lawyer.

This is the most common source of lost rights in practice. Receiving the minutes a few days after the session does not mean that the two-week period started on that day.

For that reason, the accuracy of the date on the final minutes must be separately checked. In a case where there was a contradiction between the date the minutes were drawn up and the date of the signature on them, the 9th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation did not find the minutes to be in due form, and emphasised that **where there is doubt as to a forfeiture period, it must be interpreted in favour of the person against whom the period runs**. That is an important rule of interpretation in the employee's favour.

## The Safety Valve: Where the Action Was Brought Directly

The Law provides a rescue mechanism found in no other field. According to the continuation of Article 20/1 of the Labour Law:

> Where the action is dismissed on procedural grounds because it was brought directly without applying to a mediator, **the decision of dismissal is served on the parties of the court's own motion**. A mediator may be applied to **within two weeks** of the service, again of the court's own motion, of the decision of dismissal once final.

In other words, if you brought a reinstatement action directly without applying to mediation at all and your action was dismissed on procedural grounds, you are not treated as having missed the one-month period: you may restart the process by applying to a mediator within two weeks of service of the final decision of dismissal.

The point to note is that the period runs from service of the **final** decision of dismissal; where an appeal has been lodged, finality is awaited.

## Is Limitation Suspended?

Yes — and given how narrow the one-month period is, this is decisive. In the period from the application to the mediation bureau until the date the final minutes are drawn up, **limitation is suspended and no forfeiture period runs**.

Since the process can extend to four weeks, without that protection the one-month application period would in practice be meaningless.

## What Is Negotiated in Mediation?

Three items come to the table in a reinstatement dispute, and they must be kept apart.

**Reinstatement.** The employee's actual return to work. It rarely happens in practice; a monetary solution is usually preferred.

**Idle-period pay.** Under Article 21 of the Labour Law, the employee is paid the wages and other entitlements that accrued for the period of non-employment until the decision became final, **up to a maximum of four months**.

**Compensation for failure to reinstate.** Compensation of not less than **four** and not more than **eight months'** wages, payable where the employer fails to reinstate the employee within one month of their application.

Settlement in mediation is usually built on those two monetary items. It is critical that these items be shown **separately** in the settlement document from other claims such as severance and notice pay; otherwise, a settlement on a single lump sum gives rise to argument about which claims were closed.

## The Consequence of a Reinstatement Settlement

If a settlement is reached in mediation, no action may be brought on the matters agreed. Giving up the reinstatement claim by settlement also means the end of the right to benefit from the job-security provisions.

By contrast, if the settlement document deals only with the reinstatement claim and never mentions other employee claims, a separate mediation process and judicial route remain open for those claims. In practice, combining the two applications — discussing reinstatement and employee claims in the same mediation file — is preferred both for time and for cost; but in that case the scope of the settlement document must be drafted far more carefully.

## For the Employer's Side

**The date of service of the notice of termination must be documented.** The start of the one-month period depends on it, and a limitation defence is one of the strongest defences in reinstatement files.

**Attendance is compulsory.** An employer who fails without valid excuse to attend the first meeting is liable, even if successful in the action, for half of the other side's litigation costs and half of the attorney's fee determined under the Minimum Attorney's Fee Tariff.

**The burden of proving that the termination rests on a valid reason lies with the employer** (Art. 20/2 of the Labour Law). This is the basic datum to be observed in setting the negotiating limit; a reason for termination that cannot be supported by documents turns, in the action, into compensation for failure to reinstate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: I missed the one-month period — do I have no chance at all?

Because the period is a forfeiture period, as a rule no possibility of bringing an action remains. The only exception is where you brought the action directly without applying to mediation: if the action is dismissed on procedural grounds, the decision is served of the court's own motion and a mediator may be applied to within two weeks of service of the final decision.

### Q: Should I apply separately for reinstatement and for employee claims?

Both may be advanced together in the same mediation application, and this is preferred in practice. But it must not be forgotten that the reinstatement claim is subject to the one-month period while the other claims are subject to a five-year limitation period; the time pressure comes from reinstatement.

### Q: If we settle on reinstatement in mediation, is there no need to bring an action?

Correct. Once a settlement is reached, no action may be brought on the matters agreed. Where the settlement document is signed jointly by the parties and their lawyers together with the mediator, it is deemed a document having the character of a judgment without an annotation and may be put directly to enforcement if the obligation is not performed.

### Q: Is a reinstatement claim amenable to mediation?

Yes. Article 3 of Law No. 7036 expressly brings the reinstatement claim within the scope of mandatory mediation; the claim is both amenable and subject to compulsory application to a mediator.

### Q: I am not sure of the date on the final minutes — what should I do?

Obtain the original or a mediator-certified copy immediately; the mediator is obliged to retain the documents for five years. If there is a contradiction in the date, that creates doubt as to the forfeiture period and, according to the Court of Cassation's case law, is interpreted in favour of the person against whom the period runs.
