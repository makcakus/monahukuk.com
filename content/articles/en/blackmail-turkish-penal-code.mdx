Most criminal offenses begin with conduct that is unlawful from the outset. Blackmail, as defined in Article 107 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), works differently: the person making the demand is often someone who genuinely holds the right, or owes the obligation, they are threatening to exercise. What turns that otherwise lawful position into a crime is not the underlying entitlement itself, but the decision to use it as leverage to extract an unfair advantage from another person's will. For foreign nationals and international counsel encountering a blackmail allegation in Turkey — whether as a complainant or a suspect — understanding this structure is essential, because the same statement can be charged as blackmail, as a simple threat, or as robbery depending on a handful of technical distinctions.

## The Interest Article 107 Protects

Blackmail is classified among the "Offenses Against Freedom" in the Turkish Penal Code. Its core protected interest is a person's freedom to form and express decisions without being coerced by unlawful pressure. But the offense is not one-dimensional: where the demand concerns an act or a payment, the victim's freedom of lawful conduct and property interests are at stake; where the pressure consists of a threat to disclose damaging information, the victim's honor and reputation are also protected. This dual structure allows Article 107 to reach both economically motivated coercion and reputational extortion within a single provision.

## Two Distinct Forms of the Offense

Article 107 sets out two independently punishable forms of conduct, both carrying the same statutory penalty.

### Blackmail Based on an Existing Right or Obligation

Under the first form, a person commits blackmail by invoking something they are actually entitled to do (or are actually obligated to do, or to refrain from doing) in order to compel another person into unlawful conduct, into an act they are not otherwise obligated to perform, or into providing an unjust benefit. The leverage itself is lawful — filing a criminal complaint, initiating a lawsuit, starting enforcement proceedings to collect a genuine debt, or giving testimony are all things the person is free to do. The offense is not the exercise of that right as such, but converting the choice of whether to exercise it into a bargaining chip used to extract something from the victim.

### Blackmail Targeting Honor and Reputation

The second form, added to the Code in 2005, punishes threatening to disclose or attribute facts capable of damaging a person's honor or reputation, done with the intent of securing a benefit for oneself or for someone else. Notably, the law does not require the threatened disclosure to be false. The test is whether the information is capable of harming honor or reputation, not whether it is true — so a threat to reveal accurate but damaging information falls squarely within this provision as well.

## Who Can Commit It, and How the Conduct Must Take Shape

Blackmail is not a status offense: anyone can be a perpetrator, and anyone can be a victim. The statute describes the conduct as "compelling" under the first form and "threatening" under the second, but it does not prescribe any particular method — the offense can be committed verbally, in writing, by electronic message, or through unambiguous conduct. What matters is that the communication actually reaches its target and is capable of exerting real pressure on that person's decision-making. Vague, abstract, or plainly non-serious statements do not satisfy the elements of the offense.

## Completion of the Offense and the Question of Attempt

One of the most contested aspects of blackmail concerns whether the perpetrator must actually obtain the demanded benefit for the crime to be complete. For the honor-and-reputation form, the statutory text is clear: the threat itself is enough, and the law does not require the benefit to actually be obtained. This makes it a offense of pure conduct — it is complete the moment the threat is made, even if the demand is refused outright.

The first form, built around "compelling" the victim, is read differently by different commentators. The prevailing view treats it the same way: exposing the victim to pressure capable of compelling them is sufficient, and actual compliance is not a condition for punishability. A minority view argues that "compelling" necessarily implies a result, so that the offense remains at the attempt stage unless the victim's will is actually overridden. In practice, this disagreement can be decisive in cases where the money or benefit demanded was never paid.

An attempt is possible wherever the conduct can be broken into discrete stages — for example, a blackmail message sent by post or through an intermediary that never reaches its intended recipient. Where the demand is made face-to-face, in one continuous and immediate act, there is generally no room for an attempt to be distinguished from completion.

## The Mental Element: Intent and Specific Purpose

Blackmail can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent form of the offense. Under the first form, the perpetrator must know that the entitlement they are invoking genuinely belongs to them (or is genuinely their obligation), and must be aware that they are pressuring the victim into unlawful conduct or into something the victim is not obligated to do. The second form goes further and requires a specific purpose: the perpetrator must be acting with the intent of securing a benefit for themselves or for someone else. Without that specific purpose — for instance, where the perpetrator is simply lashing out in anger with no intention of gaining anything — the conduct is not blackmail, though it may still qualify as a threat if the elements of that offense are met. The benefit pursued need not be financial; any advantage for the perpetrator or a third party falls within the scope of this intent requirement.

## Distinguishing Blackmail From Threats and From Robbery

In an ordinary threat, the perpetrator announces an unlawful harm they will inflict on the victim, and there is no requirement that the perpetrator be pursuing any personal gain. In blackmail, by contrast, the means used is lawful in principle, while the purpose behind it is not. The practical test is straightforward: does the perpetrator actually have the right to do what they say they will do? If the answer is yes, and that entitlement is being used to extract a concession, the conduct is properly classified as blackmail rather than a threat.

The line between blackmail and robbery is drawn along timing and the severity of the threat. In robbery, the use of force or threat produces its effect immediately — the taking or handing over of property occurs at once and is essentially unavoidable in that moment. In blackmail, the pressure is forward-looking, leaving the victim room to weigh options and resist, and what the perpetrator threatens to do is not a serious attack on life or physical safety but a lawful act or a disclosure they are otherwise free to make.

## Participation and Concurrence With Other Offenses

Blackmail is compatible with every form of participation in crime; a person who merely relays the blackmail message can still be held liable as a joint perpetrator or as an accessory if they acted with the requisite intent. Where the same demand is repeated against the same victim over time as part of a single ongoing scheme, the provisions on continuing offenses may apply. If the perpetrator actually follows through on the threat — publishing the alleged facts, or using intimate images or recordings as the instrument of pressure — those separate acts are assessed independently alongside the blackmail charge; they are not elements of blackmail itself but distinct unlawful acts in their own right.

## Penalty and Procedural Features

Both forms of the offense carry the same penalty: imprisonment from one to three years, together with a judicial fine of up to five thousand days. The two penalties are cumulative, not alternative — a conviction results in both being imposed together. The statute does not provide for any aggravated version tied to a specific circumstance; factors such as the perpetrator's status, the method used, or the victim's position are taken into account only when the court determines the sentence within the basic range.

Blackmail is not conditioned on a private complaint: it is investigated by the public prosecutor's office on its own initiative once it comes to light, and a victim's decision to withdraw a complaint does not by itself terminate the proceedings. It is also not among the offenses eligible for mediation. Given the upper limit of the applicable penalty, blackmail cases are heard before the courts of first instance for criminal matters (asliye ceza mahkemesi).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it still blackmail if the information the perpetrator threatens to disclose is actually true?**
Yes. Under the honor-and-reputation form of Article 107, the law asks only whether the disclosed information is capable of damaging honor or reputation, not whether it is accurate. A threat to reveal true but damaging facts, made for personal gain, still falls within the offense.

**Does the victim have to actually pay or comply for the crime to be complete?**
For the honor-and-reputation form, no — the threat itself completes the offense regardless of the outcome. For the first form, based on an existing right or obligation, the prevailing view in Turkish practice is likewise that exposing the victim to compelling pressure is enough, though a minority position ties completion to the victim's will actually being overridden.

**How is blackmail different from a criminal threat under Turkish law?**
A threat announces an unlawful harm without requiring any personal gain motive. Blackmail requires that the perpetrator be using something they are actually entitled to do, or a threat to disclose damaging information, specifically to obtain a benefit. If the perpetrator has no genuine right to what they are threatening and no gain-driven purpose, the conduct is more likely to be a threat than blackmail.

**Can a blackmail complaint be withdrawn once it has been filed?**
Withdrawal does not automatically end the case. Blackmail is investigated and prosecuted independently of a private complaint once the authorities become aware of it, and it is excluded from the mediation process available for some other offenses.
