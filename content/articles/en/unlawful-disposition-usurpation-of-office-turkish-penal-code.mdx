Foreign investors and property owners dealing with Turkish public authorities sometimes encounter officials who requisition, occupy, or otherwise dispose of movable or immovable property outside the procedure the relevant legislation actually requires — occasionally even while offering some form of payment or compensation. Turkish criminal law addresses this specific abuse under Article 261 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK), a narrow offence sitting in the part of the Code devoted to crimes against the reliability and proper functioning of public administration. A neighbouring provision, Article 262, punishes a structurally different problem — a person improperly taking up or refusing to relinquish a public post — while a third provision once grouped with them, Article 263 on unlicensed educational institutions, has been fully repealed since 2013 and no longer has any criminal effect. Understanding how these three provisions relate to, and differ from, one another is essential for anyone assessing exposure or seeking redress in this area of Turkish administrative-criminal law.

## The Shared Concern: Trust in How Public Power Is Exercised

Articles 261 through 263 of the TCK were placed together historically because each addresses a different way that the exercise of public authority can go off the rails procedurally, but they are treated in Turkish legal doctrine as entirely independent, narrowly drawn offences with their own distinct elements. Article 261 targets a public official who forces a disposition over someone else's property without following the procedure the applicable legislation prescribes. Article 262 targets the unauthorised assumption, or wrongful continuation, of a public function — and notably, unlike Article 261, it is not restricted to public officials as offenders; it can in principle be committed by anyone who takes up or clings to a public role without a lawful basis to do so. Article 263, by contrast, no longer exists as a criminal offence at all. Because the three provisions differ so sharply in who can commit them and what conduct they punish, each must be analysed on its own terms rather than as variations on a single theme.

## Article 261: Unlawful Disposition Over Another Person's Property

The interest Article 261 protects is the individual's property right against arbitrary, procedure-less interference by state power — and, more broadly, public confidence that officials will only touch private property through the channels the law actually authorises. The provision reaches a public official who, knowing that the conditions set out in the relevant legislation are not being met, forcibly disposes of someone's movable or immovable property — and critically, the offence is not avoided simply because the official pays some form of compensation for the property, even if the payment is offered in good faith. What matters is not whether money changed hands, but whether the taking or use of the property followed the procedure the law requires for that kind of official action (for example, the specific steps governing expropriation, the limits on a seizure power, or the conditions for an emergency requisition).

As an offence that can only be committed by a specific category of person, the perpetrator must be a public official who, by virtue of that role, has some practical ability to exercise power over private property. The victim is the natural or legal person whose property is disposed of improperly. Critically, mere non-compliance with procedure is not enough on its own: the disposition must also carry an element of compulsion — the property owner is not acting voluntarily but is being forced into the arrangement. Conversely, where an official seizes or temporarily uses property strictly by following the special procedure that emergency legislation (for instance, rules governing states of emergency or disaster response) itself lays down, the conduct falls outside the scope of the offence because it complies with the required procedure. The mental element required is direct intent — the official must actually be aware that the applicable legal conditions are not satisfied. An official who is genuinely unaware of the procedural defect cannot be held liable under this provision, and the offence cannot be committed through mere carelessness or negligence.

Article 261 is expressly a residual, secondary provision: where the same conduct amounts to a more serious offence — most obviously embezzlement, extortion, or something resembling robbery — that heavier charge takes over and Article 261 does not apply. This means the first analytical step in any case is always to rule out whether the conduct actually fits a graver offence. An attempt is possible where the disposition is only partially carried out, and the general rules on participation apply, meaning that private individuals who instigate or assist an official's unlawful disposition can also be held liable as accomplices.

## Article 262: Improperly Taking Up or Refusing to Leave a Public Post

Article 262 punishes two distinct forms of conduct relating to how a public function is occupied: (i) attempting to carry out a public duty in violation of the applicable laws and regulations, or (ii) continuing to exercise a public function after having been properly notified to relinquish it. The interest protected here is the principle that public duties may only be discharged by the persons and through the procedures the law designates — once it becomes unclear who is actually exercising a given function and on what legal basis, public order itself is undermined.

The two alternative forms of conduct place the offender in very different positions. Under the first, the person has no procedural standing or authority for the role at all and may never have had any prior connection to it. Under the second, the person was previously exercising the function legitimately but has since lost the legal basis to continue, having been formally notified to step down, and simply disregards that notice. Completion of the offence does not require that the function actually be carried through to a concrete result — for the first form, the statute itself uses the language of "attempting," which elevates the attempt to the completed offence. The mental element is intent: the person must be aware either that the assumption of the role is procedurally improper, or that a valid notice to vacate has been given.

Article 262 must be read strictly on its own terms and kept separate from other statutory provisions dealing with wholesale usurpation of a public role carried out with no colour of authority whatsoever. Notably, the text does not require the offender to hold the status of public official — what matters is simply that a public function has been assumed or retained improperly. Where the conduct consists of continuing in the role over time, it can be treated as a continuing offence, meaning the limitation period only begins to run once the person actually leaves the post.

## Article 263: A Repealed Provision With No Remaining Effect

Article 263, formerly titled "unlicensed educational institution," was fully repealed in 2013 by Article 13 of Law No. 6460. The consequence for criminal liability is complete decriminalisation: opening or operating an educational institution without the required authorisation is no longer a crime under the Penal Code at all, and any regulatory oversight or sanctions in this area are now handled exclusively through the relevant administrative legislation, including administrative fines. Under the "more lenient law" principle codified in Article 7 of the TCK, this repeal applies fully retroactively — even conduct that occurred before the repeal can no longer be prosecuted, pending investigations and prosecutions must be discontinued, and the enforcement of any final convictions under the old provision comes to an end.

## Penalties and Procedure

Article 261 carries a sentence of six months to two years' imprisonment, provided the conduct does not amount to a more serious offence; Article 262 carries a sentence of three months to two years. Both are comparatively low-ceiling offences that function largely as a symbolic safeguard of proper procedure in public administration rather than as serious custodial crimes in practice. Both are prosecuted ex officio — neither provision conditions prosecution on a victim's complaint, so the absence of a formal complaint does not bar an investigation. Because the conduct is connected to the exercise of a public function, the special procedural rules that apply to the investigation of public officials should be taken into account, while limitation periods and the competent court follow the general rules of Turkish criminal procedure. In practice, these two offences are frequently examined alongside more serious charges such as misconduct in public office, embezzlement, or extortion, and where such an overlap exists, the more serious offence always takes priority — a rule the legislation states explicitly for Article 261.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an official avoid liability under Article 261 by paying the property owner for the item taken?**
No. The offence does not require that the property be taken without any compensation. Even a good-faith payment does not remove liability if the disposition itself was forced and did not follow the procedure the applicable legislation requires.

**Can a private individual, rather than a public official, be prosecuted under Article 261?**
Not as the principal offender — Article 261 can only be committed by a public official acting in that capacity. A private person who instigates or knowingly assists the official's unlawful disposition can, however, be held liable as an accomplice under the general rules on participation.

**Is opening an unlicensed private school still a criminal offence in Turkey?**
No. The provision that once criminalised this, Article 263 of the Penal Code, was fully repealed in 2013. Oversight of unlicensed educational institutions today is handled through administrative regulation and administrative fines, not the Penal Code, and this applies even to past conduct.

**What happens if the same facts could support both Article 261 and a charge like embezzlement or extortion?**
The more serious offence takes priority. Article 261 is explicitly secondary: if the conduct meets the elements of a heavier crime, that charge applies instead, and Article 261 falls away.
