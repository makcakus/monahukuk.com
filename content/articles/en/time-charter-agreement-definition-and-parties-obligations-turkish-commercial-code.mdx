TTK Art. 1131-1135 open the Code's chapter on time charter — a contract that looks similar to bareboat charter on the surface but rests on a fundamentally different legal structure.

## The Key Split: Commercial vs. Technical Management

Under Art. 1131, in a time charter the disponent owner ("tahsis eden") hands over only the *commercial* management of an already-manned, ready ship for a fee — deciding what cargo to carry, which ports to call at, which carriage contracts to sign. Unlike bareboat charter, the disponent owner keeps technical management: crewing, physical navigation, seaworthiness. Art. 1131(2) makes this explicit — whoever holds technical management is deemed the ship's *possessor*, meaning possession never passes to the charterer at all. This single distinction — possession and technical control staying with the disponent owner — is the structural fault line separating time charter from bareboat charter throughout the entire chapter.

## Split Command Over the Captain

Art. 1133 requires the disponent owner to have the ship ready at the agreed time and place, and — critically — to keep it seaworthy for the *entire contract term*, not just at delivery (contrast this with the delivery-only warranty in bareboat charter). Art. 1134 gives the charterer full commercial management, and requires the captain to follow the charterer's commercial instructions under the contract — while remaining, in every technical and navigational respect, the disponent owner's employee. The captain is thus split by subject matter: technical command stays with the owner, commercial routing instructions come from the charterer.

## Running Costs Follow Commercial Control

Art. 1135 requires the charterer to bear all costs of commercial operation, especially fuel — a direct consequence of the charterer deciding how often and where the ship sails. Costs tied to technical management (maintenance, classification, crew wages) remain with the disponent owner. Fuel quality disputes are a recurring flashpoint here: Turkish courts have held the charterer liable for damage from off-spec fuel it supplied, since fuel procurement duty sits with the charterer and the crew typically has no means to test fuel quality before use.

## Practical Takeaway

For time charterers, this cost allocation means budgeting for fuel and voyage-related costs is a core contractual obligation — and fuel quality control, since the ship's crew cannot verify it, becomes a real commercial risk worth addressing contractually.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a time charter agreement?

Under TTK Article 1131/1, a time charter agreement is <b>a contract under which the disponent owner undertakes to place the commercial management of a manned vessel at the disposal of the charterer for a specified period and in return for hire</b>. Three elements are determinative: the vessel being <b>manned</b> (together with its crew), the transfer of <b>commercial management</b> only, and this being done <b>for a specified period</b>.

### How is technical management distinguished from commercial management?

Under TTK Article 1133/1, <b>the disponent owner assumes the technical management of the vessel</b>: it is their responsibility to keep the vessel seaworthy, fit for the voyage and fit for the cargo, to employ the crew, and to provide maintenance and repair. Under Article 1134/1, <b>the commercial management of the vessel belongs to the charterer</b>: it is the charterer's decision which voyages are to be undertaken, which ports are to be called at, and which cargo is to be taken on board. Under Article 1134/2, the master is obliged to comply with instructions relating to commercial management.

### Who bears the fuel costs?

The charterer. Under TTK Article 1135/1, <b>the charterer bears all expenses arising from the commercial operation of the vessel, including in particular the expenses necessary for supplying fuel of a quality and quantity sufficient to ensure the regular operation of the machinery</b>. This also includes port and canal dues, loading and unloading expenses, and agency costs. Crew wages, insurance and maintenance expenses, on the other hand, belong to the disponent owner as part of technical management.
