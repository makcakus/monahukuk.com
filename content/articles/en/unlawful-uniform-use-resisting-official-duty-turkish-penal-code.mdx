Foreign residents and visitors involved in an altercation with Turkish police, gendarmerie or municipal enforcement officers are sometimes surprised at how broadly Turkish criminal law defines "interference" with a public official's duties — and at how a seemingly separate issue, wearing an unauthorized uniform or insignia, is regulated by a neighboring provision built on the same underlying concern. Articles 264, 265 and 266 of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK") sit together in the same chapter because each protects a different facet of the same interest: the public's ability to trust the outward appearance of state authority, and the ability of public officials to actually carry out their duties without being physically obstructed or having their own official tools turned against the public. This article explains each of the three offenses in plain terms for an international audience navigating a Turkish criminal file.

## Wearing an Official Uniform or Insignia Without Authority (Article 264)

Article 264(1) criminalizes publicly wearing the official uniform of a rank, public office or profession without authorization in a manner that is likely to mislead others, or wearing decorations or medals one is not entitled to. The offense does not require the person to falsely claim to hold the position in question — what matters is that the outward appearance is capable of creating a misleading impression in the eyes of the public. This is why the law protects a collective interest (public trust in the visible markers of official status) rather than a specific individual, and why anyone can commit it and, in principle, anyone in the public can be misled by it.

Two conditions narrow the scope of the offense considerably. First, the conduct must occur "publicly" — wearing a uniform in a private setting, such as a costume rehearsal or a private party, generally falls outside the provision. Second, the circumstances must be such that the conduct is capable of misleading others; a context that makes clear the wearer is not actually claiming the office (a clearly theatrical or satirical setting, for example) does not fit the intended target of the rule. The offense requires intent — the person must be aware of, and want, the misleading impression the conduct is likely to create; Turkish criminal doctrine also accepts that this can be satisfied by a lower threshold of intent (dolus eventualis) in appropriate cases. The penalty under the first paragraph is imprisonment from three months to one year.

Article 264(2) addresses a further scenario: if the unauthorized uniform is actually used to facilitate the commission of another crime, the sentence for the uniform offense itself is increased by one third. Importantly, this increase concerns only the uniform-related conduct — the other crime that the uniform helped facilitate is punished separately and cumulatively under its own provisions. The logic mirrors the approach taken in Article 266 (discussed below): using a trust-based advantage — here, a misleading appearance of official authority — as a tool for further wrongdoing is treated as an aggravating factor rather than folded into a single combined charge.

## Resisting a Public Official's Duty With Force or Threats (Article 265)

Article 265(1) punishes the use of force or threats against a public official with the specific purpose of preventing that official from carrying out their duty, with a penalty of six months to three years' imprisonment. The interest protected here is the uninterrupted, unobstructed performance of public duties, and the victim is the official actually carrying out the task in question.

What distinguishes this offense from a simple verbal outburst — and, more importantly, from the separate crime of insulting a public official under Article 125 of the Penal Code — is the requirement of actual force or a genuine threat, not merely disrespectful or offensive language. Where Article 125 addresses conduct that wounds an official's honor or dignity, Article 265 is aimed at conduct that seeks to physically or psychologically stop the official from performing the act itself. The offense also requires a specific intent: the force or threat must be used precisely "in order to prevent the performance of the duty." Where that particular purpose is absent, the same physical acts are assessed instead under the general provisions on intentional injury or threats.

A point debated in Turkish legal scholarship is whether the offense requires the official to actually be stopped from performing the duty. The prevailing view treats Article 265 as a conduct offense assessed by reference to risk rather than a result-based offense: using force or a threat with the purpose of obstruction is enough to complete the crime, regardless of whether the obstruction actually succeeds. This view also affects how far an attempt at this offense can meaningfully be prosecuted, since an offense that does not require a specific result leaves little room for a distinct "attempt" stage.

The aggravating circumstances of Article 265 scale the penalty according to several factors:

- If committed against a person performing a judicial function, the penalty rises to two to four years (paragraph 2).
- If the offender disguises themselves to avoid recognition, or the act is committed jointly by more than one person, the penalty is increased by one third (paragraph 3).
- If committed with a weapon, or by drawing on the intimidating power of an actual or presumed criminal organization, the penalty is increased by one half (paragraph 4). This last aggravator is particularly significant where resistance to law enforcement is carried out as an extension of an armed organization's policy of using force — in such cases the surrounding organizational pressure on society, not merely the individual offender's own conduct, is what justifies the heavier increase.
- Paragraph 5 clarifies how this offense combines with intentional injury: if the resistance results in an aggravated form of intentional injury, the provisions on intentional injury apply in addition to Article 265, rather than being absorbed into a single increased sentence. This is a case of genuine cumulation of two separate offenses, not a single aggravated penalty.

## Misuse of Official Equipment to Commit a Crime (Article 266)

Article 266 addresses a different concern: a public official who uses tools or equipment held by virtue of their office to commit a crime. Where the public-official status of the offender is not already a defining element of the underlying offense, the sentence for that offense is increased by one third. This is not a freestanding crime but a sentencing aggravator — the underlying offense (theft, injury, or any crime other than one where official capacity is already a constituent element, such as embezzlement) is first punished according to its own rules, and only then increased because the official misused equipment tied to their position.

The condition that the official capacity not already form part of the underlying offense's definition matters because applying the same characteristic twice — once to establish the crime, and again to aggravate its punishment — would otherwise violate the principle against double punishment for the same element (non bis in idem). This is why Article 266 has no role to play in an embezzlement case, for instance, where the offender's status as a public official is already baked into the definition of the crime itself. The provision also does not require that the official have received the equipment for safekeeping or have any special custodial responsibility over it; it is enough that the item was in the official's hands by reason of their duty at the relevant time. This connection between the office and the tool used is the threshold condition for the aggravator, and it confines Article 266 to cases where the specific opportunities created by public office are what made the crime possible.

## How These Three Provisions Fit Together

Although Articles 264, 265 and 266 punish conduct of very different character — a misleading appearance, physical resistance, and misuse of an official tool — they share a common thread: each protects a different dimension of how a democratic state exercises authority through its officials. Article 264 protects the public's ability to trust what an official uniform or insignia signals. Article 265 protects an official's practical ability to carry out an assigned task without being forced to stop. Article 266 protects against the specific risk that access granted by public office will be turned into an instrument of crime. Because these three offenses are frequently at issue in the same set of facts — for example, an altercation during a police stop that escalates into a resistance charge — distinguishing which provision (or combination of provisions) actually applies is often the decisive step in building a defense or evaluating exposure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is insulting a police officer during an argument the same offense as resisting under Article 265?**
No. Offensive or disrespectful language toward an official is assessed under the separate offense of insulting a public official (Article 125 of the Penal Code). Article 265 requires actual force or a genuine threat used with the specific purpose of preventing the official from carrying out their duty — verbal insult alone does not meet this threshold.

**Does a person have to actually stop a police officer from completing a task for the resistance offense to be complete?**
According to the prevailing view in Turkish criminal law scholarship, no. Article 265 is treated as complete once force or a threat is used for the purpose of obstruction, regardless of whether the official was actually prevented from finishing the task.

**Can wearing an unauthorized uniform in a private, non-misleading context still be prosecuted under Article 264?**
Generally, no. The offense requires that the conduct occur publicly and be capable of misleading others. A private setting where no one is realistically misled about the wearer's actual status — a costume party or a theatrical rehearsal, for example — does not fit the conduct the provision targets.

**If a public official commits a crime using equipment issued for their job, is that always punished under Article 266?**
Only when the official's public capacity is not already a defining element of the underlying crime. If official status is itself part of what makes the underlying offense a crime — as with embezzlement — Article 266 does not apply on top of it, since that would penalize the same characteristic twice.
