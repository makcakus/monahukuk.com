Turkish criminal law treats attempts to overthrow or forcibly obstruct the constitutional organs of the state as among the most severe offenses in the entire Penal Code. Articles 309 through 313 of the Turkish Penal Code ("TCK") criminalize violent attacks on the constitutional order itself, on the President, on Parliament, on the Government, and armed insurrection against the state. These provisions are frequently invoked in politically charged investigations, and foreign nationals — whether residents, business figures, or individuals connected to organizations under scrutiny — can find themselves swept into proceedings under these articles even without having personally used force. This article explains what each provision actually requires, how Turkish courts distinguish it from lesser offenses, and what defendants and their counsel should understand about the process.

## The Protected Interest: Why These Are Offenses Against the State, Not Individuals

Articles 309-313 sit within the part of the Penal Code addressing offenses against the sovereignty symbols and organs of the state. What they protect is not the physical safety of any particular office-holder, but the ability of the constitutionally established organs — the legislature, the executive, and the presidency — to actually function according to the process the Constitution sets out. This is why the victim in a legal sense is the state itself, not an individual, and why these offenses are prosecuted regardless of any private complaint. The underlying principle across all five articles is the same: political power in Turkey may only change hands through lawful, peaceful means, and any attempt to force that change through violence falls within this chapter, regardless of which specific organ is targeted.

## The Common Element: Force and Violence

Every core offense in this group — attacking the constitutional order (Article 309), attacking the legislature (Article 311), and attacking the government (Article 312) — is built around the same essential element: the use of force and violence. Political opposition, criticism of the government, peaceful assembly and demonstration, or pursuing a change of power through lawful and legitimate channels fall entirely outside these provisions. For any of these offenses to be established, the prosecution must show that the accused resorted to force and violence, and that this force was of a kind genuinely capable of neutralizing the targeted constitutional organ. Where the conduct amounts to no more than verbal threats or purely symbolic acts, Turkish courts and legal commentary treat that as falling short of this threshold, and the conduct may instead fall under separate, less severe offenses such as resisting a public official in the performance of duty.

## Attacking the Constitutional Order (Article 309): Why an Attempt Is Treated as a Completed Crime

Article 309 punishes anyone who uses force and violence to abolish the constitutional order established by the Turkish Constitution, replace it with a different order, or prevent that order from actually functioning. The distinctive feature of this provision is that the law criminalizes the attempt itself as the completed offense — it does not require that the accused actually succeeded in changing or dismantling the constitutional order. Once violent acts directed toward that goal have begun, the offense is already complete; there is no need to prove the coup or insurrection actually succeeded. This is a notable departure from the general rules on attempted offenses elsewhere in the Penal Code, and it means criminal liability can attach at a very early stage of the conduct. Where other crimes — such as homicide, injury, or damage to public property — are committed in the course of this offense, Turkish law requires that those crimes be punished separately and cumulatively, rather than being absorbed into the Article 309 sentence. Legal entities implicated in this offense are subject to their own specific security measures rather than criminal penalties, since criminal liability under Turkish law attaches only to natural persons.

## Attacks on the President (Article 310): Protecting the Office, Not Just the Person

Article 310 addresses violent attacks directed at the President personally, treating them as an assault on the highest representative office of the state. The doctrinal point worth noting is that what the law protects is not primarily the President's personal physical safety — that is already covered by ordinary offenses against the person — but the continuity and authority of the office itself, which is why this provision sits among the offenses against the state rather than among crimes against individuals. The article distinguishes two levels of conduct. An assassination of the President is punished at the most severe level, and even an attempt is treated as a completed offense in the same way as Article 309. A violent attack that falls short of an assassination attempt is punished by increasing the sentence applicable to the underlying offense, with a statutory floor ensuring that the final sentence cannot fall below a set minimum term. This two-tier structure allows the same article to capture both the most extreme scenario and lesser, though still serious, physical attacks on the President.

## Attacks on Parliament and on the Government (Articles 311-312)

Article 311 criminalizes using force and violence to abolish the Grand National Assembly of Turkey or to prevent it from carrying out its duties, in whole or in part. What is protected here is the functioning of the body in which the national will is embodied. As with Article 309, an attempt is sufficient — the accused need not have actually dissolved Parliament or halted its legislative activity, so long as violent acts aimed at that outcome were undertaken. Article 312 mirrors this structure for the executive branch, punishing the use of force and violence to abolish the Government or prevent it from performing its duties, in whole or in part. The target here must be the Government acting collectively; an individual act of force aimed at obstructing a single minister or official does not fall under this article but under separate, narrower provisions dealing with obstruction of individual public officials. Both articles apply the same rule on cumulative punishment for other offenses committed in the course of the attack, and Turkish appellate case law has drawn a further distinction: where the attack actually succeeds in disrupting the constitutional order itself, the more serious Article 309 offense applies instead of Article 312, which is reserved for cases where the Government's functioning is obstructed without the broader constitutional order being disrupted.

## Armed Insurrection (Article 313): Distinguishing Instigators, Leaders and Participants

Unlike the preceding articles, Article 313 does not describe a single act but a tiered structure of liability depending on the accused's role. It punishes, first, anyone who incites the public to armed insurrection against the Government, with the applicable penalty depending on whether the insurrection actually takes place — the instigator's liability increases if the incitement leads to an actual uprising. Second, the article separately punishes whoever actually directs or leads the insurrection, treating that role as the most serious, while treating mere participants in the uprising more leniently. This creates a functional hierarchy of responsibility: instigator, leader, and ordinary participant are each assessed differently rather than under one undifferentiated standard. A further provision treats it as an aggravating factor where the insurrection is carried out by exploiting circumstances created by a state of war. As with the other articles in this chapter, any other crimes committed during the insurrection are punished separately and in addition. This graduated structure means that two people who took part in the same uprising can face very different sentences depending on whether they merely joined in, actively directed events, or incited others from the outset.

## Intent, Investigation and the Role of Causation

All of these offenses require intent — the accused must have acted knowingly and willfully to use force against the constitutional order or its organs. None of them can be committed negligently. Because the victim is the state, these are not offenses subject to private complaint; prosecutors pursue them on their own initiative regardless of whether any individual comes forward. Given the gravity of the charges and their direct bearing on public order, Turkish practice places considerable weight on how precisely the prosecution establishes who used force, in what manner, and against which specific organ — vague or generalized allegations of participation in a broader plot are not a substitute for concrete evidence of the accused's own conduct. Establishing a clear causal link between the force used and the constitutional organ it was allegedly directed against is often the central battleground in these cases, and it is where a well-prepared defense can have the most impact.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a person have to succeed in overthrowing the government to be convicted under Article 309?**
No. Turkish law treats the attempt itself as the completed offense. Once violent acts aimed at abolishing or replacing the constitutional order have begun, criminal liability attaches even if the attempt ultimately fails.

**Is peaceful political opposition or protest covered by these articles?**
No. All of these offenses require the use of force and violence directed at neutralizing a constitutional organ. Criticism of the government, lawful protest, or pursuing political change through legal means falls entirely outside their scope.

**What is the difference between being charged under Article 309 and under Article 312?**
Article 312 applies where force is used to obstruct the Government's functioning without disrupting the broader constitutional order. If the force actually succeeds in disrupting that constitutional order itself, Turkish courts have held that the more serious Article 309 applies instead.

**Can someone be convicted under Article 313 just for participating in an uprising without leading it?**
Yes, but the law distinguishes roles carefully. Ordinary participants are punished more leniently than those who actually directed or led the insurrection, and instigators face a penalty that depends on whether the incitement actually resulted in an uprising.
