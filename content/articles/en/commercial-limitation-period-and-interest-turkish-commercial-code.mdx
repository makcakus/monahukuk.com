Articles 6 through 10 of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK) address three practical questions that arise in nearly every commercial dispute: how strictly a limitation period can be altered by contract, whether multiple debtors on a commercial obligation are jointly liable by default, and how interest is calculated and when it starts to run.

## Limitation Periods Cannot Be Shortened or Extended by Contract

Under TTK Art. 6, limitation periods set by statutes containing commercial provisions are mandatory unless the law itself allows otherwise — they cannot be shortened or lengthened by agreement. This is a notable departure from the general obligations regime, where limitation periods are typically only relatively mandatory. Periods such as the one- and three-year limitation windows for unfair competition claims, or the five-year period for settling a current account relationship, fall squarely within this rule.

## The Presumption of Joint and Several Liability (Art. 7)

Where two or more people jointly incur an obligation and the matter is a commercial transaction for at least one of them, TTK Art. 7 makes them jointly and severally liable by default — unless the law or their contract says otherwise. This reverses the general civil law rule, under which joint liability normally requires an explicit agreement. The presumption also extends to guarantors of a commercial debt, which means the strict formal requirements the Code of Obligations imposes for a "joint and several" guarantee (such as a handwritten notation) do not apply to commercial guarantees — Art. 7 as special law displaces them.

## Freedom to Set Interest, and the Narrow Exception for Compound Interest (Art. 8-9)

Art. 8 allows interest rates in commercial transactions to be freely agreed, disapplying the caps that otherwise limit contractual and default interest. This freedom is not unlimited: Turkish courts will still reduce an excessive contractual penalty or interest rate ex officio where it would financially ruin the debtor. Compound interest is prohibited as a general rule, with a narrow exception for current-account and mutual-loan arrangements between two merchants, subject to a minimum three-month compounding interval; interest applied in violation of this rule is treated as void. Consumer protection rules always take precedence where applicable. For default interest specifically, Art. 9 refers back to general legislation, meaning the statutory caps on default interest still apply even in commercial dealings.

## When Interest Starts to Run (Art. 10)

Absent an agreement to the contrary, interest on a commercial debt starts running from the due date if one was fixed, or from formal notice (ihtar) if it was not.

Foreign businesses lending to or contracting with Turkish merchants should note that while interest rates can be freely negotiated, courts retain discretion to intervene against oppressive rates — freedom of contract in Turkish commercial law is real but not absolute.
