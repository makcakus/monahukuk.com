TTK Art. 654-657 govern registered instruments (nama yazılı senet) — the least freely transferable of the three negotiable instrument categories under Turkish law, sitting between bearer instruments (easiest to transfer) and order instruments.

## Definition: A Positive and a Negative Requirement

Art. 654 defines a registered instrument by two conditions: it is issued in a specific person's name, and it neither contains an "order" clause nor is legally classified as an order instrument (such as a bill of exchange, promissory note, or check, which are order instruments by default). Turkish courts apply this second condition strictly — a note that is by law an order instrument (e.g., a promissory note) only becomes registered if a "non-negotiable" clause is inserted by the original issuer; the same clause added later by an endorser only binds that endorser personally and does not strip the instrument of its negotiable character.

## Burden of Proof and Transfer as Assignment

Because a registered instrument names a specific holder, Art. 655 requires a debtor to pay only someone who is both the physical holder and the named party (or their legal successor) — mere possession, sufficient for a bearer instrument, is not enough here. A debtor who pays without this proof is not discharged against the true owner. This heightened proof requirement is why Turkish courts treat transfer of a registered instrument as governed by the ordinary rules on assignment of claims: the debtor may raise against the transferee any defense that was valid against the transferor, including defenses arising anywhere earlier in a chain of successive transfers.

## The "Pay to Any Holder" Exception

Art. 656 recognizes a middle ground: if the instrument itself reserves the debtor's right to pay any holder in good faith, without demanding proof of identity, the debtor is discharged by a good-faith payment to whoever presents it — though the debtor is never obligated to use this shortcut and may still insist on the stricter proof under Art. 655.

## Cancellation

Absent special provisions, registered instruments are cancelled following the same procedure as bearer instruments (Art. 657), with the debtor free to simplify the cancellation notice requirements or, alternatively, to reserve the right to pay directly against a formal proof-of-payment document without needing a court cancellation order at all.

## Practical Takeaway

A party receiving a registered instrument as part of a Turkish transaction should treat the transfer as an assignment of claim, not a clean negotiable transfer — meaning any defense the debtor could have raised against a prior holder can follow the instrument down the chain to the current holder.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a registered instrument?

Under TTK Article 654/1 <b>a negotiable instrument that is drawn in the name of a specific person, does not contain an order clause, and is not deemed to be an order instrument by law</b> is a registered instrument. The definition has two elements: the <b>positive</b> element is that the instrument is made out to a specific person; the <b>negative</b> element is that it carries no order clause and is not treated as an order instrument by statute. Bills of exchange, promissory notes and cheques, being order instruments by law, fall outside this category.

### How is a registered instrument transferred?

Not by endorsement, but under the rules on <b>assignment of a claim</b>, together with delivery of the instrument. Under TTK Article 655/1 <b>the debtor is obliged to pay only to persons who hold the instrument and are the person named in it, or who prove that they are that person's legal successor</b>. For this reason a registered instrument circulates markedly less freely than a bearer or order instrument; in return, the debtor's defences are more broadly protected.

### What is an incomplete registered instrument?

A special type regulated in TTK Article 656/1: <b>where a registered instrument reserves for the debtor the right to pay its value to any holder, the debtor is discharged by a payment made in good faith to the holder even without requiring proof of their status as creditor</b>. The instrument retains its registered character, but the debtor is given the possibility of discharging the debt by paying the holder. Bank passbooks are the classic example of this type.
