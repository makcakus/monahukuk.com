Foreign nationals living, working or investing in Turkey sometimes first encounter the Turkish criminal justice system not through a courtroom, but through a knock on the door: police arriving to search a home, an office, or a vehicle. Search and seizure are among the oldest and most intrusive investigative tools available to Turkish prosecutors and law enforcement, and precisely because they reach into private life, property and business premises before any court has weighed the evidence, the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (CMK) surrounds them with detailed conditions. Understanding those conditions — who may order a search, what must be written into the order, and what happens to evidence gathered outside the rules — matters enormously for anyone facing a criminal investigation in Turkey, or advising someone who is.

## The Threshold for Searching a Suspect: Reasonable Suspicion

Article 116 CMK sets the trigger for searching a suspect or defendant's person, belongings, home, workplace or other premises: there must be reasonable suspicion that the person could be apprehended there or that evidence of the offense could be found there. This is a lower bar than the "strong suspicion based on concrete evidence" required for measures like arrest, but it is still more than a hunch — it must rest on identifiable facts connecting the place or person to the offense under investigation, not merely an investigator's subjective impression. Interestingly, the legislature has moved this threshold up and down over successive amendments, at one point raising it to the stricter "strong suspicion" standard before lowering it back to "reasonable suspicion" — a back-and-forth that reflects an ongoing policy debate between investigative efficiency and the intensity of interference with personal liberty. It is also worth noting that Article 116 only covers the suspect or defendant's own person and property; searching a third party's premises requires the separate, and stricter, conditions set out for third-party searches, which apply only when the search targets a specific identified person or a specific identified item.

## Who May Order a Search: Judge, Prosecutor, or Police Superior

Article 119 CMK establishes a tiered authorization system. As a rule, only a judge may order a search. Where delay would be prejudicial, a public prosecutor may issue the order instead, and if the prosecutor cannot be reached, a police superior may issue a written order — but any search carried out on that basis must be reported immediately to the chief public prosecutor's office. Crucially, the law carves out an important exception: a home, a workplace, or any enclosed space not open to the public may only be searched pursuant to a judicial decision, or, where delay would be prejudicial, a written order from the prosecutor. A police superior's written order is not sufficient authority to search these categories of premises. Whatever form the authorization takes, it must specify the act giving rise to the search, identify the person to be searched or the address of the premises (or the item sought), and state how long the authorization remains valid; a gap in any of these particulars can render the order too vague to be lawful. Where a search of a home, workplace or other enclosed space is carried out without the public prosecutor present, two members of the local council of elders or two neighbors must be present as witnesses — a procedural safeguard intended to keep the search transparent and open to later scrutiny. Established case law of the Court of Cassation's General Assembly on Criminal Matters holds that where the circumstances call for a judicial search order and none was obtained, the search is unlawful, and evidence gathered that way cannot form the basis of a conviction under Article 38(6) of the Constitution together with Articles 206(2)(a) and 217(2) CMK — regardless of whether the defendant later confessed.

## What Happens to Seized Items: Custody and Return

Article 123 CMK governs the fate of items recovered during a search. Property that appears useful as evidence, or that could be subject to confiscation, is taken into official custody; if the person holding it will not hand it over voluntarily, it may be formally seized. Seizure is meant to be temporary. Once its purpose has been served — the investigation concludes, the item no longer has evidentiary value, or confiscation is no longer warranted — the item is, in principle, returned to its owner. Anyone whose property has been seized may at any time ask a judge to rule on the matter under Article 127(4), a safeguard that prevents a seizure from simply continuing indefinitely without judicial oversight.

## Authorizing a Seizure and the Strict Time Limits That Follow

Seizure decisions follow the same tiered structure as search orders: a judge, or, where delay would be prejudicial, the public prosecutor, or, if the prosecutor cannot be reached, a police superior acting by written order. But a seizure carried out without a prior judicial decision is on a short clock — under Article 127(3), it must be submitted for a judge's approval within twenty-four hours, and the judge must rule within forty-eight hours of the seizure; if no ruling is issued in time, the seizure automatically lapses. This tight timetable exists precisely to stop a measure that began outside judicial control from continuing unchecked. The identity of the officer carrying out the seizure must also be recorded in the official minutes, so the measure remains open to later review. The Court of Cassation's 7th Criminal Chamber has underscored the same point from a different angle, holding that a search carried out without a written search order — issued either by a court or, in urgent circumstances, by the public prosecutor — is procedurally unlawful, and a conviction cannot be based on evidence obtained that way.

## Digital Evidence: The Separate, Stricter Regime for Computers

Because digital data is both fragile and unusually revealing of private life, Article 134 CMK deliberately departs from the ordinary search-and-seizure rules and imposes a considerably stricter regime for searching, copying and seizing computers, computer programs and computer records. Applying this article requires more than reasonable suspicion — it demands "strong suspicion based on concrete evidence" (the higher threshold otherwise reserved for measures like arrest) together with a finding that the evidence could not realistically be obtained by any other means, an ultima ratio requirement built directly into the statute. The decision must, as a rule, come from a judge; where a prosecutor issues it due to urgency, that decision must be submitted to a judge for approval within twenty-four hours. If digital evidence cannot be decrypted or accessed, or if the process would take too long on-site, the hardware itself may be seized to allow the work to be completed off-site, but it must be returned without delay once decryption and copying are complete. During any seizure of computer data, a full backup of all system data must be made, a copy of that backup must be given to the suspect or their lawyer with the exchange recorded in an official minute, and — importantly — investigators may also copy some or all of the data without seizing the hardware at all, provided the copying is likewise documented and signed. The Court of Cassation's 16th Criminal Chamber has explained in detail that preserving the integrity of digital evidence requires taking a forensic image on-site at the time of seizure, giving a copy of that image to the suspect or counsel, and sealing the original media so it remains available for inspection; a process that skips these steps violates Article 134 and renders the resulting evidence unusable as the sole basis for a conviction. Notably, a search order issued under the general Articles 116 and 119 does not by itself authorize searching computers — even where a general search order covers a workplace, seizing the computers found there without a separate Article 134 decision produces unlawfully obtained evidence.

## The Consequences: Absolute Versus Relative Procedural Defects

The most concrete consequence of a defective search or seizure is whether the resulting evidence can be used at trial at all. Turkish doctrine and Court of Cassation case law distinguish between "absolutely unlawful evidence" and "relatively unlawful evidence"; where the defect is absolute, Articles 206(2) and 217 CMK bar any conviction from resting on that evidence, full stop. The Court of Cassation's General Assembly on Criminal Matters has confirmed this repeatedly: where the situation called for a judicial search order and none was obtained, the resulting search is unlawful and the evidence it produced cannot support a conviction. This body of case law makes clear that search and seizure rules are not mere administrative formalities — a breach can directly determine the outcome of a criminal case, sometimes years after the search itself took place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can police search my home in Turkey without a court order?**
Only in limited circumstances. As a rule, a home search requires a judge's decision. Where delay would be prejudicial, a public prosecutor may authorize it in writing instead, but a verbal instruction or an order from a police superior alone is not sufficient for a home, workplace, or other enclosed private space.

**If police search my office computers, do they need a separate authorization from the one covering the office itself?**
Yes. A general search order for a workplace does not extend to the computers found there. Searching, copying or seizing computer data requires a separate decision under Article 134, which applies a stricter standard than an ordinary search and generally requires a finding that the evidence could not be obtained any other way.

**What happens to evidence taken in a search that did not follow the proper procedure?**
It depends on the nature of the defect, but where the procedural breach is treated as absolute — for example, no judicial search order was obtained when one was required — Turkish law and settled Court of Cassation case law bar that evidence from being used to support a conviction, regardless of what it shows.

**How long can the authorities hold onto property or devices seized during an investigation?**
Not indefinitely. A seizure made without prior judicial approval must be submitted to a judge within twenty-four hours, and the judge must decide within forty-eight hours or the seizure lapses automatically. Once the evidentiary or confiscation purpose no longer applies, the property should be returned; unreasonable delay in returning seized items can itself give rise to a compensation claim.
