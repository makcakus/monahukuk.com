Foreign media companies, news agencies and press professionals working in Turkey are often surprised to learn that Turkish journalists are not employed under the general Labour Law (Law No. 4857) at all. Instead, editorial staff at newspapers, news websites, periodicals and news or photo agencies fall under a dedicated statute: Law No. 5953 of 13 June 1952, commonly known as the Press Labour Law. The legislature carved out this separate regime because journalism has features that do not fit neatly into an ordinary employment relationship — intellectual and creative labour predominates over routine tasks, careers are tied closely to professional reputation, and the profession's public-information function intersects with the public interest. This article explains who qualifies as a "journalist" under this law, how the regime differs from standard Turkish labour law on severance, notice and termination, and what employers and press employees in Turkey need to know in practice.

## Who Counts as a "Journalist" Under Law No. 5953

Article 1 of the Press Labour Law defines its scope as covering people who perform intellectual and artistic work of any kind at newspapers, internet news sites and periodicals published in Turkey, or at news and photo agencies, and who fall outside the "employee" definition used in the general Labour Law. That wording sounds circular at first, but it reflects a deliberate line: a "journalist" is a distinct category, separate from an ordinary employee, and the decisive test is whether the person's work is "intellectual and artistic" in nature. Turkish Court of Cassation case law consistently treats this test as meaning direct performance of journalistic work itself, and it has repeatedly held that writers, reporters, editors, proofreaders, translators, photographers, camera operators, illustrators and cartoonists satisfy this test and qualify as journalists. By contrast, staff in a press organisation's advertising, marketing, sales, personnel or accounting departments, and administrative or secretarial staff, remain outside the definition even if their work has an intellectual component — they continue to be governed by the ordinary Labour Law. The purpose of the Press Labour Law is to protect people who are directly engaged in intellectual journalistic work in view of the profession's particular risks and uncertainties, which is why courts interpret its scope narrowly and by reference to the actual nature of the work performed.

## Who Is Excluded: Civil Servants and Public-Sector Personnel

Article 2 of the law carves out an exception of its own. Even where someone would otherwise meet the Article 1 definition, civil servants and public personnel employed by the State, provincial administrations, municipalities, state economic enterprises, or companies in which such entities hold more than half of the capital, are excluded from the Press Labour Law's coverage. The rationale is straightforward: these individuals are already subject to their own separate legal regime — civil service law or the relevant institution's own personnel rules — so applying a second, overlapping employment statute to them would be unnecessary and inconsistent. In practice this means that a press employee doing identical editorial work may be covered by entirely different rules depending on whether their employer is a private publisher or a public institution.

## Seniority-Based Severance: How It Differs From the General Labour Law

The Press Labour Law's severance regime — often referred to in practice as "journalist's severance compensation" — diverges from the general Labour Law's severance rules in two important respects, both rooted in Article 6. The first difference concerns how seniority is calculated. Under the general Labour Law, seniority is limited to the time an employee has spent with the same employer at the same workplace. Article 6 of the Press Labour Law instead measures a journalist's seniority from the date the person first entered the profession. Turkish Court of Cassation decisions confirm this consistently, holding that a journalist's professional seniority reflects the total time spent in the profession, and that for severance and annual leave purposes this seniority runs from the journalist's first entry into journalism — meaning that even a change of employer does not reset the clock. This gives journalists a broader form of protection than employees under the general regime enjoy.

The second difference lies in the severance rate itself. Article 6 sets the compensation at one month's pay for each full or partial year of service, calculated on the journalist's final salary. While this nominally mirrors the general Labour Law's 30-day-wage formula, because a journalist's professional seniority survives a change of employer, in practice a journalist can accumulate a materially higher total entitlement than an ordinary employee with the same number of years at a single company. The law also sets a two-tier notice period tied to a seniority threshold rather than the graduated scale used under the general Labour Law: journalists with at least five years of uninterrupted service are entitled to three months' written notice before termination takes effect, while those with less than five years' service are entitled to one month. The final paragraph of Article 6 further provides that the job-security provisions of the general Labour Law (Articles 18, 19, 20, 21 and 29, covering unfair dismissal protection and collective dismissal rules) apply to journalists by analogy, meaning journalists can, under certain conditions, also benefit from job-security protection.

## The 2023 Constitutional Court Ruling: No More Five-Year Threshold

Until 2023, the version of Article 6 in force made severance eligibility itself conditional on having completed at least five years in the journalism profession — a bar considerably higher than the one-year threshold that applies to ordinary employees under Article 14 of the now-superseded Labour Law No. 1475 (a provision Article 120 of Law No. 4857 keeps alive specifically for severance purposes). The same version of Article 6 also excluded any fractional year of service under six months from the severance calculation entirely, so a journalist's final, incomplete year counted only if it reached the six-month mark.

On 4 May 2023, the Constitutional Court (Case No. 2021/62, Decision No. 2023/89, published in the Official Gazette on 14 June 2023) struck down both provisions on referral from an Ankara labour court. The Court held that requiring journalists to complete five years before qualifying for any severance at all, and denying credit for fractional years under six months, created an unjustified disparity with employees under the general Labour Law regime — a disparity it found disproportionate and therefore in violation of the constitutional right to property (Article 35) and the equality principle (Article 10). Both provisions were annulled with immediate effect from the date of publication.

The practical result is that Article 6 no longer states any minimum length-of-service threshold before severance becomes payable, and every fraction of a year of service — however short — now counts toward the calculation, not only fractions of six months or more. This is one of the more significant recent developments in the Press Labour Law regime, and it should be factored into any severance calculation for a journalist whose employment involved less than five years' total professional seniority or ended partway through a year of less than six months.

## Terminations That Do Not Trigger Severance

The Press Labour Law also takes a stricter approach than the general Labour Law when it comes to which types of termination actually give rise to a severance entitlement. Court of Cassation case law has repeatedly held that the law expressly lists the circumstances in which a journalist's own termination of the contract entitles them to severance, and that non-payment or late payment of wages is not among those listed circumstances — so a journalist who resigns on that basis cannot claim severance compensation on that ground alone. This is a notable departure from the general Labour Law, where Article 24 gives an employee a broad right to terminate for just cause — including for unpaid wages — while preserving the right to severance. The Press Labour Law takes a narrower, closed-list approach on this point.

At the same time, Article 11 of the Press Labour Law grants journalists a distinct just-cause termination right that has no equivalent in the general Labour Law: if the orientation or character of the publication changes in a way that clearly injures the journalist's honour, reputation or moral interests, the journalist may terminate the contract without waiting out the notice period, and may claim the same compensation they would have received had the employer terminated the contract without cause. This provision directly safeguards the ideological and professional-reputation dimension that is unique to the press profession.

## Resignation and Wage-Related Rules Specific to Journalists

Article 7 governs a journalist's own resignation and sets a single, fixed requirement: at least one month's prior written notice to the employer, regardless of the journalist's length of service. This differs from the general Labour Law's graduated notice periods, which scale with seniority. The Press Labour Law also contains a number of wage-related provisions tailored to the profession — covering wages during military service and pregnancy, wages where the journalist is imprisoned or the publication is suspended, and death compensation — along with separate provisions on non-publication days for daily newspapers and weekly rest. Annual paid leave is likewise calculated by reference to professional seniority rather than time with a single employer: a journalist with one year of service is entitled to four weeks of annual leave, rising to six weeks once professional seniority exceeds ten years — a different, profession-specific scale from the seniority bands used under the general Labour Law.

## The Press Card: Evidence, Not a Legal Precondition

Holding an official press card is not stated anywhere in the text of the Press Labour Law as a formal precondition for the law to apply. In practice, however, a press card is treated as strong evidence that the holder is actually performing "intellectual and artistic" journalistic work, and courts do take it into account in disputed cases. What is actually decisive is whether the Article 1 test — genuine intellectual and artistic work — is met on the facts. The Court of Cassation has determined journalist status by looking at the real nature of the work performed, independently of whether the person carries a press card. Working for a press organisation is therefore not, by itself, sufficient to fall under the Press Labour Law; the work must actually consist of genuine editorial, reporting or writing activity.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does every employee of a Turkish newspaper or news website fall under the Press Labour Law?**
No. Only those performing intellectual and artistic journalistic work — writers, reporters, editors, proofreaders, translators, photographers, camera operators, illustrators and cartoonists, for example — fall under Law No. 5953. Staff in advertising, sales, personnel, accounting or purely administrative roles remain subject to the general Labour Law even if employed by the same press organisation.

**If a journalist changes employer, does their seniority reset to zero?**
No. Under Article 6, a journalist's seniority for severance and annual leave purposes is calculated from their first entry into the journalism profession, not from the start date with their current employer. This is one of the most significant differences from the general Labour Law regime.

**Can a journalist claim severance after resigning because their salary was not paid?**
Generally, no. Court of Cassation case law holds that the Press Labour Law's list of circumstances entitling a journalist to severance on their own termination does not include non-payment or late payment of wages, unlike the broader just-cause resignation right available to employees under the general Labour Law.

**Is holding a press card required for the Press Labour Law to apply?**
No. The law's text does not make a press card a formal precondition. Courts look at whether the person's actual work meets the "intellectual and artistic work" test in Article 1; a press card is treated as supporting evidence rather than a determinative requirement.

**Does a journalist still need five years in the profession before qualifying for severance?**
No. That threshold, along with the rule excluding fractional years of under six months from the calculation, was struck down by the Constitutional Court in 2023 (Case No. 2021/62, Decision No. 2023/89) for creating an unjustified disparity with employees under the general Labour Law. Article 6 no longer imposes a minimum length-of-service threshold, and every fraction of a year now counts toward the calculation.
