Foreign businesses operating in Turkey routinely hand property to third parties for a limited purpose: a rental car to a driver, inventory to a warehouse operator, funds to an agent authorized to make payments on their behalf, or equipment to a repair shop. When that party does something with the property outside the agreed purpose — sells it, keeps it for personal use, or simply denies ever having received it — Turkish criminal law treats this as a distinct offense from theft or fraud. Article 155 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) addresses precisely this situation: possession that began lawfully and with the owner's consent, and was later diverted from its agreed purpose. Understanding how this offense is defined, and how it differs from neighboring property crimes, matters for any business that regularly places assets or funds in the hands of employees, contractors, or agents in Turkey.

## The Interest the Law Protects

Article 155 protects more than an owner's property interest in a narrow sense; its central concern is the reliability of transfers of possession made for a limited purpose. When someone hands over an item — for safekeeping, for use in a specific way, or for management on the owner's behalf — the law protects the legitimate expectation that the recipient will respect that limitation. The offense is completed when the recipient knowingly defeats that expectation, regardless of whether the underlying relationship was based on a written contract, an informal understanding, or a professional engagement.

## What Separates This Offense from Theft and Fraud

The dividing line between breach of trust and its two closest relatives — theft and fraud — is the moment at which possession passes to the wrongdoer, and the state of mind that accompanies that moment. In a breach of trust under Article 155, the property is handed over lawfully and with the owner's genuine consent, for safekeeping or a specified use; the offense arises only afterward, when the recipient decides to use the property outside that agreed purpose. In theft, by contrast, possession is taken unlawfully from the outset, without the owner's consent. In fraud, possession does pass through a transfer, but that transfer itself is procured through deception that corrupts the owner's will at the moment of handover. Put differently: in breach of trust there is no defect in the owner's consent when possession changes hands — the wrongful intent forms only later, at the point the property is misused. This three-way distinction is the single most frequently litigated question in Turkish criminal practice involving property handed over voluntarily, because the same facts (an item that ends up missing or unreturned) can support radically different charges depending on when dishonest intent is shown to have formed.

## Who Can Commit the Offense, and What Conduct Is Punished

Anyone who has been given possession of property for a defined purpose can be held liable — the offense is not limited to a particular profession or status, but it does require that a genuine transfer of possession actually took place. The victim is the property's owner or the person who transferred possession. The law recognizes two distinct ways of committing the offense. The first is disposing of the property outside the purpose for which it was transferred — selling it, pledging it, consuming it for one's own benefit, or otherwise dealing with it inconsistently with the agreed use. The second, entirely independent, form of conduct is denying that the transfer ever took place: the recipient simply refuses to acknowledge having received the property at all, thereby appropriating it through denial rather than through any physical act of disposal. Both routes must result in a benefit to the offender or to a third party, though the law does not require proof of any separate, quantifiable harm beyond the wrongful disposal or denial itself.

## Denial as an Independent Route to Liability

A point often overlooked is that denying the transfer is a self-standing way of committing the offense, distinct from and not dependent on actually disposing of the property. Even if the offender has not yet sold, used, or otherwise dealt with the item, simply denying that it was ever entrusted to them is enough to complete the crime. This rule exists because proving what became of an item that a custodian denies ever receiving can be extremely difficult, and the law protects the owner's right to demand the item back even when met with an outright denial rather than a visible act of misuse.

## The Mental Element

Breach of trust can only be committed intentionally, not through carelessness or negligence. The offender must know that the property was transferred for a specific, limited purpose and must nonetheless choose to use it outside that purpose or to deny the transfer, or must foresee and accept that this is the practical result of their conduct. The statutory reference to acting "for one's own or another's benefit" does not require proof of a special ulterior motive — it is satisfied once the disposal or denial has objectively produced a benefit for the offender or a third party. Because intent is essential, a genuine misunderstanding about the scope of the purpose for which the property was handed over, or a good-faith mistake about what use was authorized, removes criminal liability.

## The Aggravated Form: Professional, Commercial, and Fiduciary Relationships

The offense carries substantially heavier penalties where the property was entrusted to the offender as a function of a professional occupation, a commercial relationship, a service relationship, or any other capacity involving authority to manage another person's assets. The rationale is that the offender in these cases is not an ordinary private party but someone entrusted with property precisely because of an institutional or professional role — a custodian, an agent, a commission agent, or a manager with administrative authority are all typical examples. Because the betrayal exploits a structured relationship of trust rather than a casual one, the law treats this version of the offense as significantly more serious than the basic form.

## The 2025 Aggravating Factor for Motor Vehicles

A 2025 legislative amendment added a further aggravating circumstance: where the object of the offense is a land, sea, or air motor vehicle, the penalty otherwise applicable is increased. This addition responds to a recurring pattern in practice — rental vehicles, borrowed boats, and similar high-value vehicles that are sold off or simply not returned once entrusted to a driver, renter, or operator — by attaching a specific, heavier consequence to that category of property regardless of whether the basic or aggravated form of the offense otherwise applies.

## The Complaint Requirement

Prosecution of the basic form of the offense depends on a complaint filed by the victim; without one, the authorities will not open an investigation or proceed with a prosecution. The aggravated form involving professional, commercial, or fiduciary relationships, however, is prosecuted ex officio — meaning the authorities can act on their own initiative once they become aware of the conduct, without waiting for or depending on a complaint from the victim. This distinction matters in practice because it determines whether a victim's decision to withdraw a complaint can bring the proceedings to an end. The motor-vehicle aggravating factor is not an independent offense; it simply increases the sentence otherwise applicable under the basic or aggravated form, so the complaint requirement follows whichever of those two forms applies to the underlying conduct.

## Relationship to the Separate Offense of Using a Discharged Instrument

A closely related but legally distinct offense, found in a neighboring article of the Penal Code, punishes the use of a promissory instrument that has lost its economic basis because the underlying debt has already been paid or the obligation otherwise discharged. Unlike breach of trust, this offense is not about failing to return an item entrusted for a purpose; it is completed by the act of using an instrument that should no longer have any legal effect, not merely by failing to hand it back. What the two offenses share is a wrongdoer maintaining control over something — an item or a document — after any legitimate basis for doing so has ended, but the underlying mechanics differ: one concerns a transfer of possession for a defined purpose, the other concerns a document whose economic basis has already been extinguished. The penalty range for this related offense mirrors the basic form of breach of trust, and it is likewise dependent on a victim's complaint.

## Attempt, Participation, and Multiple Acts

Attempt is conceptually possible where the act of disposal can be broken into separable steps, but the denial-based route to liability is generally treated as completed in a single instant and therefore not suited to an attempt analysis. General principles of complicity apply: a third party who knowingly benefits from the diverted property, aware of its origin, may be exposed to liability as an instigator or accessory. Where multiple acts of disposal occur within the scope of the same underlying relationship of trust, they are generally treated under the rules governing a single continuing offense rather than as separate crimes.

## Penalties and How Cases Are Proven

The basic form carries a prison term ranging from six months to two years together with a judicial fine; the aggravated form involving professional, commercial, or fiduciary relationships carries a substantially higher range of one to seven years' imprisonment together with a judicial fine of up to three thousand days. Where the motor-vehicle aggravating factor applies, whichever of these two sentencing ranges otherwise governs is increased. In practice, litigation turns on three things: establishing that a genuine complaint exists where required, proving the exact purpose for which possession was transferred through concrete evidence such as a contract, a delivery record, or witness testimony, and connecting the offender's knowledge at the time of transfer to the later act of disposal or denial.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How is breach of trust different from theft if property ends up missing either way?**
The difference lies in when possession passed and with what intent. If the offender possessed the item lawfully from the start, with the owner's genuine consent, and only later misused it or denied having it, this is breach of trust. If possession was taken without the owner's consent from the outset, it is theft, even if the same item later turns up sold or missing.

**Does the offense require the entrusted property to actually be sold or damaged?**
No. Disposing of the property outside its agreed purpose is one way of committing the offense, but simply denying that it was ever received is an equally sufficient and independent basis for liability, even if the property has not been touched.

**Can a business proceed with a criminal complaint even if the underlying arrangement with the other party was informal, with no written contract?**
Yes, in principle. What matters is that a genuine transfer of possession occurred for a specific purpose and that the recipient's later conduct departed from it. A written contract, delivery record, or other documentation makes proving the purpose of the transfer considerably easier, but the absence of a formal contract does not by itself defeat a claim.

**Is a criminal complaint always necessary to start a prosecution under Article 155?**
Only for the basic form of the offense. Where the aggravated form applies — because the property was entrusted as part of a professional, commercial, or service relationship — prosecutors can act on their own initiative without waiting for a complaint from the victim.
