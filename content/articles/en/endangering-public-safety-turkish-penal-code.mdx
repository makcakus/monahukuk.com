Foreign residents and businesses operating in Turkey sometimes encounter Article 170 of the Turkish Penal Code (TCK) in unexpected contexts — a celebratory gunshot, a bonfire that gets out of hand, or a workplace incident involving a landslide or flood risk. Unlike many public-order offenses that punish conduct simply because it is inherently risky, Article 170 requires proof that a real and specific danger actually materialized in the circumstances of the case. Understanding this concrete-danger threshold, the range of acts it covers, and how it differs from its negligent counterpart in Article 171 is essential for anyone facing, or advising on, a prosecution under this provision.

## The Protected Interest and the Concrete-Danger Requirement

Article 170 sits within the part of the Penal Code dealing with offenses against society, and the interest it protects is the collective safety of the public rather than any single victim's personal safety. What sets it apart from many other public-safety offenses is that the law does not criminalize the underlying conduct merely because it is abstractly risky. The text requires that the act be carried out in a manner that is dangerous to the life, health or property of others, or in a manner capable of causing fear, anxiety or panic among people. Turkish criminal law scholarship treats this as making Article 170 a "concrete danger" offense: it is not enough that the conduct belongs to a category of behavior generally considered hazardous. The court must find, on the specific facts, that an actual and identifiable danger arose — taking into account factors such as whether people were nearby, the nature of the location, how intense the conduct was, and the time of day. Firing a gun into the air in a deserted field and doing the same thing in the middle of a crowded street are treated very differently under this analysis, even though the physical act is identical, because the concreteness of the resulting danger differs. The law also recognizes an alternative basis for liability: conduct capable of provoking fear, anxiety or panic, independent of any direct threat to life or property, which extends the offense to acts that primarily disturb public order and a sense of collective security.

## Qualifying Acts and the Distinction Drawn for Certain Firearms

Article 170's first paragraph lists several alternative acts, any one of which can trigger liability if it is accompanied by the danger or fear/panic potential described above: starting a fire, causing a building collapse, a landslide, an avalanche, a flood or an inundation, discharging a firearm, or using explosives. A legislative amendment made through Law No. 7571 in December 2025 added a distinction within the firearm category: where the discharge involves a device that fires only blank cartridges or gas cartridges — rather than a firearm capable of firing live ammunition — the applicable penalty range is materially lower than for an ordinary firearm discharge. This reduced penalty is confined strictly to that category of device; the use of explosives is not eligible for it and is always assessed under the offense's standard penalty range. The distinction reflects an underlying idea that the degree of concrete danger a weapon can generate should influence the severity of punishment even within a single category of prohibited conduct — the same broad act (discharging a firearm) can carry very different consequences depending on the lethal or injurious capacity of the specific device used.

## The Aggravating Circumstance: Acts Committed Where People Are Gathered

The same 2025 amendment introduced a second paragraph establishing an aggravating circumstance: where any of the acts described above is committed in a place where people are gathered collectively, the penalty is increased by half. This aggravation is consistent with the concrete-danger logic underlying the whole offense — the same conduct becomes more dangerous, and exposes a larger number of potential victims, when carried out in a setting where many people are physically present. The statute does not define what counts as a place of collective gathering, leaving the scope of the concept to be worked out through judicial practice; examples that come to mind include concerts, stadiums, busy streets or marketplaces, but each case still requires an assessment of how many people were actually present and the nature of the location at the relevant time. A merely theoretical possibility that people might have been present is not sufficient to trigger the aggravation — what matters is whether a collective gathering actually existed at the moment the act was committed.

## A Two-Tier Structure: Causing the Event Versus Merely Creating the Danger of It

A further distinction operates within Article 170 itself. The first paragraph punishes a person who actually brings about the event in question — for example, someone who actually starts a fire or actually causes a building to collapse, a landslide, an avalanche, a flood or an inundation. A separate, third paragraph addresses a lesser scenario: a person who merely creates the danger of a fire, a building collapse, a landslide, an avalanche, a flood or an inundation, without the event itself occurring, faces a considerably lighter penalty. The distinction turns on how far the conduct actually progressed — in the first paragraph the harmful event has actually occurred, whereas in the third paragraph only a concrete danger of that event has arisen. Notably, this lighter, danger-only tier does not extend to discharging a firearm or using explosives; it applies only to the fire/collapse/landslide/avalanche/flood/inundation category. This makes Article 170 a two-tier danger offense rather than a single uniform standard, and correctly identifying which paragraph a defendant's conduct falls under has a direct and significant effect on the sentencing range that applies.

## Intent, Negligence and the Related Offense Under Article 171

Article 170 is, in its core form, an intentional offense: the perpetrator must knowingly and willingly carry out one of the qualifying acts, aware that it is capable of creating a concrete danger or provoking fear, anxiety or panic. Whether the conduct was committed with direct intent or with a form of intent where the perpetrator foresaw but did not directly want the outcome can affect how culpability is assessed in a given case. Turkish law addresses the negligent counterpart separately, in Article 171, which covers a person who negligently causes a fire, a building collapse, a landslide, an avalanche, a flood or an inundation, where the act is dangerous to the life, health or property of others. Notably, Article 171 does not extend to discharging a firearm or using explosives — those acts are considered to require a deliberate, volitional form of conduct that does not fit comfortably within a negligence-based framework grounded in a breach of a duty of care. The penalty under Article 171 is markedly lower than under Article 170's first paragraph, reflecting the lower degree of culpability inherent in negligent as opposed to intentional conduct. Where negligence is at issue, distinguishing between conscious negligence (where the risk was foreseen but not desired) and unconscious negligence (where it was not foreseen at all) can matter for assessing how foreseeable the outcome was to the defendant.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence With Other Offenses

Because Article 170 is a concrete-danger offense, the question of attempt is debated: since the offense is generally considered complete once the concrete danger itself has materialized, whether conduct that falls short of producing that danger can be treated as an attempt needs to be examined on the specific facts of each case. General rules on participation in an offense apply where more than one person is involved, with each participant's individual contribution and intent assessed separately. From a concurrence-of-offenses perspective, the most significant point is that because Article 170 punishes danger rather than harm, if the conduct progresses further and causes an actual injury — death, bodily harm or damage to property — the perpetrator can additionally be held liable for the corresponding harm-based offense (such as intentional homicide, intentional injury or damage to property). Article 170 is then applied either together with, or as a lesser-included stage behind, those harm-based offenses, depending on how the facts unfolded.

## Procedure, Penalties and Sentencing Considerations

Offenses under Article 170 are directed at the public at large rather than at a specific individual, so they are not subject to a private complaint requirement — prosecutors investigate and prosecute on their own initiative. In terms of penalties, the standard range under the first paragraph runs from one to five years' imprisonment, with the reduced-penalty firearm scenario, the aggravated collective-gathering scenario, and the danger-only third-paragraph scenario each carrying their own distinct ranges. The negligent offense under Article 171 carries a penalty of three months to one year's imprisonment. Given these penalty levels, cases are generally heard before the criminal court of first instance, and the applicable statute-of-limitations periods for prosecution and sentencing follow from the maximum penalty prescribed for the relevant paragraph. Depending on the specific sentence imposed, individualized sentencing mechanisms — such as deferral of the announcement of the verdict or suspension of the sentence — may also be available, and whether they apply depends both on the correct classification of the underlying act and the particular circumstances of the file.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it enough that an act like firing a gun into the air is generally considered dangerous?**
No. Article 170 requires proof that a concrete, specific danger actually arose in the circumstances of the case — factors such as the presence of people nearby, the location, and the time of day are all relevant to that assessment. A generic risk associated with the type of conduct is not, by itself, sufficient.

**Does using a blank-firing or gas-cartridge gun carry the same penalty as using an ordinary firearm?**
No. Following the December 2025 amendment, discharging a device that fires only blank or gas cartridges carries a materially lower penalty than discharging an ordinary firearm capable of firing live ammunition. This reduced range does not apply to the use of explosives, which is always assessed under the standard penalty range.

**What is the difference between actually causing a fire and merely creating the danger of one?**
Article 170's first paragraph applies where the harmful event — a fire, building collapse, landslide, avalanche, flood or inundation — has actually occurred. A separate, third paragraph applies where only the danger of such an event arose without the event itself materializing, and it carries a considerably lighter penalty. This danger-only tier does not cover firearm discharge or the use of explosives.

**Can someone be prosecuted under Article 170 and also for a resulting injury or death?**
Yes. Because Article 170 punishes the creation of danger rather than actual harm, if the conduct in question goes on to cause an actual injury, death or property damage, the perpetrator can additionally face liability for the corresponding harm-based offense, assessed together with or alongside the danger-based charge depending on the facts.
