Foreign nationals who become involved in a Turkish criminal matter — whether as a suspect, a victim, or a witness — often expect the process to unfold in open court from the outset, as it might in other legal systems. In Turkey, however, every criminal case first passes through a confidential investigation phase run by a public prosecutor, and it is during this phase, long before any courtroom hearing, that most of the decisions affecting the eventual outcome are made. Understanding how this phase starts, who controls it, and why so little information about it is made public is essential for anyone navigating a Turkish criminal matter from abroad, and this article explains the framework set out in Articles 157 through 161 and Article 169 of the Turkish Criminal Procedure Code (Ceza Muhakemesi Kanunu, "CMK").

## The Prosecutor's Duty to Investigate Once a Suspicion Arises

Under CMK Article 160, a Turkish public prosecutor has no discretion about whether to look into a matter once information suggesting a crime has occurred reaches them. The threshold is deliberately low: the prosecutor does not need firm proof or even a formal complaint, only an impression that an offense may have been committed. That impression can come from a report to the authorities, a police finding, a press account, or any other source, and once it exists, the prosecutor is obligated to open a fact-finding inquiry immediately. This is often described as the "principle of legality" or "mandatory prosecution" — Turkish prosecutors cannot simply decline to look into a plausible allegation because it appears minor or inconvenient. At the same time, the same provision makes clear that the prosecutor's role is not that of a one-sided accuser: the law requires the prosecutor, acting through the judicial police under their command, to gather and preserve evidence both against and in favor of the suspect, and to safeguard the suspect's rights throughout. For foreign suspects, the practical consequence is that an investigation may already be underway, and evidence already being collected, well before anyone is formally notified of it — which is one reason the confidentiality rules discussed below matter so much in practice.

## Why the Investigation Stays Confidential

Article 157 establishes that procedural steps taken during the investigation phase are, as a general rule, confidential. This serves two purposes at once: it protects the integrity of an inquiry that is still gathering evidence, and it protects a person who has not been convicted of anything from being publicly branded a criminal before any court has examined the case. Confidentiality is not absolute — the law itself carves out exceptions (for example, matters that later become public through open trial proceedings), and confidentiality can never be used to undermine a suspect's or defendant's right to mount a defense. In practice, the main point of friction is timing: Turkish appellate practice treats the investigation phase as continuing not just until an indictment is filed, but until the court has formally accepted that indictment, meaning that even a completed indictment awaiting acceptance remains protected information. On the other side of the balance, press freedom is recognized as a competing interest — reporting on investigation material can be lawful where no confidentiality order has been issued, the story serves a genuine public interest, and it does not go beyond what a complaint or report actually said. For a foreign national worried about publicity around a Turkish case, this means the file itself should not be circulating, but a narrow category of public-interest reporting based on lawfully available information may still occur.

## How an Investigation Is Triggered: Reports and Complaints

Article 158 sets out the practical mechanics for bringing a matter to the authorities' attention, and it draws an important distinction between two routes: an "ihbar" (report), which anyone can make regardless of whether they were personally affected, and a "şikâyet" (complaint), which is required for certain offenses that only proceed at the request of the victim. A report or complaint can be submitted to the public prosecutor's office directly or to the police, and if it is mistakenly filed with a governor's office, a district administrator, a court, or — for offenses connected to the performance of a public duty — the relevant public institution, the law requires that it be forwarded to the appropriate prosecutor's office without delay. Turkish citizens and foreign nationals abroad can also report or complain about crimes committed overseas that are subject to prosecution in Turkey through Turkish embassies and consulates. Submissions may be made in writing or given orally for the record. A 2017 amendment added a safeguard against abuse: where a report or complaint is transparently baseless on its face, or so vague and generic that no investigation could meaningfully test it, the prosecutor can close the matter without ever assigning the accused person the formal status of "suspect" — and the person who made the report can be notified and object to that closure decision. This matters for anyone worried about a malicious or speculative report being filed against them abroad: the law does not require a full investigation to be opened on unsubstantiated grounds. Finally, if a case proceeds to trial and it later emerges that the offense was one that required a victim's complaint, the case still continues unless the victim affirmatively withdraws that complaint.

## The Prosecutor's Authority Over the Police

Article 161 defines the relationship between the prosecutor and the judicial police not as one of general administrative hierarchy, but as a direct chain of command tied to the specific investigation. The prosecutor can conduct any inquiry personally or direct the judicial police to do so, and can demand information relevant to the investigation from any public official. Police officers, in turn, must report to their supervising prosecutor immediately on incidents they have handled, arrests made, and measures applied, and must carry out the prosecutor's instructions without delay. Instructions are ordinarily given in writing, though urgent matters can be communicated verbally provided they are documented in writing as soon as practicable afterward. Turkish appellate courts have applied this chain of command strictly even in undercover work: police conducting an investigation under a concealed identity without the prosecutor's knowledge or authorization act outside the law, and evidence gathered that way can be treated as unlawfully obtained. For someone being investigated in Turkey, this structure means that questioning the legal basis for a specific police action — was it actually authorized by the prosecutor, and can that be shown — is a legitimate and sometimes decisive line of defense.

## Documenting Investigative Steps

Article 169 requires that essentially every step taken during the investigation — taking a suspect's statement, questioning a suspect formally, hearing witnesses or expert opinions, conducting an on-site inspection or examination — be reduced to a written record, and that a clerk be present alongside the prosecutor or magistrate for these procedures. That record must be signed by the police officer, prosecutor, or magistrate involved along with the clerk, and it must note where and when the procedure took place, its start and end times, and the identities of everyone who participated or had a relevant connection to it. If a defense lawyer or legal representative was present, their name and signature are recorded as well. Anyone present is meant to have the parts of the record concerning them read aloud or given to them to read, and to sign in confirmation; if someone refuses to sign, the reasons for that refusal are also written into the record rather than simply left unexplained. This paperwork requirement is not a bureaucratic formality — it is what allows a defense team, later in the case, to test whether a given step was actually taken the way the file claims it was, and the absence of a required record for a particular procedure is itself a recognized ground for challenging the reliability of evidence obtained through it. The investigation phase concludes once the prosecutor concludes the evidence gathered supports a sufficient level of suspicion, at which point an indictment is drafted and the case moves into the trial phase; any preventive measure imposed during the investigation, most significantly pre-trial detention, remains separately subject to its own legal review.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Will I be told that an investigation has been opened against me in Turkey?**
Not necessarily, and not immediately. Because investigation proceedings are confidential by default, a suspect may not learn of an inquiry until they are summoned for a statement, questioned, or otherwise formally contacted. This is one of the more disorienting aspects of the Turkish system for foreign nationals used to earlier notice.

**Can someone file a report against me that goes nowhere?**
Yes, and the law anticipates this. If a report or complaint is plainly unfounded on its face or purely vague and generic, the prosecutor can close the file without ever treating the accused person as a formal suspect, and the reporting party can be notified and given the chance to object to that closure.

**What is the practical difference between a "report" (ihbar) and a "complaint" (şikâyet)?**
A report can be made by anyone, regardless of whether they were personally harmed, and is sufficient to trigger an investigation into most offenses. A complaint is the mechanism required for offenses that Turkish law treats as prosecutable only at the victim's request; without it, or if it is later withdrawn before trial, that category of case generally cannot proceed.

**If Turkish police interviewed me without going through the prosecutor, is that a problem?**
It can be. The judicial police are required to act under the prosecutor's direction, and Turkish appellate courts have found that police action taken outside that chain of command — for example, using a concealed identity without prosecutorial authorization — can render the resulting evidence unlawful. This is a point worth raising with defense counsel promptly.
