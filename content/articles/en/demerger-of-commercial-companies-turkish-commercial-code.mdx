Articles 159 through 179 of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK) regulate demergers — in a sense the mirror image of a merger. Where a merger combines multiple estates into one company, a demerger splits a single company's estate among several existing or newly formed companies. It is a common tool for corporate groups refocusing on core activities, separating business lines, or resolving shareholder disputes by dividing assets.

## Full vs. Partial Demerger

In a full demerger, a company's entire estate is divided and transferred to other companies, and the demerging company ceases to exist and is struck from the registry. In a partial demerger, only part of the estate is transferred, and the demerging company continues to exist, albeit smaller (Art. 159). Only capital companies and cooperatives may demerge, and only into capital companies or cooperatives — general and limited partnerships cannot demerge (Art. 160).

## Asset Allocation and Shareholder Protection

The demerger agreement or plan must itemize precisely which assets and liabilities go to which company, down to individual real estate, securities, and intangible assets (Art. 167). For any asset not expressly allocated, a default rule applies: in a full demerger it passes to all recipient companies in proportion to net assets received; in a partial demerger it remains with the demerging company (Art. 168). Critically, for any liability left unallocated in a full demerger, all participating companies are jointly and severally liable — a significant creditor safeguard. Shareholders of the demerging company are entitled to shares of proportionate value in the recipient companies (following the Art. 140 rule also used for mergers), and the Code distinguishes between "proportionate" demergers, where each shareholder receives the same relative stake across all resulting companies, and "non-proportionate" demergers, where allocation can differ — often used to split up disputes between shareholders (Art. 161).

## Creditor Protection: The Two-Tier Liability System

Creditors are called upon by public notice to submit claims, and companies must secure any claim raised within three months (Art. 174-175) — notably, before the shareholder vote approving the demerger, earlier than the equivalent step in a merger. Distinctively, Art. 176 introduces a two-tier liability structure specific to demergers: the company to which a debt is expressly allocated is primarily liable; if it fails to pay, the other participating companies become secondarily, jointly and severally liable — but only once the primary debtor has entered bankruptcy, concordat, or shown certified insolvency, or its head office has moved abroad beyond practical reach.

## Employee Transfers

Employment contracts transfer to the recipient company along with the relevant business unit unless the employee objects; even where an employee objects, the former and new employer remain jointly liable for the employee's outstanding claims (Art. 178).

Foreign investors should treat the itemized asset schedule in a demerger agreement as the single most important document in the transaction — any asset or liability left out defaults to a statutory allocation rule that may not match commercial intent.
