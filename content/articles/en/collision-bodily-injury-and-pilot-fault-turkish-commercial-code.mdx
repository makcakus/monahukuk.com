Having covered joint-fault liability for cargo and ship damage — where shipowners' liability toward third parties is explicitly *not* joint and several — the series now examines a sharp reversal of that rule for bodily injury, plus the special treatment of pilot fault (TTK Art. 1290-1291).

## Bodily Injury: Joint and Several Liability

Art. 1290(1) covers the same joint-fault scenario as the cargo-damage rule but reaches the opposite result for a different kind of loss: where a collision results from the joint fault of the colliding ships, the shipowners are **jointly and severally liable** for death, injury, or impaired health suffered by people on board. A victim — an injured crew member, passenger, or their surviving dependents — can recover the full amount from either shipowner, regardless of that owner's actual share of fault. If the proportion of fault cannot be determined, or is equal, liability between the shipowners themselves is split equally.

The reversal from the cargo rule is deliberate. Cargo claimants are typically commercially sophisticated and insured, and can absorb an unrecovered shortfall. A victim of death or bodily injury is often not — if a less-at-fault shipowner could only be sued for its own share, and the more-at-fault owner turned out to be insolvent or unreachable, an injury victim could be left without full compensation. Turkish law prioritizes the victim's certainty of recovery over apportionment precision in this context.

## Internal Recourse Between Shipowners

Art. 1290(2) balances this by providing that, as between the shipowners themselves, each is liable only in proportion to its own degree of fault — the classic joint-and-several structure, where the victim can collect the full amount from either party, but the ultimate cost is redistributed internally through recourse claims.

## Pilot Fault: Advisory vs. Compulsory Conning Pilots

Art. 1291 addresses collisions caused by a pilot's fault and draws a sharp line based on control. Where a ship is under an advisory pilot — whether compulsory or optional — the shipowner remains liable for that pilot's fault, because the master retains final decision-making authority and simply chooses whether to follow the pilot's advice. Where the ship is under a compulsory **conning** pilot — one who actually takes over navigation and whom the master has no legal right to refuse — the shipowner is *not* liable, because control has genuinely passed out of the owner's hands.

## Practical Takeaway

This control-based distinction has been applied to protect third-party tugboat or pilotage operators from claims for damage caused by pilot error, since liability for an advisory pilot's fault rests with the piloted vessel's own owner, not the service provider.
