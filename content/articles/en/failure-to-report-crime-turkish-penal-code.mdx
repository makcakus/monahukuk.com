Turkish criminal law does not limit itself to punishing the people who commit crimes — in three separate provisions of the Turkish Penal Code (Türk Ceza Kanunu, "TCK"), it also punishes silence. Articles 278, 279 and 280 create a graduated set of duties to notify the authorities of a crime that is underway or has just occurred, with the intensity of the duty rising according to who the observer is: an ordinary member of the public, a public official, or a health professional. For foreign residents, businesses operating in Turkey, and especially healthcare staff working in Turkish clinics and hospitals, understanding when silence itself becomes a criminal offense is essential, because these are not offenses of doing something wrong — they are offenses of doing nothing when the law required action.

## A Tiered System of Reporting Duties

The Turkish Penal Code treats failure to report a crime as what Turkish legal doctrine calls a "genuine omission offense" (gerçek ihmali suç): the law does not wait for harmful conduct, it criminalizes the failure to perform a specific expected action — notifying the competent authorities. All three provisions share this structure, but they differ sharply in who is bound and why. Article 278 imposes a baseline duty on any person who becomes aware of a crime. Article 279 imposes a heavier, aggravated duty on public officials, tied to the trust inherent in their office. Article 280 imposes a distinct professional duty on physicians, dentists, pharmacists, midwives, nurses and other health-service providers, arising from the unique access to information their profession gives them. As the class of potential offenders narrows to those with specialized access to information, the law expects — and demands — a correspondingly stronger response.

## What Interest the Law Protects

All three provisions ultimately aim to protect the proper functioning of justice and public safety by ensuring that crimes do not go unreported simply because a witness chooses to stay silent. The emphasis, however, shifts from one article to the next. Article 278 protects the general public interest in the judicial system being informed of ongoing wrongdoing. Article 279 protects public confidence in government service and the state's ability to respond to crime without officials looking the other way. Article 280 is aimed specifically at ensuring that healthcare encounters are not used to conceal offenses — particularly offenses against children, persons with physical or mental disabilities, or pregnant individuals who cannot defend themselves. This creates a genuine and much-discussed tension for health professionals between patient confidentiality and the duty to report: Turkish law resolves that tension by placing society's interest in knowing about the crime above the professional's ordinary duty of medical secrecy.

## Who Must Report, What Triggers the Duty, and What Counts as Reporting

**Under Article 278**, any person can be an offender. The duty is triggered either by a crime that is still ongoing, or by a completed crime whose consequences can still be contained or limited. Importantly, the law does not impose a general civic duty to report every crime after the fact — only where the harmful consequences remain capable of being limited. This is why the article separates an ongoing crime from a completed-but-still-containable one into two distinct scenarios, since the timing of the offense changes what is expected of the observer. Where the victim is a child under fifteen, a person with a physical or mental disability, or someone who cannot defend themselves because of pregnancy, the applicable penalty is increased by half, reflecting the law's heightened concern for vulnerable victims.

**Under Article 279**, only a public official can be an offender, and the duty is limited to crimes the official learns of through their position. The offense can be committed in one of two ways: failing to report at all, or unreasonably delaying the report. By covering delay as well as outright silence, the law reaches officials who eventually pass on information too late to be useful, not just those who never do. Because a public official's access to the information flows directly from their office, and because that office carries a public trust, the penalty here is set higher than the general duty under Article 278.

**Under Article 280**, the offender must again fall into a defined professional category: physicians, dentists, pharmacists, midwives, nurses, and other health-service providers. The triggering event is lower than certainty — the law only requires that the professional encounter an indication, in the course of their work, that a crime has been committed, and then fail to report it or unreasonably delay doing so. The provision deliberately does not require the health professional to make a formal legal determination that a crime occurred; it only requires them to pass along what they observed to the competent authority. In all three provisions, the offense is a hazard-type offense that is complete the moment the report is not made or is delayed — no separate harmful outcome needs to be shown.

## The Mental Element: What the Offender Must Know

All three offenses require intent. The offender must be aware that the situation they encountered constitutes, or could constitute, a crime, and must nonetheless choose not to report it, or to delay reporting it. Turkish law also treats conditional intent as sufficient — it is enough that the person foresaw the possibility that what they were seeing was a crime and remained indifferent to the need to report it. Ordinary negligence, such as genuinely failing to notice the signs of a crime, does not meet this standard and does not give rise to liability under these articles. Where the failure to report stems from a genuine lack of awareness or a reasonable misjudgment of the situation, no criminal intent exists and no liability follows. This distinction matters most under Article 280, where how obvious the "indication" of a crime actually was becomes a central factual question in any given case.

## Differences Between the Three Offenses and the Prosecution Regime

The core distinction between the three provisions is the offender's status and the corresponding intensity of the duty. Article 278 sets a minimum duty applicable to everyone, while Articles 279 and 280 impose a heavier duty tied to a specific status — reflecting the greater access to information that public officials and health professionals have compared with an ordinary member of the public. Article 278 also contains an exemption for people who would otherwise be entitled to decline to testify as a witness (typically close relatives of the person involved); this exemption reflects a decision to give the right not to testify against a close relative priority over the reporting duty, though it does not affect any separate duty to prevent a crime from occurring. None of these three offenses require a private complaint — all are prosecuted by the public prosecutor on its own initiative, because the interest being protected is a public one, not an individual right that only the victim can choose to pursue.

## Attempt, Participation and Overlap with Other Offenses

Because these are genuine omission offenses, most Turkish legal commentary considers an attempt to commit them conceptually impossible: the offense is complete the instant the window for reporting passes, and there is no ongoing act that could be interrupted partway through. On participation, since Articles 279 and 280 require a specific personal status (public official, or a defined health professional), a person who does not hold that status can generally only be held liable as an instigator or an aider, not as a principal offender. Where the person who failed to report was themselves a participant in the underlying crime, no separate failure-to-report offense arises, since a person cannot be required to report their own crime. This distinction matters in practice for pinpointing exactly when the reporting duty is triggered. A public official's failure to report a crime learned of through their duties can also trigger a separate disciplinary liability under other relevant statutes — a track independent of criminal liability.

## Penalties and Jurisdiction

All three offenses carry prison sentences and are prosecuted ex officio. Jurisdiction lies with the criminal court of first instance (asliye ceza mahkemesi), regardless of the nature of the underlying crime that went unreported. Under Article 278, the aggravating factor is tied to the victim's vulnerability, whereas under Articles 279 and 280 the offender's professional status is itself what elevates the base penalty. In practice, the most contested evidentiary question in these cases tends to be exactly when, to which authority, and with what content a report was — or should have been — made.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an ordinary citizen have to report every crime they become aware of?**
No. Article 278 limits the general duty to crimes that are still ongoing, or completed crimes whose consequences can still be limited. A general duty to report every crime after the fact, regardless of whether anything can still be done about it, does not exist under Turkish law.

**Can a doctor be prosecuted for not reporting a patient's injuries if they are not certain a crime occurred?**
Potentially, yes. Article 280 only requires that the health professional encountered an indication that a crime had been committed — not legal certainty. Reporting the observed facts to the competent authority is what is expected, not a formal determination that a crime took place.

**Is failing to report a crime a complaint-based offense that the victim must pursue?**
No. All three offenses are prosecuted by the public prosecutor on their own initiative, because the protected interest is considered a public one rather than a private right belonging exclusively to the victim.

**Does a close relative of the person involved have to report the crime?**
Generally not under Article 278: people who would be entitled to decline to testify as a witness (typically close family members) are exempted from punishment for not reporting. This exemption, however, does not extend to any separate legal duty to prevent the crime itself.
