Articles 64 through 88 of the Turkish Commercial Code (TTK) set out a merchant's bookkeeping obligations in detail. While heavily technical and closely tied to accounting practice, this area matters most for one reason: properly kept commercial books can serve as conclusive evidence in a dispute between merchants, which means non-compliance is not merely a tax or administrative risk — it can be the difference between winning and losing a case.

## The Duty to Keep Books and Physical Formalities

Every merchant must keep commercial books that transparently reflect its economic and financial position (Art. 64). Entries must be complete, accurate, timely, and orderly, and cannot be altered in a way that obscures the original content (Art. 65). For physical books — the journal, ledger, inventory book, share ledger, and board minute book — opening approval must be notarized at incorporation, and closing approval must be notarized within set deadlines each year. These formalities are a precondition for the books to later serve as evidence in court.

## Financial Statements, Valuation Principles, and Retention

Merchants must prepare an inventory at opening and at the end of each fiscal period, along with year-end financial statements (balance sheet and income statement) prepared under Turkish Accounting Standards, in Turkish and in Turkish Lira (Art. 66-77). Valuation follows core accounting principles — consistency between periods, going concern, prudence, and accrual accounting (Art. 78). Books, correspondence, and supporting documents must be retained for ten years from the end of the calendar year in which the last entry was made (Art. 82).

## Evidentiary Role in Litigation

Under the Code of Civil Procedure, properly kept books — with valid opening and closing approvals and internally consistent entries — can serve as conclusive evidence for or against the party keeping them. Turkish courts have held that a party keeping only a simplified "business ledger," rather than the full statutory books required under Art. 64, cannot benefit from the evidentiary admission presumption even where the opposing party fails to produce its own books. Courts may also order production of commercial books on their own motion, even where neither party has relied on them as evidence.

Foreign investors operating a Turkish subsidiary should treat compliance with these bookkeeping formalities as a litigation-readiness issue, not just a compliance checkbox — poorly maintained books can become evidence against the company itself in a future dispute.
