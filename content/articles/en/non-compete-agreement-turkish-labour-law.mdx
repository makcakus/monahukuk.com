Foreign employers hiring senior staff, engineers or sales personnel in Turkey often want to restrict what an employee can do once they leave — join a competitor, open a rival business, or take clients with them. Turkish law allows this through a post-employment non-compete clause, but the rules are narrower and more employee-protective than in many other jurisdictions. The subject is governed by Articles 444 to 447 of the Turkish Code of Obligations (TBK), a framework built on a deliberate tension: the employer's interest in protecting trade secrets and client relationships it invested in building, against the employee's constitutionally protected freedom to work and contract. Because of that tension, Turkish courts treat non-compete clauses as an exception that must be construed narrowly and in the employee's favour, not as an ordinary contract term to be enforced at face value.

## What Makes a Non-Compete Clause Valid

Article 444 sets three cumulative conditions that must all be met before a non-compete undertaking has any binding force. First, the employee agreeing to the restriction must have full legal capacity — an undertaking signed by a minor or a person under legal restriction is not binding without the consent of their legal representative. Second, the agreement must be in writing; this is not a matter of evidentiary convenience but a condition of validity itself, meaning an oral or implied non-compete promise produces no legal effect at all. Third, and by far the most litigated condition, the clause is only valid if the employment relationship actually gave the employee access to client connections, production secrets, or insight into the employer's business, and if the use of that knowledge would be capable of causing the employer significant harm. Turkish courts scrutinize this third condition closely: a job title such as "manager" or "director" is not enough on its own if the employee had no real access to sensitive client information or trade secrets in practice. What matters is the substance of the knowledge the employee actually acquired and the seriousness of the harm its use by a competitor could cause — not the label on the employment contract.

## Limits on Scope, Duration and Geography

Even where a non-compete clause meets the validity test, Article 445 prevents it from becoming an open-ended restraint on the employee's livelihood. The restriction must be reasonable in its geographic scope, its duration, and the range of activities it covers, and it may not unfairly jeopardize the employee's economic future. As a general rule the maximum duration allowed by law is two years, departures from which require special circumstances. These three dimensions — place, time and subject matter — are not assessed in isolation but together: a narrow geographic restriction paired with a long duration can raise the same fairness concerns as a broad geographic restriction paired with a short one. Importantly, Article 445 gives the judge the power to scale back an excessive clause rather than strike it down entirely — narrowing its scope or shortening its duration to a fair level instead of voiding the whole agreement. This is a targeted application of the broader Turkish contract-law principle of partial invalidity. When making this assessment, courts also weigh whatever consideration the employer may have promised the employee in exchange for the restriction — such as extra compensation or a bonus — and the presence of such consideration works in favour of the clause being found reasonable.

## Penalty Clauses and the Consequences of Breach

Under Article 446, an employee who breaches a valid non-compete undertaking is, in principle, liable for the full extent of the employer's resulting loss. In practice, most contracts set a liquidated penalty (a penalty clause) to make this liability concrete and easier to enforce. Where such a clause exists, the employee may discharge the non-compete obligation simply by paying the agreed penalty amount, unless the contract says otherwise — but if the employer's actual loss exceeds that amount, the excess can still be claimed separately. Article 446 also gives the employer one further, exceptional remedy: if the contract expressly and in writing reserves this right, and if the importance of the employer's threatened or violated interest together with the seriousness of the employee's conduct justifies it, the employer may seek an injunction to stop the breaching conduct itself, not just monetary compensation. This is a powerful tool for situations where money alone cannot undo the damage — for instance where client relationships are effectively lost for good — but it remains subject to a proportionality check, and courts calibrate it to the gravity of the specific case rather than granting it automatically.

## When the Non-Compete Obligation Ends

Article 447 identifies two situations in which a non-compete clause lapses even though it was validly created. The first is where it is established that the employer no longer has any genuine interest in maintaining the restriction — for example where the employer has exited the relevant line of business entirely or the protected information has lost its confidential character. The second, and far more common in practice, ties into termination: if the employer terminates the employment relationship without just cause, or if the employee resigns for a reason attributable to the employer, the non-compete obligation ends automatically. The logic is straightforward — it would be inequitable for an employer who wrongfully dismisses an employee, or whose conduct forces the employee to resign, to then rely on a non-compete clause to block that employee from finding new work. Conversely, where the employee resigns without just cause or through their own fault, the non-compete restriction remains in force.

## Practical Points for Employers and Employees

A non-compete clause is legally ancillary to the underlying employment contract — whether attached to it or executed as a separate document — which means the invalidity of the main employment contract does not automatically invalidate the non-compete clause; each must be assessed on its own terms. The most frequently litigated issue in practice is whether the employer paid the employee any consideration for the restriction, and whether that consideration is proportionate to the scope of the restraint. An unpaid, one-sided non-compete clause is not automatically void under the law, but the absence of consideration is a factor courts weigh in the employee's favour when assessing fairness. It is also worth noting that although the non-compete regime sits within the Code of Obligations' general provisions on service contracts, it applies with equal force to employees governed by the Labour Law No. 4857, since that law contains no separate non-compete provision of its own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a verbal non-compete promise enforceable in Turkey?**
No. Under Article 444, the written form is a condition of the clause's validity, not just a matter of proof. A verbal or implied non-compete undertaking has no binding effect.

**Can a non-compete clause last indefinitely?**
No. Article 445 caps the duration at two years except in special circumstances, and even within that period the scope must be reasonable in terms of place, time and line of business. A court can shorten or narrow an excessive clause rather than void it outright.

**Does a non-compete clause survive if the employer fires the employee?**
It depends on the reason. If the employer terminates the contract without just cause, or the employee resigns for a reason attributable to the employer, Article 447 ends the non-compete obligation automatically. It only survives dismissal for cause or an unjustified resignation by the employee.

**If my contract has a penalty clause for breach, can the employee just pay it and move on?**
Generally yes — under Article 446 the employee can discharge the obligation by paying the agreed penalty, unless the contract states otherwise. However, if the employer's actual loss exceeds that amount, the excess remains claimable, and in serious cases the employer may also seek an injunction against the ongoing breach if that right was expressly reserved in writing.
