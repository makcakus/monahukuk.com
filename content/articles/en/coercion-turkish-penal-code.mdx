Foreign nationals facing criminal proceedings in Turkey often encounter the term "cebir" — physical force or coercion — long before they encounter a charge that is actually called coercion. The word appears throughout the Turkish Penal Code (TCK): in robbery, in sexual assault, in unlawful deprivation of liberty, in offenses against the freedom to work. In most of those provisions, coercion is simply one ingredient of a larger crime. Article 108 of the TCK exists for the residual case — where a person used physical force to make someone else act, refrain from acting, or submit to something, but no other statute already captures that specific use of force. Understanding this residual, "catch-all" character is the key to understanding when Article 108 applies at all, and why it is frequently the wrong charge even when force was clearly used.

## A Backstop Provision, Not a Standalone Prohibition

Article 108 is best understood as a safety net rather than an independent rule with its own natural scope. It applies whenever a person uses physical force against another to compel them to do something, to stop doing something, or to allow the offender to do something to them. But the provision only engages once a threshold question is answered in the negative: does the force used already form part of the definition, or an aggravating circumstance, of some other specific offense? If a victim is forced to hand over property, that is robbery. If an employee is forced to resign from a union, that falls under offenses against trade union rights. If a public official is forced to abandon a duty, that is obstruction of a public official through force. Article 108 only fills the gap left when the compelled conduct is not already the subject of its own dedicated offense. In practice, this means the analysis in most files does not start with Article 108 — it ends there, after every more specific possibility has been ruled out.

## What the Provision Protects

Coercion protects two distinct interests at once. The primary interest is personal autonomy — the victim's freedom to decide and act according to their own will, which is violated the moment someone is forced outside their own choice. The secondary interest is bodily integrity, because the law does not treat coercion as abstract psychological pressure; it defines it as physical force that reaches the level of an intentional injury. The offense sits within the section of the Code dealing with crimes against personal liberty, which signals that autonomy is the primary value at stake, while the physical injury component is a secondary value that nonetheless drives how the penalty itself is calculated.

## The Physical Elements of the Offense

Neither the offender nor the victim needs to have any particular status for coercion to apply — in principle, anyone can commit it against anyone. An offender's official capacity can, however, pull the case into a different statute entirely: force used by a public official while on duty may instead raise questions of torture or ill-treatment under separate provisions. The victim must also be someone capable of actually carrying out the compelled conduct; force directed at a person who lacks any capacity to understand or comply is not "coercion" in the legal sense but a different offense altogether.

The conduct itself is confined to three forms: forcing the victim to do something, to refrain from doing something, or to permit the offender to do something to them. The first two push the victim toward an active or passive behavior; the third secures the victim's submission to the offender's own act. Critically, what the offender is forcing the victim to do does not need to be unlawful in itself — even forcing someone to perform an act they were already legally obligated to do can constitute coercion, because what the law protects is not the content of the act but the victim's right to decide on it freely.

The intensity of the force used is usually the single most decisive factor in how a case is resolved. Because the law fixes the penalty for coercion by reference to "the penalty for intentional injury," the physical force applied must itself reach the threshold of an intentional injury offense — meaning it must cause pain or impair the victim's health or perceptual faculties. Contact that falls short of this threshold, leaving no mark and causing no real pain, does not amount to coercion, though it may support a different charge if the facts allow. The force must also be directed at the person; force directed at property is only relevant here to the extent it indirectly affects the victim's body.

## The Mental Element: Force Used for a Purpose

Coercion can only be committed intentionally; there is no negligent version of this offense. General intent to apply force is not enough on its own — the offender must also act with the specific purpose of steering the victim toward, or away from, a particular behavior. This purposive element is what separates coercion from ordinary intentional injury: the identical physical act is treated as simple injury if there was no intent to compel anything, and as coercion if that intent was present. Whether the offender's underlying motive might be viewed as morally justifiable makes no difference to the legal classification.

## Distinguishing Coercion From Threats

Coercion and threats both aim to override a victim's will, but they operate through different means. A threat works by warning of a future harm and instilling fear; coercion works by applying physical force in the present moment. A threat says "do this, or I will do that to you"; coercion translates the words directly into action. In real cases, a threat is often followed by physical force against the same victim, and courts must then work out whether the threat was merely preparatory to the coercion or amounted to a separate, independently punishable act.

## How the Penalty Is Calculated

Article 108 does not set its own penalty range. Instead, it directs that the sentence for intentional injury be increased by between one-third and one-half. This produces a two-step calculation: the court first determines which injury provision the actual harm falls under — a minor injury treatable with simple medical intervention, an injury in its basic form, or an aggravated injury with more serious consequences — and calculates the corresponding base sentence. Only then is that base sentence increased under Article 108. As a result, the punishment for coercion tracks the severity of the physical harm inflicted and can vary considerably from case to case; any aggravating circumstances tied to the underlying injury are already factored into the first step of this calculation.

## Attempt, Participation and Concurrence With Other Offenses

The offense is complete once the coercive act is carried out — it is not necessary for the victim to actually perform the compelled behavior. Because the physical conduct can be broken into stages, an attempt is possible if the act is interrupted for reasons beyond the offender's control. All forms of participation apply, so a person who restrains the victim while an accomplice applies the force can be held responsible to the extent of their role in the joint act. As a rule of concurrence, coercion is not punished separately once the same force has already been counted as an element of a different offense. Coercion applied against several people at once produces as many separate offenses as there are victims, while repeated coercion against the same person over time is assessed under the rules governing a continuing offense.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is coercion under Article 108 a separate crime from intentional injury?**
Yes, in the sense that it requires proof of an additional purpose — compelling specific conduct — beyond the injury itself. But procedurally the two are linked: the injury provision the harm falls under determines the base sentence, which Article 108 then increases.

**Does the victim have to actually comply with the demand for coercion to be complete?**
No. The offense is complete once the coercive act — the physical force applied for the purpose of compelling conduct — has been carried out, regardless of whether the victim ultimately did what was demanded.

**Is coercion subject to a private complaint requirement, and can it be resolved through mediation?**
No. Coercion is prosecuted automatically by the authorities regardless of the victim's wishes, and because it is not subject to a complaint requirement and is not listed among mediation-eligible offenses, it is ordinarily not open to mediation.

**If force was used to make someone hand over property or abandon a duty, why wouldn't Article 108 apply?**
Because Article 108 only fills gaps left by more specific offenses. Where the compelled conduct is already the subject of a dedicated provision — robbery, obstruction of a public official, offenses against trade union rights, and others — that provision applies instead, and coercion is not charged separately for the same force.
