Every liability cap this series has covered — 400,000 SDR for bodily harm, tiered caps for baggage and vehicles, aggregate rules across multiple defendants — collapses entirely under one condition. TTK Art. 1267 defines it.

## A Cumulative, High Threshold

Art. 1267(1) strips the carrier of the Art. 1262, 1263, and 1264(1) caps if the damage is proven to result from an act or omission done (i) with intent to cause such damage, or recklessly, and (ii) with knowledge that such damage would probably result. Both elements are required together — reckless conduct alone, without proof of subjective awareness of the probable outcome, is not enough. This is a materially higher bar than ordinary negligence or even gross negligence, deliberately so: the commercial value of a liability-limitation system depends on it being reliable, and courts reserve forfeiture for conduct that knowingly and recklessly disregarded a known risk.

Notably, forfeiture reaches even a limit the parties raised by written agreement under Art. 1264(1) — whichever limit would otherwise apply, statutory or contractual, is lost.

## Extending to Servants, Assessed Individually

Art. 1267(2) applies the identical standard to a carrier's or actual carrier's servant found personally to have acted with that same intent or recklessness. Crucially, forfeiture is assessed person-by-person: the carrier losing the cap does not automatically mean every servant loses it too, and vice versa.

## Case Law: What Recklessness Looks Like in Practice

The 2026 Istanbul Regional Court of Appeal decision (12th Civil Chamber, 2026/420-2026/757, not yet final) supplies a detailed factual template. An infant fell into the water boarding a passenger boat and suffered catastrophic injury. The court found: the boarding area was extremely narrow due to the vessel's curved bow design; waves from other boats in the area caused the freeboard height to change abruptly; the captain, per an official accident investigation report, cast off lines and began engaging the engine before boarding was complete; and only one crew member — rather than the two required by the operator's own safety management procedures — supervised the boarding. Taken together, the court held these facts showed the carrier and its servants (including the captain) knowingly disregarded a known, ongoing risk and deliberately bypassed procedural safeguards — satisfying Art. 1267's "reckless and with knowledge" standard. The award, based on real damages of roughly 48.7 million TL, was more than double the SDR-cap equivalent.

## Practical Takeaway

For carriers, the forfeiture standard is demanding but not merely theoretical — known, unaddressed safety risks combined with documented procedural shortcuts can meet it. For claimants alleging catastrophic harm, establishing this standard can double or more the recoverable amount compared to the statutory cap.
